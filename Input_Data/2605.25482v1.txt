ABSTRACT
Context. Orbital inclination is crucial in determining the mass of the binary. The astrometric excess noise contain the orbital motion
information, which can be used to constrain the inclination.
Aims. We aim to constrain the orbital inclination of a binary system by combining radial velocity measurements with the astrometric
excess noise from the Gaia DR3 solution.
Methods. The astrometric excess noise is directly related to the orbital parameters. For a binary system with a radial velocity solution,
it can be treated as a function of the orbital inclination. Using the Gaia nominal scanning law and the estimated centroid uncertainties,
we simulate Gaia astrometric epoch observations to reproduce the expected excess noise. By sampling di ﬀerent inclinations and
comparing the resulting simulated excess noise with the value reported in Gaia DR3, we can constrain the inclination to a speciﬁc
interval.
Results. We have developed a method to constrain the orbital inclination within a speciﬁc range, enabling a more accurate determi-
nation of the binary mass, particularly for spectroscopic binaries. Internal and external validations demonstrate the robustness of the
method, although certain limitations remain. It is most reliable for systems exhibiting a strong astrometric signal of binary motion,
while caution is required when applying it to binaries with weak astrometric wobbles or poorly sampled orbits.

1. Introduction
The mass of stars is of crucial importance in understanding their
properties, evolution. The inclination of the orbit plays a crucial
role in accurately measuring the mass of the binary directly dur-
ing the orbit solution, speciﬁcally in the case of spectroscopic bi-
naries. In binary systems observed through radial velocity mea-
surements, it is di ﬃcult to independently determine the semi-
major axis and the inclination of the orbit plane. This is partic-
ularly evident in binary systems that contain a luminous and an
unseen dark component, such as a black hole ( Liu et al. 2019;
El-Badry et al. 2023a,b). In such cases, the challenge of break-
ing the degeneracy between the semi-major axis and inclination
becomes even more pronounced. To overcome such degeneracy,
additional data or measurements are required. In most cases, the
astrometric method is considered the primary approach for di-
rectly measuring the inclination. The astrometric method utilizes
the measurement of the positions of the binary stars over time.
By tracking the changes in their positions, the orbital motion
can be determined. The masses can then be calculated by apply-
ing Kepler’s laws, which relates the masses of the stars to the
observable quantities (e.g., semi-major axis, period, and inclina-
tion) (Martin et al. 1997; Martin & Mignard 1998; Martin et al.
1998; Wright & Howard 2009).
There have been various e ﬀorts to solve the orbital parame-
ters of the binary systems using the astrometric epoch data from

entire sky at optical wavelengths, gathering astrometric, photo-
metric, and radial velocity data ( Prusti et al. 2016). The recently
released Gaia Early Data Release 3 ( Gaia EDR3) provides as-
trometric measurements for a vast number of sources, totalling
more than 1.8 billion, with G magnitudes ranging from 3 to 21
(Lindegren et al. 2021; Brown et al. 2021). These datasets are
derived from observations gathered by the Gaia satellite during
its operational period spanning 34 months, from July 25, 2014,
to May 25, 2017. With these huge amount of astrometric epoch
data, Gaia has provided a non-single star catalogue, containing
338 215 acceleration solutions, about 165 500 orbital solutions,
and 869 VIM solutions (Halbwachs et al. 2023; Holl et al. 2023).
The Gaia astrometric epoch data holds signiﬁcant value in
obtaining orbital solutions for both resolved and unresolved bi-
naries (Pourbaix 2011), black holes, neutron stars, white dwarfs
with a luminous companion ( Barstow et al. 2014; Mashian &
Loeb 2017; Breivik et al. 2017; Y alinewich et al. 2018; Y am-
aguchi et al. 2018), and potentially even detecting exoplanets
around nearby stars ( Sozzetti et al. 2001; Turon et al. 2010; Per-
ryman et al. 2014; Sozzetti et al. 2014). Eﬀorts have been made
to estimate the solvability of orbital parameters using simula-

The astrometric excess noise ϵ, as provided by the Gaia cat-
alogue, refers to the additional noise observed from a source 1. It
quantiﬁes the level of discrepancy, in terms of an angle, between
the actual observations of a source and the best-ﬁtting standard
astrometric model utilizing ﬁve astrometric parameters. To sta-
tistically align the residuals in the astrometric solution, the as-
sumed observational noise in each observation is quadratically
increased by ϵ. When the excess noise value is 0, it indicates
that the source exhibits good astrometric behaviour, meaning the
residuals of the ﬁt align statistically with the assumed observa-
tional noise. Conversely, a positive excess noise value signiﬁes
that the residuals are statistically larger than expected. There-
fore, the astrometric excess noise ϵ contains the orbital motion
information if it is a binary system.
The excess noise has been used to search for astrometric bi-
naries (e.g., Belokurov et al. 2020; Penoyre et al. 2020; Stas-
sun & Torres 2021; El-Badry et al. 2021), or search for the X-
ray binary with excess noise ( Gandhi et al. 2022). As stressed
by Lindegren et al. (2021), both the RUW E and excess source
noise serve to quantify the deviation between the Gaia observa-
tions and the model that best ﬁts them. Belokurov et al. (2020)
used the RUW E to select the unresolved stellar companions with
Gaia DR2 astrometry. By translating the RUW E into the magni-
tude of the image centroid oscillations, they are able to identify
instances where a single-source astrometric model fails to per-
form e ﬀectively, resulting in a high RUW E value that can be
detected.
The excess noise from Gaia DR1 astrometry has been
widely used to constrain the masses of radial-velocity exoplan-
ets ( Kiefer et al. 2019; Kiefer 2019; Kiefer et al. 2020, 2021).
In these studies, the excess noise was derived by randomly sam-
pling epochs and along-scan (AL) orientations, and by inverting
the chi-square relation between the residuals and the AL uncer-
tainties. However, Gaia DR1 was based on only 14 months of
observations, during which systematic errors were not yet well
characterized in the astrometric epoch data. Such a limited tem-
poral baseline can lead to an inaccurate estimation of excess
noise. Moreover, exoplanets generally have low masses, produc-
ing small orbital motions of their host stars. As a result, the cor-
responding excess noise induced by orbital motion is typically
weak and easily masked by other noise sources, such as attitude
and calibration errors.
More recently, Kiefer et al. (2025) modelled Gaia DR3
proper motion anomalies and astrometric excess noise to search
for substellar companions, including brown dwarfs and exoplan-
ets.This approach is particularly e ﬀective in the low-amplitude
regime, where the orbital astrometric signal is small compared
to Gaias measurement precision. However, in systems exhibit-
ing stronger orbital motionwhether due to more massive com-
panions or more favorable orbital conﬁgurationsa fraction of the
orbital wobble can be partially absorbed into the proper motion
and parallax terms of Gaias ﬁve-parameter solution, leading to
underestimated astrometric excess noise. For example, this de-
generacy is especially pronounced for orbital periods close to

To overcome such limitations, we extend and modify the
framework of Kiefer (2019) by combining Gaia astrometric
astrometric excess noise with radial velocity (RV) measure-
ments.This combined approach is expected to provide a more
reliable estimation of the orbital inclination and companion mass
and is applicable to binaries across a wide range of mass regimes,
from stellar companions to systems hosting white dwarfs, neu-
tron stars, or black holes. It therefore provides a uniﬁed and con-
sistent framework for interpreting Gaia astrometric excess noise
over the full spectrum of binary masses.
This paper is organized as ﬂowing. Firstly, we study the in-
ﬂuence of all orbit parameter to the excess noise. Then we try to
use simulation data to recover the excess noise of the non-single
stars provided by the Gaia DR3. Last, we will use the excess
noise to constrain the inclination of the binary systems with ra-
dial velocity observations.
2. Methodology
2.1. Gaia astrometry of the binary motion
The Gaia satellite is performing its observations from the L2
Lagrange point of the Sun and Earth-Moon system. With its two
identical reﬂecting telescopes separated by a highly stable basic
angle of 106.5 ◦, Gaia observes the combined two ﬁelds of view
with a single focal plane covered with CCD detectors ( Prusti
et al. 2016). As the satellite rotates around its axis perpendicular
to the viewing directions of the two telescopes, Gaia will scan
across all objects located along the circle path. Meanwhile, the
spin axis is carefully managed to precess slowly across the sky,
following the nominal scanning law. This allowsGaia to observe
each object at multiple epochs, capturing its motion throughout
Gaia’s operational period.
For the binary systems of BHs, NSs, or white dwarfs with
a luminous companion, the motion of the bright object can be
described with ﬁve astrometric parameter ( ∆α∗T , ∆δT , µα∗, µδ,
ϖ) and seven Keplerian parameters ( a, e, i, P, M0, Ω, ω). Gaia is
optimized for one-dimensional measurements in the along-scan
(AL) direction, i.e., of the ﬁeld angle η. Therefore, the orbital
motion of an arbitrary object model to the local scan coordinates
is (Perryman et al. 2014):
ηbinary(t) = [∆αT cos δ + µα∗(t − T ) + BX + GY ] sin θ
+[∆δT + µδ(t − T ) + AX + FY ] cos θ + fηϖ (1)
Where θ is the position angle of the scan, fη is the along-scan

parallax, µα∗ = µα cos δ and µδ is the proper motion. [ A, B, F, G]
are the four Thiele-Innes constants that are equivalent to the Ke-
plerian ones. Details of the binary kinematic model can be found
in Wright & Howard (2009).

2.3. Inclination estimation principle
As indicated in the previous section, the astrometric excess noise
ϵ contains the orbital motion information if it is a binary system.
To analysis the relationship between excess noise and orbital pa-
rameters in a binary system, we vary one orbital parameter at
a time while keeping the others constant to observe the corre-
spondence4. Each combination results in a di ﬀerent simulated
astrometric excess noise ϵ, which follows a certain correspon-
dence with the orbital parameters, see Figure 2. The following
Keplerian parameters can be obtained from the radial velocity
(RV) data: P, the period of the orbit; K, the semi-amplitude of
the RV signal; ω, the argument of periastron; e, the eccentricity
of the orbit; M0, the date of periastron.
That is, for a given binary system with RV solution, the as-
trometric excess noise ϵ can be treated as a function of the in-
clination and the ascending node: ϵ ∼ ϵ(i, Ω). Therefore, we can
constrain the inclination by sampling di ﬀerent possible inclina-
tion and comparing the resulting ϵ with the value provided by

4. Veriﬁcation and application
4.1. Internal veriﬁcation
To verify our method, we randomly select a sample of the NSS
(nss_solution_type = AstroSpectroSB1) with 221 sources 8. We
estimate the sine values of the inclination interval using our
method and then compare it with the inclination provided by
Gaia. The results are shown in Table 1 and Figure 8. The upper
and lower bound of the sine value of the inclination given by this
method can be used to constrain the inclination. Approximately
185/221(83.7%) of the sine values of the orbital inclination given
in Gaia DR3 fall within the estimated range. This percentage in-
creases to 92.1%(59 /64) when the excess noise is greater than
1 mas. The outliers are likely caused by the source having rela-
tively low excess noise, an insu ﬃcient number of observations,
or relatively short orbital periods 9.
4.2. Application
To further verify the e ﬀectiveness of this method, we applied it
to a binary star system containing a dark, unseen compact ob-
ject and a visible companion. We estimated their orbital incli-
nation using the radial velocity observation data of the visible
companion and the excess noise provided by Gaia. Considering
the solvability of the orbit with Gaia observations, we select 6 bi-
nary systems from the literature that have orbital periods longer
than 70 days and radial velocity observations, see Table 2. Simi-
lar to Kiefer et al. (2019) and Kiefer (2019), the sine inclination

4.3. Limitation
The above results verify the eﬀectiveness of our method, but also
highlight its limitations. Speciﬁcally, it can only provide a cer-
tain range of inclinations and has limited e ﬀectiveness for some
targets. For example, for targets with long orbital periods (com-
pared to Gaia’s observation interval), insu ﬃcient Gaia sampling
results in inaccurate excess noise estimates. For targets with
short periods, the corresponding astrometric orbital wobbles are
generally small, making the excess noise easily drowned out by
other noise. Additionally, for unresolved binaries, Gaia’s astro-
metric observations only capture the photocenter of the system.
As a result, the excess noise estimated from Gaia astrometric
data is smaller than the actual value due to unknown magni-
tude/ﬂux bias.

5. Conclusions
Orbital inclination is crucial in determining the mass of astro-
metric binary systems. Astrometric epoch data is indispensable
for accurately resolving orbital inclination. When astrometric
epoch data is not available, the excess noise can be used as an
indicator to indicate the signal of a binary system motion devi-
ates from single star model.
We have developed a method to simulate the excess noise
of a binary system. By applying the established Gaia DR3 NSL
scanning law, the centroid error from the Gaia DR3 astrometric
solution, and the input parameters from the NSS catalog, we can
precisely replicate the excess noise observed in Gaia DR3.
For binary systems with radial velocity data, we can repro-
duce the excess noise for speciﬁed values of inclination and as-
cending node Ω. By sampling the orbital parameter space sta-
tistically, we obtain estimates for the excess noise, which are
then compared to the values reported in the Gaia DR3 cata-
logue. This method allows us to constrain the inclination to a
speciﬁc interval, which is very useful for estimating the mass
of the binary. We also perform internal and external veriﬁcation
of this method. The results show that while this method is quite
eﬃcient, it has limitations. It is more reliable for systems with
a strong astrometric signal of binary motion and those that are
well-sampled by the Gaia scanning law within the observation
period.

Appendix A: Inclination Interval Estimation
The inclination interval is determined by comparing simulated
and observed Gaia astrometric excess noise. Starting from the
orbital parameters constrained by radial-velocity measurements
and the Gaia ﬁve-parameter astrometric solution, we sample the
orbital inclination and the ascending node within their allowed
ranges (see Appendix B). For each realization, the binary as-
trometric motion is simulated using the Gaia scanning law, in-
cluding single-epoch astrometric uncertainties, to generate Gaia-
sampled observations (see Section 3). The corresponding simu-
lated astrometric excess noise is then computed following the
methodology in Section 2.2. Repeating this procedure over the
sampled parameter space yields the excess noise as a function
of inclination, and the allowed inclination interval is obtained
by the values for which the simulated excess noise is consistent
with Gaia DR3 (see, for example, Figure 9). This procedure is
summarized in the ﬂowchart shown in Figure A.1.
Fig. A.1. Flowchart of the inclination interval estimation. Kepler or-
bital parameters and Gaia astrometric initial values are used to simulate
Gaia–sampled binary motion. By matching the simulated excess noise
with the Gaia–reported value, the allowed inclination range is derived.
Appendix B: Parameter space and priors
We sample from a grid of 10,000 values, uniformly distributed
between 0 and π for inclination and between 0 and 2 π for Ω.
For each combination of ( i, Ω), along with the other Keplerian
and astrometric parameters, we use Markov Chain Monte Carlo
(MCMC) simulations to estimate a corresponding set of excess
noise values, ϵ. The ﬁnal parameter values are determined as
the medians of the MCMC distribution, with the uncertainties
based on the 16th, 50th, and 84th percentiles of the estimated ϵ
distribution.

Appendix C: Excess noise neglecting orbital
wobbleinduced astrometric bias
In the simulations considered here, the excess-noise computation
is based on modelling only the sky-plane motion of the photo-
center induced by the binary orbit, under the implicit assump-
tion that proper motion and parallax have been fully removed.
Within this framework, the entire orbital wobble is e ﬀectively
attributed to the astrometric excess noise. However, in the Gaia
ﬁve-parameter solution, a non-negligible fraction of the orbital
signal can be absorbed into the ﬁtted proper motion and paral-
lax terms, particularly for systems with strong astrometric sig-
natures. Neglecting this coupling leads to systematically larger
values of the predicted excess noise. To quantify this e ﬀect, we
apply this excess-noise calculation to the Gaia DR3 non-single-
star sample and directly compare the predicted values with the
excess noise reported in the Gaia DR3 catalog. As illustrated in
Figure C.1, the predicted excess noise shows a median ratio of
about 1.8 relative to the catalog values.
