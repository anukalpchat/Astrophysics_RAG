ABSTRACT
We present a high-resolution hydrodynamical simulation of the formation and evolution of nuclear structures in a Milky Way-mass
galaxy using the SMUGGLE multiphase ISM and stellar feedback model. The system naturally develops a bar of length≈5 kpc in
isolation, driving sustained gas inflows toward the center that lead to the formation of a nuclear stellar disk (NSD) and a nuclear star
cluster (NSC). By considering only stars born after bar formation, we cleanly isolate the nuclear structures and recover a clear inside-
out growth of the NSD. Consistent with observational studies, we find that stellar feedback induces repeated shocks that regulate the
size of the nuclear gas disk and drive gas from its outer edge toward the NSC region. Over time, the NSD and NSC share similar mass
growth and star formation histories, except during accretion of a massive star cluster with mass≈3×10 7 M⊙, comparable to the most
massive cluster observed near the NSC of NGC 4654. Our results suggest that both the evolutionary timescale of the bar (and thus of
the NSD) and the accretion history of star clusters are essential for obtaining tighter scaling relations among nuclear structures and
their host galaxies. Finally, our results favor a lower bulge mass for the Milky Way than in our model (B/D≈0.045) to explain the
compact size of its nuclear disk.

1. Introduction
Stellar bars are common features in disk galaxies (Eskridge et al.
2000; Wang & Zhou 2025) and dynamically interact with both
the dark matter (DM) halo and the gaseous medium. Angular
momentum exchange with the DM halo promotes bar growth
and slowdown (Athanassoula 2003; Kwak et al. 2017, 2026b),
while the non-axisymmetric perturbation from the bar drives
gas inflows toward the galactic centre (Combes & Gerin 1985;
Athanassoula 1992; Combes & Elmegreen 1993; Kwak et al.
2026a), thereby exerting positive torques on the bar rota-
tion (Beane et al. 2023; Kwak et al. 2026a). Depending on the
spheroidal bulge mass and the resulting bar properties—which
control the location of the inner Lindblad resonance and the ef-
ficiency of gas inflow—the nuclear structures follow markedly
different evolutionary paths (Kwak et al. 2026a).
In the first paper of the SMUGGLE-Ring project
(Kwak et al. 2026a, hereafter Paper I), we showed that
varying the bulge-to-disk mass ratio (0<B/D<0.091)
significantly affects the formation epoch, size, star formation
rate, and kinematics of nuclear structures. These quantities fall
within the range spanned by recent observations (Gadotti et al.
2019; de Sá-Freitas et al. 2023b; Gadotti & de Sá-Freitas 2025;
Gleis et al. 2026; Le Conte et al. 2026) and led to a proposed
young-bar versus old-bar scenario for the Milky Way, depending
on bulge mass and bar age. As demonstrated in Paper I and
references therein, NSDs are rotationally supported stellar
systems that expand outward because bar-driven gas primarily
forms stars at the outer edge of the nuclear gas disk, with that
star-forming edge itself migrating outward over time.
In contrast, NSCs are dense and pressure-supported struc-
tures at the centers of galaxies (Neumayer et al. 2020). The for-
mation mechanism of NSCs remains an active area of research,
with two primary channels being (i) the inspiral of star clusters
(see e.g. Hartmann et al. 2011, Abbate et al. 2018, Tsatsi et al.
2017a, Mastrobuono-Battisti et al. 2021) and (ii) in-situ star for-
mation driven by gas inflows (see e.g. Fahrion et al. 2022b,
2024, Aharon & Perets 2015, Brown et al. 2018). For example,
Nogueras-Lara et al. (2023) proposed that the nuclear structures
in the Milky Way may have originated from a single progen-
itor, yet the scaling relations between NSCs and NSDs pre-

2. Results
2.1. Isolated Galaxy Model
For our galaxy model r1c14b05, we increase the resolution of
model r2c14b05 from Paper I by a factor of 10 and evolve
the system in isolation for 4 Gyr using the SMUGGLE (Stars
and MUltiphase Gas in GaLaxiEs) interstellar medium (ISM)
and stellar feedback framework (Marinacci et al. 2019). Model
r1c14b05 is a Milky Way-mass galaxy consisting of a stellar
disk, a gas disk, a stellar bulge, and a dark matter (DM) halo. The
baryonic and DM particles have masses of 10 3 M⊙ and 104 M⊙,
respectively. We refer to the stellar particles in the initial condi-
tions asinitialstars, to the newly formed stars asnewstars, and
to the stars younger than 0.1 Gyr asbluestars. More details are
provided in Appendix A.1 and Paper I.
2.2. Bar Formation
Consistent with Paper I, our galaxy model naturally develops a
bar around 1 Gyr without imposing a fixed bar potential. This
enables us to examine the time evolution of the bar and the
resulting changes in the nuclear structures. By the end of the
evolution, the bar grows stronger and larger, reaching around
5 kpc, a length comparable to that of the Milky Way bar (e.g.,
Portail et al. 2017), and drives gas inflows toward the center, as
shown in Figure B.1, with details provided in Appendix B.1.
2.3. Nuclear Structures
The morphological evolution of the bar and nuclear structures
is illustrated in Figure 1. The top panel shows the face-on pro-
jections of the stacked surface density distribution of all stars
and gas within a 15×15 kpc 2 box at 1.5, 2.6, and 4.0 Gyr (see
Fig. A.3 in Paper I). As the bar rotates and dynamically interacts
with the DM halo, it becomes more prominent and elongated
by driving disk stars onto bar-supporting orbits, thereby leaving
low-density zones (dark gaps) in the inner disk (Athanassoula
2003; Kwak et al. 2017; Kim et al. 2025). At the corresponding
times, the face-on projections of surface density of blue stars in
the bottom panel of Figure 1 shows the formation of star forming
nuclear ring/disk, which is relatively small and nearly circular at
1.5 Gyr, but its ellipticity briefly increases toe≈0.7, as also
shown in Paper I. However, owing to the enhanced bar length
and strength in our high-resolution model, the bar persistently
brings more gas from the outer disk region into the inner kilo-
parsec (Fig. B.1). This delays gas depletion in the nuclear gas
disk, allowing the NSD to maintain ongoing star formation ac-
tivity instead of forming a nuclear stellar ring.

Guillard et al. (2016) used hydrodynamical simulations to
show that the formation of massive star clusters and their sub-
sequent ‘wet migration’ into NSCs can significantly alter the
chemodynamical properties of NSCs in dwarf galaxies. In our
Milky Way-mass galaxy model, this densest star cluster notably
increases the size, mass, and SFR of the NSC, whereas the effects
of minor mergers are negligible, which is consistent with the
observational results that merger events play a more dominant
role in the growth of lower-mass NSCs (Fahrion et al. 2022b,a,
2024). Interestingly, the mass of the densest star cluster in our
simulation is comparable to that of the most massive star clus-
ter (≈3×10 7 M⊙) observed near the NSC of NGC 4654, which
exhibits a complex stellar population and is expected to merge
within a few hundred million years (Fahrion et al. 2024). Such
cluster inspirals are important for reproducing the kinematical
properties of the Milky Way’s NSC (Tsatsi et al. 2017b).
The SFR trend is also similar between the NSC and NSD,
exhibiting spikes when stellar feedback events drive shocks on
the outer region of the nuclear gas disk (Fig. 3). These spikes ap-
pear with a slight delay in the NSC relative to those in the NSD.
The merger of the massive star cluster spikes the SFR only in
the NSC. Overall, the masses and SFRs of the NSD and NSC
co-evolve unless a merger event occurs. In comparison, in en-
vironments with sustained external gas inflow and/or gas-rich
galaxy mergers, more massive star clusters may form (Li et al.
2022), which can contaminate the intrinsic link between NSCs
and NSDs.
3. Discussion and Summary
This work presents the first self-consistent hydrodynamical sim-
ulation that examines the detailed formation and evolution of the
NSD and NSC in a Milky Way-mass galaxy. Most of the liter-
ature on nuclear structures is based on local simulations (e.g.,

A&A proofs:manuscript no. main
Moon et al. 2023) that employ a fixed bar potential, whereas
our system naturally forms a realistic bar that evolves over
time through dynamical interactions with the gas and DM halo,
thereby driving gas inflows from the disk regions. With real-
istic multiphase ISM and stellar feedback, our simulation nat-
urally reproduces observed features in the nuclear regions of
barred galaxies (e.g., gas cavities by the feedback in NGC 3351;
Leaman et al. 2019). The feedback events generate shocks and
redistribute angular momentum, thereby enhancing star forma-
tion in the NSC (Kolcu et al. 2023, 2026). In the local simula-
tion of nuclear ring (Moon et al. 2023), such mass inflow and
star formation in the NSC region do not occur unless a strong
magnetic field is present. Aligning with Paper I, our results sup-
port the inside-out formation scenario for nuclear structures as
the NSD expands outward over time with its star-forming edge
(e.g., SFT in Fig. B.3). Although the NSC grows mostly via in
situ star formation, its mass and size are affected by the merger
with a massive star cluster similar to the observed one near the
NSC in NGC 4654 (≈3×10 7 M⊙) (Fahrion et al. 2024).
Based on the NSD mass-size relation and the relation be-
tween NSD mass and galaxy mass, Gadotti & de Sá-Freitas
(2025) suggested that the formation of NSDs and NSCs is not
directly connected because the corresponding scaling relations
for NSCs do not agree with those for NSDs. Our results indicate
that the observed mass scaling relations of NSDs and NSCs can
be affected by the evolutionary timescale of nuclear structures as
well as by the accretion history of star clusters. In secular evolu-
tion, the longer the evolution time the greater divergence in the
ratio of mass and size between the NSC and the NSD. For exam-
ple, the ratioR NSC/RNSD decreases from 0.4 to 0.2 concurrently
with the ratioM NSC/MNSD decreasing from 6 to 2 (Fig. 3), while
the galaxy mass remains the same. Thus, considering the time
factor such as the age of the bar, which is directly related to the
age and size of the NSD, may result in tighter scaling relations.
The accretion of star clusters is known as one of the major
formation channels for NSCs (Guillard et al. 2016; Fahrion et al.
2021, 2022b). Although our model is limited to the isolated case,
even a single massive star cluster merger results in a rapid in-
crease in both the size and mass of the NSC. In a cosmological
context that includes tidal forcing and gas-rich mergers, the for-
mation efficiency of young massive star clusters would increase
(Li et al. 2022), potentially leading to a higher impact on NSC
growth. Because NSCs are the densest stellar objects located at
the centers of galaxies, they may preserve information about the
accretion history of their hosts (Neumayer et al. 2020). Future
work within the SMUGGLE-Ring project will extend this anal-
ysis to a broader range of systems, enabling a systematic explo-
ration of how bar properties, NSD sizes, and bulge mass evolve
with time and connect to these scaling relations.
Our results suggest that the Milky Way may host a relatively
low-mass classical bulge. In our model withB/D≈0.045, the
smallest radius of the NSD is 0.21 kpc, whereas the Milky Way’s
NSD is significantly more compact, with a radius of∼0.1 kpc
(Schultheis et al. 2025, and references therein). Furthermore, the
NSD in our simulation grows to∼0.5 kpc around 3 Gyr, which
is 2 Gyr after the bar formation. Consistent with Paper I, we
suggest that, to explain the compact size of the Milky Way’s
NSD, its spheroidal bulge should have a mass ratio lower than
B/D≈0.045 (Nepal et al. 2026), favoring that the Galactic bar
might be younger (Nepal et al. 2024). In future work, we will re-
visit our constraints on the bulge mass by including the effects of
magnetic fields, which may alter the star formation history and
evolutionary timescale of NSDs.
Finally, the mass of our nuclear structures (M NSD =0.9–
3.7×10 8 M⊙ withR NSD =0.21∼0.51 kpc) is notably lower
than that of the Milky Way estimated by Sormani et al. (2022)
(MNSD =10.5 +1.1
−1.0 ×10 8 M⊙). Note that the initial stars and the
blue stars (age<t bar) are not included in our mass calculation.
Their combined mass within 0.5 kpc reaches 2.5×10 9 M⊙. We
suggest that applying selection criteria based on dynamics and
age to the observed data would provide a more accurate estimate
of the actual NSD mass.

Appendix A: Method
A.1. Initial Condition

A.2. The SMUGGLE Framework
The SMUGGLE (Stars and MUlti-phase Gas in GaLaxiEs)
model (Marinacci et al. 2019) is an explicit, comprehensive stel-
lar feedback framework and implemented in the moving-mesh
hydrodynamics code AREPO (Springel 2010; Weinberger et al.
2020). It treats the ISM as a true multiphase medium and in-
jects feedback energy and momentum locally from star parti-
cles without relying on decoupled wind particles or artificial
pressurization. This approach overcomes the limitations of tra-
ditional sub-grid effective equation-of-state models, which tend
to produce overly smooth ISM morphologies and unresolved
disk structures. By self-consistently generating the hot, warm,
and cold phases of the ISM, SMUGGLE accurately regulates
star formation, drives realistic galactic outflows, and captures
the multiphase structure of the gas, particularly its dense phase
atn≳1 cm −3.
Key physical processes in SMUGGLE include supernova
feedback with momentum injection distributed radially within a
defined superbubble radius, radiative feedback from young mas-
sive stars (photoionization imposing a temperature floor in H II
regions and radiation pressure with infrared optical depth), and
stellar winds from OB and AGB stars. Cooling and heating pro-
cesses are modeled in detail down toT≈10 K, incorporating
primordial, metal-line, fine-structure, and molecular cooling to-
gether with cosmic-ray and photoelectric heating. Star formation
follows a stochastic, probabilistic prescription: gas cells above a
density threshold ofn≈100 cm −3 and satisfying a gravitational
boundedness criterion are converted into stellar particles with
efficiencyϵ=0.01. This combination of physics enables con-
verged results at various mass resolutions and has been success-
fully applied to a wide range of galaxy formation and evolution
studies. More details of the SMUGGLE model are provided in
Marinacci et al. (2019).
