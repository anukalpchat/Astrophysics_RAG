Abstract
The bulk motion of E-region radar aurora provides a sparsely distributed, direct mea-
surement of the ionospheric electric field in intermittent bursts. We present a tracking
procedure foricebearVHF measurements of Farley-Buneman waves. Each cluster is
represented as an α-shape; frame-to-frame association is a Hungarian linear-assignment
problem with a cost combining centroid distance and shape Intersection-over-Union; kine-
matic prediction amounts to a degenerate Kalman filter. Births, deaths, splits, and merg-
ers are monitored; each tracked trajectory is reduced to per-segment velocities by piecewise-
linear regression. We validate againstin-situobservations. During the G5 storm of 10
May 2024, on closed dayside field-lines, our method recovers a five-second cluster mov-
ing at 11,240±660 m/s, implying an electric field strength of ≈560 mV/m, a value
that exceeds documented sub-auroral thermal emission speeds and the most extreme re-
ported sub-auroral drifts. The detection is consistent with extreme E-field structures ap-
pearing as short-lived bursts, representing field variability, and we provide parameter-
izations of this variability for space weather modeling.
Plain Language Summary
Earth’s aurorae, or northern and southern lights, mark the location of electric currents
that flow in the upper atmosphere when the solar wind pushes hard against Earth’s ex-
tended magnetic field. The strongest of these electrical currents — the auroral electro-
jets, at roughly 105 km above Earth’s surface — are driven by electric fields that are dif-
ficult to measure: satellites pass through them too quickly to map their structure, and
ground-based radars typically return difficult-to-interpret plasma motions. We present
a machine-learning method that automatically tracks compact clusters of radar echoes,
clusters that correspond directly to plasma turbulence excited by the strong electric fields.
This is made possible by the advanced scientific imaging radaricebear, located in Saskatchewan,
Canada. Each tracked radar echo cluster is automatically followed across consecutive frames;
its motion is then converted into a local electric field measurement, validated against four
years of polar-orbiting satellite measurements (2020–2024). During the May 2024 geo-
magnetic superstorm — the most powerful storm in two decades — the method detected
a transient (fast-appearing and fast-disappearing) event in which an electric structure
travelled at over 11,000 meters per second, faster than any value previously documented
in this region of space.
1 Introduction
In Earth’s upper atmosphere, at the lower edge of the partially ionized layers of the iono-
sphere, intense, small-scale plasma turbulence is observed during geomagnetic storms,
in the form of crashing waves that appear inside the electrical currents belonging to the
auroral electrojets. Compared to the large scales of the aurorae and the magnetosphere-
ionosphere (MI) coupling, these turbulent waves, a few meters in size, aremicroscopic,
and the highly structured, turbulent, plasma that is created around aurorae subsequently
disturbs radio propagation through the ionosphere (Bahcivan et al., 2006), a signal whose
characteristic wavelength is just a few hundred meters across.
Electrojet turbulence, created by Farley-Buneman (FB, Farley, 1963; Buneman, 1963)
waves, is excited by the externally applied electric field (Ivarsen et al., 2025a), a field that
produces a relative velocity between ions and electrons at altitudes between 90 km and
120 km. Here, the ions experience collisions and their motion is restricted, while the elec-
trons are unaffected owing to their much smaller sizes. The resulting polarization causes
instability (St.-Maurice and Hamza, 2001), when the drift speeds exceed ∼400 m/s
(∼20 mV/m).

The electric field, which triggers FB waves, is proportional to Joule heating, and thevari-
ability, or spatio-temporal variance, of this field represents a vital quantity in space weather
modeling (Codrescu et al., 1995; Rosenqvist et al., 2006; Deng and Ridley, 2007; Knipp
et al., 2004). Moreover, it is exceedingly hard to measure this quantity reliably, as or-
biting satellites produce static one-dimensional slices through the plasma, and the rou-
tine means to measure electric fields, using a conventional Superdarnradar (Thomas
and Shepherd, 2018), cannot resolve scales below 45 km of size and 1 minute of duration.
However, the very-high-frequency (VHF) radaricebearcan measure scales down to 1 km
and 1second, and recent advances in radar tracking has yielded a way to quantify the
ionospheric electric field, bypassing earlier limitations caused by turbulence physics.
To explain how, we begin with a description of the auroral ionosphere, where the crash-
ing of FB waves occur fast and numerous. The sum total of these crashing waves out-
line, in time and space, the structure of the driving electric field imposed by the mag-
netosphere (Ivarsen et al., 2025a). The short-lived, orephemeral, quality of the waves,
means that the occurrence of FB turbulence around aurorae act as tracers, tracing the
shape and trajectory of moving electric field structures (Ivarsen et al., 2024c). This was
first exploited by the tracking efforts of Ivarsen et al. (2024b), who provided electric field
measurements around auroral arcs. This is enabled by the plasma being “frozen into” the
magnetic field at altitudes higher than around 150 km, meaning that the motion of elec-
tric field structures in the lower ionosphere tracks the convection electric field (Ivarsen
et al., 2024b,c).
Building on the former, we present in this paper a comprehensive method to identify and
track clusters of radar echoes as they appear and dissipate in the space above the Rab-
bit Lake observation station in Saskatchewan, Canada. This is achieved by amending
the tracking method presented by Ivarsen et al. (2024b) using modern tracking technolo-
gies usually applied in aviation and defense contexts, as well as those applied to video-
feed monitoring. Our paper concludes that the method can detect extreme ( > 10, 000
m/s) events in the auroral ionosphere.
2 Methods
The suite we have implemented is a three-stage chain operating onicebearLevel-2 echo
data (Huyghebaert et al., 2019, 2021). Recently, Ivarsen et al. (2024b) implemented an
algorithm to automatically track clutters, or clusters, of radar backscatter targets through
time and space. The present paper improves on that procedure in four respects.
(i)The point-cloud footprints are represented as α-shapes (Edelsbrunner and M¨ ucke, 1994),
which support shape-overlap arithmetic via the Intersection-over-Union (IoU).
(ii)Frame-to-frame association is formulated as a linear assignment problem on a cost
matrix combining centroid-to-prediction distance with the IoU between each previous-
frame α-shape, translated by its kinematic prediction, and each current-frame α-shape
— following the IoU-association lineage of SORT and DeepSORT (Bewley et al., 2016;
Wojke et al., 2017) — and solved by the Hungarian/Munkres algorithm (Kuhn, 1955),
with the gating radius tightening once a track has a velocity estimate.
(iii)The births, deaths, splits, and mergers of tracked clusters are monitored and book-
kept explicitly, allowing the evolution of the complex auroral-electrojet field structure
to be reconstructed without imposing one-to-one correspondences at every frame; the
motion model is a degenerate Kalman filter that maintains a one-step finite-difference
velocity per track without covariance propagation (cf. Yang and Baum, 2017, for the explicit-
noise form).
(iv)Forward-greedy sliding-window piecewise-linear regression (Keogh et al., 2001) con-
verts each tracked centroid trajectory into per-segment velocities.

2.1 F rame-to-frame association
After a new cluster has been identified, the algorithm looks at where each cluster has
just been, then predicting where that cluster should appear next, and then matching pre-
dictions to the new frame’s proposals under a cost that combines centroid distance and
shape overlap. The labels assigned by the associator are the object identities that the
system ultimately reports, whose evolution is measured. Of some importance, polygons
called α-shapes (Edelsbrunner and M¨ ucke, 1994) are drawn around the cluster footprints.
The problem has Ni clusters at frame t and Nj clusters at the most recent non-empty
frame within a nine-frame lookback window (so the tracker tolerates brief detection gaps),
and we cast association as a linear assignment problem on an Ni×Nj cost matrix. For
each previous-frame cluster j this computes a predicted position at frame t by linear ex-
trapolation using that cluster’s instantaneous velocity. Thegating radiusis the maximum
allowed centroid-to-prediction distance, in km, for a current cluster to be considered as
a possible match for j, which starts out at a value between Dgate =10–40 km, but which
is tightened to a value between Dpredict =5–15 km during tracking. Pairs separated
by more than the gating radius are excluded from the assignment problem entirely (in-
finite cost).
For each pair (i,j ) that areinsidethe gating radius, we compute the Intersection-over-
Union (IoU) of the current cluster’s polyshape against the previous cluster’s polyshape
translated by the predicted displacement (see, e.g., Bewley et al., 2016; Wojke et al., 2017,
for optical implementations in industry applications). IoU is area(intersection)/area(union)
and is therefore a coefficient that lies in [0 , 1]. Writing d =Dmat(i,j ) for the centroid-
to-prediction distance (in km), the cost for the pair is,

2.2 Births, deaths, mergers, and splits
Once the foregoing assignment problem is solved, the method maps out the four ways
a cluster can fail to participate cleanly in a 1-to-1 match (that is, cease to be tracked),
yielding the lineages of the observed shapes. For each new shape, if the intersection be-
tween it and a previous shape covers more than 5% of the current cluster’s area, the cur-
rent cluster is interpreted as a split product of the matched parent, giving the next, fresh,
target an existing velocity estimation and gating. This allows velocities to be monitored
over time and space, as in a flow, consistent with the long tail of field strength events
appearing and quickly vanishing stochastic ensembles. This bookkeeping is borrowed from
extended-target tracking (Granstrom et al., 2017, and references therein), where a sin-
gle label cannot represent a one-to-many event and parent/child provenance must be car-
ried explicitly.

4 Discussion & Summary
We have tracked clusters of E-region radar aurora through time and space with a Hungarian-
association scheme acting on α-shape footprints, themselves enclosing clusters in 3D radar
point-clouds, and we have reduced each tracked cluster trajectory to piecewise-linearve-
locities. The procedure notably yields the distribution of electric-field structure veloc-
ities withinicebear’s field-of-view, resolved at kilometre-scales and with second-cadence.
The statistical comparison, the black and red curves in Figure 7a), shows clearly that
where the existing, reliable means of obtaining electric fields are discernible, from the
Swarm spacecraft,icebearis able to penetrate the noise limitation, exhibiting a pow-
erlaw distribution until the almost-disappearing bin count at around 9000 m/s. And, as
we demonstrated in Figure 7b), we are able to explicitly quantify the geomagnetic ac-
tivity dependencies in small-scale, auroral electric field variance.

To pinpoint the significance of this finding, we note that the auroral electric field gov-
erns the energy budget in that important region, through the field’s second moment. Height-
integrated Joule dissipation scales as,
⟨|E⊥|2⟩=|⟨E⊥⟩|2 + Tr Cov(E),(3)
with the variance termTrCov(E) =Var(E x) +Var(E y) (the trace of the covariance
of E) carrying the contribution of electric-field structure to the dissipation (Codrescu
et al., 1995). A global magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) model (a space weather model),
which is advanced on a resolved-scale convection pattern, represents this crucial term only
down to the model’s spatial resolution and update cadence. This is the sub-grid, sub-
cadence remainder — the variability commonly identified as the source of modelled Joule-
heating underestimation (Deng and Ridley, 2007; Cosgrove et al., 2011) — is absent from
the electrostatic potential that drives the heating.
The deficit described above is not alleviated at the ∼50–100 km resolution of contem-
porary global models. In convergence tests the modelled heating rate still rises as lat-
itudinal grid spacing sharpens from 2.5◦to 1.25◦(Deng and Ridley, 2007); and the mea-
sured variability spectrum retains turbulent power below 45 km (Cousins and Shepherd,
2012); and in global MHD, the electrojet turbulence responsible for part of this variance
is not intrinsic to the simulation and must be parameterised in, shifting the cross-polar-
cap potential and conductance by 10–20% once added (Wiltberger et al., 2017). Inject-
ing the observed sub-grid variability can raise the hemispheric Joule-heating rate by a
factor∼1.5 (Matsuo and Richmond, 2008; Matsuo et al., 2021).
