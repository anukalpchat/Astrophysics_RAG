ABSTRACT
The early evolution of dust in protoplanetary disks remains uncertain, as the maximum particle
size inferred from (sub-)millimeter polarization can differ by up to an order of magnitude from that
inferred from spectral energy distribution modeling. To test whether porosity and morphology can
reduce this tension, we perform light-scattering numerical simulations for two dust populations: (i)
consolidated porous particles computed with the discrete dipole approximation (ADDA) and (ii) highly
porous aggregate models, including fractal and hierarchical aggregates, computed with the multiple-
sphereT-matrix method (MSTM). Using DSHARP optical constants, we compute scattering matrices,
cross sections, and effective albedo ¯ω eff for a size distributionn(a)∝a q withq=−3.5,a min =
0.1µm, and ten wavelengths from 0.87 to 10 mm. We find that increasing porosity strengthens forward
scattering and enhances polarization nearθ≈90 ◦. For compact spheres,P(90 ◦) ¯ωeff peaks near
amax ∼λ/2πand then declines, whereas porous particles show a broader peak extending to larger
sizes, keeping polarization-based constraints compatible witha max ∼1 mm. Porosity also lowers
κabs at fixed dust mass relative to compact spheres, implying larger inferred dust masses for a given
continuum flux.

1.INTRODUCTION
Protoplanetary disks are gas-rich structures around young stars. Dust sets the (sub-)millimeter continuum opacity,
shapes continuum emission and polarization, and provides the building blocks of planet formation (P. Pinilla et al.
2012; C. P. Dullemond et al. 2018; T. Birnstiel et al. 2018; J. R. Najita & S. J. Kenyon 2014). High-angular-resolution
observations, particularly with the Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array (ALMA) and the NSF’s Karl G.
Jansky Very Large Array (VLA), reveal rings, gaps, and azimuthal asymmetries that can trap solids and promote
particle growth (see S. M. Andrews 2020).
At (sub-)millimeter wavelengths, scattering can contribute significantly to continuum emission and polarization.
Neglecting scattering can bias inferred extinction opacities and lead to substantial underestimates of disk masses,
especially in optically thick regions and when characteristic particle sizes are comparable to the observing wavelength
(C. Carrasco-Gonz´ alez et al. 2019; A. Sierra & S. Lizano 2020). The interpretation of these observables is further
complicated by degeneracies among particle size, morphology, composition, and disk structure. HL Tau illustrates this
difficulty: polarization measurements have been interpreted to favora max ∼160µm (A. Kataoka et al. 2015), whereas
the continuum spectrum has often been modeled witha max ∼1 mm compact spherical particles (e.g., L. Ricci et al.
2010; C. Carrasco-Gonz´ alez et al. 2019).

The assumption that dust particles can be represented as compact homogeneous spheres is computationally conve-
nient, but observational studies increasingly point to dust populations with complex internal structure and irregular
morphology (see C. Ginski et al. 2023), while dust-growth models predict that coagulation naturally produces porous
aggregates (S. J. Weidenschilling 1977; F. Brauer et al. 2008; L. Testi et al. 2014; S. Okuzumi et al. 2012; T. Suyama
et al. 2012; P. R. Estrada et al. 2022; T. Birnstiel 2024). Several studies have considered compact nonspherical
particles, including spheroidal and irregular shapes (e.g., F. Kirchschlager & S. Wolf 2014; R. Tazaki et al. 2016;
F. Kirchschlager et al. 2019; Z.-Y. D. Lin et al. 2024). This suggests that a more substantial departure from the
compact-sphere approximation is required, with porosity being a natural candidate.
Accurate light-scattering calculations for irregular porous particles are therefore needed to compare models with
observations to constrainamax and particle morphology. We present a porous-particle modeling framework and quantify
how porosity affects light scattering and linear polarization. Section 2 introduces the relevant fundamentals and
summarizes dust models used in previous studies. Section 3 describes the particle populations and numerical methods.
Section 4 presents the results and compares them with the compact-sphere (CS) reference model. Finally, we summarize
our findings in Section 5.

2.4.Dust Models in Previous Studies
Continuum polarization in protoplanetary disks is usually interpreted as a combination of two mechanisms: (i)
scattering of thermal dust emission and (ii) dichroic emission from aligned nonspherical particles. At optical and
near-infrared wavelengths, polarization is dominated by scattering by small particles (H. Avenhaus et al. 2018). In
the mid-infrared, it is often interpreted as dichroic thermal emission from particles aligned with magnetic or radiative
fields (B.-G. Andersson et al. 2015; R. Tazaki et al. 2017). At (sub-)millimeter wavelengths, self-scattering becomes
important when particle sizes approach the observing wavelength (e.g., A. Kataoka et al. 2015; H. Yang et al. 2016).
HL Tau illustrates this transition: early detections were interpreted as magnetic alignment (I. W. Stephens et al.
2014), whereas later ALMA multi-wavelength data showed morphologies consistent with scattering dominating at
shorter wavelengths and alignment contributing at longer wavelengths (e.g., A. Kataoka et al. 2017; T. Ueda et al.
2021; I. W. Stephens et al. 2023; Z.-Y. D. Lin et al. 2023, 2024).
The effective medium approximation (EMA) is widely used to study the effect of porosity through the effective
refractive index. EMA-based studies show that high porosity can increaseκ sca andP(θ) while decreasingκ abs,
with direct consequences for inferred particle sizes and dust masses (e.g., Y. Liu et al. 2022, 2024; S. Zhang et al.
2023). For example, F. Zagaria et al. (2025) fitted multi-frequency ALMA and VLA data of CI Tau with EMA
particles and favored porosities ofP≲0.7. Another approximation is the distribution of hollow spheres (DHS), which
reproduces several porosity signatures, including broadened and shifted spectral features (M. Min et al. 2016). Particle
morphology is an additional degree of freedom (e.g., F. Kirchschlager & G. H.-M. Bertrang 2020): DDA calculations
for aligned oblate spheroids produce stronger self-scattering polarization than compact spheres neara∼λ/2π(F.
Kirchschlager & G. H.-M. Bertrang 2020), and porous prolate/oblate particles can match the HD 142527 polarization
for moderate elongations andP≲0.7 (F. Kirchschlager et al. 2019). More recently, J. M. J´ aquez-Dom´ ınguez et al.
(2026) compared radiative-transfer models using solid compact spheres and solid irregular hexahedral particles with
identical size distributions, finding that grain irregularity can enhance the scattering opacity and suppress the Mie
polarization reversal at large size parameters, although irregularity alone is not sufficient to reproduce the observed
millimeter polarization fractions.
Fractal aggregates are a standard description of highly porous particles formed by hit-and-stick growth. R. Tazaki
et al. (2019) showed that, for aggregates composed of submicron monomers, millimeter-wave scattering polarization
favors relatively compact structures: aggregates with higher fractal dimension and/or lower porosity are more efficient
scatterers, whereas very fluffy aggregates are less likely to contribute significantly to the observed polarized emission.
Using optical and near-infrared polarimetry, R. Tazaki & C. Dominik (2022) inferred an upper limit of∼0.4µm
for the monomer radius. R. Tazaki et al. (2023) similarly found that the infrared scattered light of IM Lup is best
reproduced by aggregates with submicron monomers. T. Ueda et al. (2024, 2025) found that moderately porous dust
is favored to reproduce millimeter continuum and polarization simultaneously. These results are consistent with C.
Ginski et al. (2023), who identified two broad dust populations ranging from highly porous aggregates to moderately

3.METHODOLOGY
We model two particle populations: consolidated porous particles (CP), generated by carving voids in a spherical
seed while preserving a spherical envelope (Figure 1), and highly porousMSTMaggregate models, including fractal
aggregates (FA) and one hierarchical aggregate (HA), withP≳0.9 (Figure 2). The explored parameter space is
listed in Table 1. We evaluate ten wavelengths from 0.87 to 10 mm, spanning the ALMA/VLA bands in Table 2.
This interval samples the transition from scattering-affected, often optically thick emission at shorter wavelengths to
optically thin emission atλ≳3 mm (C. Carrasco-Gonz´ alez et al. 2019). At 3–4 mm, emission is also increasingly
midplane-dominated beyond∼20–30 AU (e.g., E. Mac´ ıas et al. 2021; A. Garufi et al. 2025).

3.2.Consolidated Porous Particles
We compute the CP models withADDA(v1.4.0; M. A. Yurkin & A. G. Hoekstra 2011) using the discrete dipole
approximation (DDA), where each target model is discretized on a cubic lattice of polarizable point dipoles. We track
discretization with|m|kd, wherek= 2π/λis the wavenumber,dis the lattice spacing, and|m|is the modulus of the
complex refractive index. We adopt|m|kd <0.3, which is equivalent to at least 21 dipoles per internal wavelength -
much finer than typically recommended for compact particles (see M. A. Yurkin & A. G. Hoekstra 2011). Orientation
averaging follows the internalADDAscheme: for each run, we use 32 uniformly spaced values of the Euler angleαand
an adaptively refined (β, γ) grid with 14–242 angle pairs. This setup keeps the relative error in orientation-averaged
Cext below 0.1%. We model CP particles as a randomly oriented ensemble (M. I. Mishchenko & M. A. Yurkin 2017).
All remaining DDA parameters follow the standardADDAconfiguration. Convergence and realization-to-realization
variability for the CP calculations are quantified in Appendix A.

4.RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
4.1.Phase Function and Linear Polarization Fraction
The upper rows of Figure 3 show the size-averaged phase functionF 11(θ). Across the studied spectral range,
increasing porosity systematically sharpens the forward cusp, as reflected in largerF 11 values atθ≲10 ◦, while
generally weakening the backscattering lobe. This ordering is clearest at intermediate wavelengths (λ= 2–4 mm). At
the longest wavelengths (λ≥7 mm), the phase functions become smoother and exhibit a broad minimum nearθ≃90 ◦.

9
The lower rows of Figure 3 show the linear polarization fractionP(θ). At short wavelengths, compact spheres
and low-porosity particles show a limited positive peak and a pronounced negative branch at large scattering angles
(θ≳120 ◦), most clearly forP≲0.7. Increasing porosity strengthens the peak, shifts it towardθ≃90 ◦, and
progressively suppresses the negative branch; the highest-porosity case remains positive at nearly all scattering angles.
At intermediate wavelengths (λ= 2–4 mm), the polarization curves are smoother and largely positive, with a broad
maximum nearθ∼90 ◦. Byλ≥7 mm, the curves approach the Rayleigh regime.

4.2.Mass Opacities
Figure 4 summarizes size-averaged absorption, scattering, and extinction opacities as functions ofa max at four
representative wavelengths. For smalla max, absorption dominates extinction. Oncea max enters the Mie/interference
regime, bothκ sca andκ ext rise rapidly and then approach a plateau. Compact spheres and low-porosity CP models
reach the highest values, whereas increasing porosity systematically reduces opacities, most strongly forMSTMaggregate

models. The anisotropy correction also grows with size and porosity: the gap betweenκ sca andκ sca,eff increases as
gincreases. At fixed dust mass,κ abs decreases with porosity, andMSTMaggregate models give the lowest values once
amax ≳100µm. Figure 5 shows the wavelength dependence of the size-averaged mass opacities at fixeda max. For
amax = 100µm,κ sca,κ abs, andκ ext decrease smoothly withλ, andκ sca,eff ≈κ sca, indicating weak forward peaking.
Fora max = 1 mm, compact spheres and low-porosity CP models yield the largestκ sca andκ ext. At longer wavelengths,
all curves converge as the size parameter decreases.

An observational consequence of adopting porous-particle opacities is a systematic change in the disk properties
inferred from continuum emission. In the optically thin limit,F ν ∝κ abs(ν)M dust Bν(Tdust) (see Section 3 of C.
Carrasco-Gonz´ alez et al. 2019); therefore, for the opacity trends found here, compact-sphere opacities would imply
smallerM dust than porous-particle opacities. In the optically thin Rayleigh–Jeans limit, the continuum spectral index
can be approximated asα≃2 +β κ, withκ abs(ν)∝ν βκ. Thus, porosity-induced changes in the opacity slope alter the
mapping between an observed spectral index and the inferredamax. A full comparison with spectral energy distribution
(SED) constraints requires radiative-transfer modeling, but the opacity trends shown here indicate that porosity can
shift the particle sizes inferred from continuum slopes. In optically thick regions, neglecting scattering and treating
extinction as pure absorption can bias the inferred dust mass low for a fixed temperature and observed intensity (C.
Carrasco-Gonz´ alez et al. 2019; A. Sierra & S. Lizano 2020).

Our results indicate that porosity broadens the range of particle sizes capable of producing significant polarized
self-scattering, rather than confining it to the compact-sphere conditiona max ∼λ/2π. In disks such as HL Tau, where
compact-sphere models givea max ∼160µm in ALMA Bands 7 and 6 (A. Kataoka et al. 2015), porous particles can
sustain comparableP(90 ◦) ¯ωeff up toa max ∼1 mm. Porosity also changesκ abs(λ) at fixeda max, especially near 1 mm,
so a given continuum slope can map to different particle sizes. Robust constraints therefore require multi-wavelength
radiative-transfer modeling with porosity-dependent opacities.
4.4.Comparison with Previous Studies
F. Kirchschlager & G. H.-M. Bertrang (2020) modeled self-scattering polarization by aligned spheroids forn(a)∝
a−3.5. Their peakP(90 ◦) ¯ωvalues are comparable to those in Figure 6, but their polarization window is narrower (see
their Figure 6). In our models, high porosity sustains similar amplitudes over larger sizes. S. Zhang et al. (2023) used
Mie theory plus EMA, with the Bruggeman mixing rule, to evaluateP(90 ◦) ¯ωeff over a wide size range. To suppress
large size-dependent oscillations, they smoothed the single-size polarization by averagingZ 11(90◦) andZ 12(90◦) over
neighboring sizes before computingP(90 ◦) (their Appendix E and Figure 14). Because our explicit porous particle
calculations use a coarser size grid, we computed additional Mie–EMA models on the same 45 sizes used in the
ADDA/MSTMruns for a direct comparison. We mixed the DSHARP composition with vacuum using the Bruggeman rule
forP= 0.70, 0.90, and 0.99 and then computed the corresponding Mie solutions. The resulting curves are shown in
Figure 8. Within the common size range, our porous particles yield slightly higherP(90 ◦) ¯ωeff than the corresponding
Mie–EMA models. Thus, porosity is represented structurally while retaining the DSHARP optical constants for the
solid material, rather than being approximated through an effective refractive index as in Mie–EMA.
According to M. J. Wolff et al. (1994, 1998), effective-medium models become inaccurate once pores or inclusions leave
the Rayleigh regime. In our baseline CP models,r pore ≃5–50µm; atλ min = 0.87 mm, this implies 2πr pore/λ≲0.4.
EMA deviations should therefore grow for larger particles and less subwavelength pores. The clearest breakdown
appears at very high porosity: the Mie–EMA curve forP= 0.99 is nearly flat witha max, while theMSTMHA
curve increases monotonically (Figure 8), consistent with the known limitations of representing very porous aggregate
structures with a homogeneous effective medium (e.g., R. Tazaki et al. 2016). For computational feasibility, ourMSTM
aggregate models adopt monomer sizes that are larger than the∼400 nm values inferred from infrared constraints
(e.g., R. Tazaki & C. Dominik 2022). In Appendix C, we show that the CP results are only weakly affected by the
tested pore sizes, whereas theMSTMaggregate results are more sensitive to the adopted monomer size: decreasinga mon
lowers ¯ωeff, especially atλ= 0.87–1.0 mm. The high-a max MSTMaggregate results should therefore be interpreted as
dependent on the computationally feasible monomer sizes used here.
5.SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS
In this work, we modeled the scattering properties of two porous dust populations across ten wavelengths from 0.87
to 10 mm. Relative to compact spheres, increasing porosity strengthens forward scattering, increasesg, and lowers
¯ωeff. Porosity suppresses Mie/interference oscillations, reducesκ abs at millimeter sizes, and increases the relative
contribution ofκ sca,eff to effective extinction. Consequently, porous-particle opacities imply larger dust masses than
compact-sphere opacities for the same continuum flux.
Compact spheres exhibit higherP(90 ◦) ¯ωeff values arounda max ∼λ/2π, whereas porous particles maintain compar-
atively high values over a broader size interval, extending up toa max ∼1 mm. Porosity therefore makes the inference
ofa max from continuum polarization morphology-dependent and can help reconcile the∼160µm sizes inferred for
HL Tau from polarization (A. Kataoka et al. 2015) with millimeter-sized particles implied by continuum emission
constraints (C. Carrasco-Gonz´ alez et al. 2019).
Our results show that porosity alters the polarized continuum spectrum relative to compact spheres. Multi-
wavelength polarization observations across the∼1–10 mm range can constrain particle porosity if the sensitivity
to low polarization fractions and the angular resolution are sufficient to separate disk regions. Such studies are cur-
rently most accessible near 1 mm with ALMA; observations around 3 mm are feasible but high-resolution mapping
is more demanding. At longer wavelengths, the continuum and polarized signals become fainter, but the ALMA
Wideband Sensitivity Upgrade and the ngVLA should improve porosity-sensitive diagnostics, with complementary
constraints from the SKA at centimeter wavelengths.
Compared with our explicit porous-particle models, Mie–EMA captures the broader polarization window at interme-
diate porosity, but it deviates more at very high porosity, especially for the very high-porosity HA case. This highlights

14
the limitations of EMA outside the Rayleigh regime. Because the monomer-size tests show that decreasinga mon lowers
¯ωeff, especially at shorter millimeter wavelengths, the high-a max MSTMaggregate results should be interpreted as an
upper-limit case for the adopted computationally feasible monomer sizes.
Future work will focus on exploring compositions in which refractory organics are replaced by more opaque
amorphous-carbon components, in line with the opacity prescriptions used by L. Ricci et al. (2010) and in the DIANA
framework of P. Woitke et al. (2016). It will also extend the catalog toa max ∼1 cm and incorporate the resulting
scattering matrices and opacities into full 3D radiative transfer simulations of protoplanetary disks.
