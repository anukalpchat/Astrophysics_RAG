ABSTRACT
We present JWST/MIRI MRS spectra of the wide-separation (projected separation= 980 au) binary
protoplanetary disks Sz 65 (K7;0.68 M⊙) and Sz 66 (M3;0.30 M⊙), reduced using the uniform pipeline
of the JWST Disk Infrared Spectral Chemistry Survey (JDISCS). Both disks show rich molecular
emission, including H2O, CO2, HCN, C2H2, and OH. The scaled spectra of the two disks exhibit
remarkably similar H2O, CO2, and HCN line emission in the 13–18µm region, with the only notable
difference being stronger C2H2 emission in the primary (Sz 65). Beyond 18 µm, the difference in
H2O line emission between the two disks increases. Both the flux ratios and the slab-model-derived
mass ratios of cold to hot H2O (∼ 200 K to ∼ 750 K) and warm to hot H2O (∼ 450 K to ∼ 750 K)
are significantly higher in the secondary (Sz 66). Because binary stars share nearly the same age
and metallicity, and both disks appear compact in millimeter emission (< 30 au), we suggest that the
excess cold H2O in the secondary is best explained by its unstructured dust disk, in contrast to the
primary, which shows gaps at 6 and20 au. The enhanced cold water in the secondary is consistent
with efficient pebble drift across the water snowline and increased H2O vapor from the sublimation
of icy mantles. Our results demonstrate that wide-separation binaries can serve as powerful control
samples for isolating the impact of individual disk properties on inner-disk chemistry and evolution.

Mid-infrared (mid-IR) spectra have been widely ap-
plied to probe the inner regions of protoplanetary disks
(Salyk et al. 2011; Pontoppidan et al. 2010; Carr & Na-
jita 2008; Pascucci et al. 2013). The InfraRed Spec-
trometer (IRS) on the Spitzer Space Telescope observed
∼ 300 protoplanetary disks with a spectral resolution of
R ∼ 600 and detected various molecular species: CO is
detected in most of the disks, whereas H2O, CO2, OH,
HCN, and C2H2 are detected in nearly half of the T
Tauri stars’ disks (Pontoppidan et al. 2010). However,
the spectral lines obtained from Spitzer are unresolved
and often suffer from blending. Recent JWST/MIRI ob-
servations achieve a much higher spectral resolution of
R ≈ 2000–3500 (Pontoppidan et al. 2024), revealing a
rich molecular inventory in protoplanetary disks. This
includes low-abundance isotopologues such as 13CO2,
13CCH2, H 13CN, C18O16O, and H 18
2 O (Grant et al.
2023; Colmenares et al. 2024; Salyk et al. 2025, 2026)
and more complex organic molecules such asC4H2 and
C6H6 (Benzene) (Tabone et al. 2023; Arabhavi et al.
2024). In addition, the increased spectral resolution has
enabled detailed modeling of molecular temperatures
and column densities.
Recent JWST/MIRI surveys—notably the JWST
Disk Infrared Spectral Chemistry Survey (JDISCS; Pon-
toppidan et al. 2024; Arulanantham et al. 2025) and
the Mid-INfrared Disk Survey (MINDS; Henning et al.
2024)—have revealed a remarkable diversity in pro-
toplanetary disk chemistry. Observations range from
water-rich spectra (Xie et al. 2023; Grant et al. 2024;
Arulanantham et al. 2025) to those dominated by CO2
(Grant et al. 2023; Vlasblom et al. 2025) or enriched
in complex hydrocarbons (Tabone et al. 2023; Arabhavi
et al. 2024, 2025), the latter predominantly found in
disks around very low mass hosts.
In addition, significant contrasts in H2O temperature
distributions have been found (Banzatti et al. 2023a;
Romero-Mirza et al. 2024; Gasman et al. 2023). Recent
studies have proposed that the diversity of H2O line flux
can be shaped by disk size and substructures (Banzatti
et al. 2023a; Krijt et al. 2025). However, Temmink et al.
(2025) have found a wide diversity in H2O abundance
within a sample of relatively compact (< 60au) disks,
and the H2O line flux may also depend on evolutionary
state(Banzattietal.2017)andmetallicity(Guadarrama
et al. 2022), which complicates isolating the effect ofdisk
structure.
Binary disks share nearly identical stellar ages, ini-
tial metallicities, and environments, therefore offer-
ing unique opportunities to investigate the relation-
ship between mid-IR spectra and disk properties. Pre-
vious mid-IR studies have focused on relatively close
(< 300au) pairs of binaries (Grant et al. 2024; Kur-
tovic et al. 2025), where dynamical interactions alter
the evolution of both disks in the binary (Zagaria et al.
2023). This can sometimes result in either no detectable
disk or only very weak molecular emission from the sec-
ondary. In contrast, wide pairs (> 300au) mostly host
unperturbed disks, making their evolution comparable
to that of single stars. This is supported by millime-
ter emission surveys, which show that the disk luminos-
ity distribution of wide pairs is similar to that of single
stars, while close pairs are significantly fainter (Harris
et al. 2012; Akeson et al. 2019; Manara et al. 2019; Panić
et al. 2021).

tance ≈ 1000au) binary at a distance of approximately
154pc identified using Gaia EDR3 data (Bohn et al.
2022). The stellar and disk parameters are summa-

2. OBSERVATIONS AND DATA REDUCTION
2.1. Observations
The JWST/MIRI data used in this work are available
at MAST: doi:10.17909/nnc1-9g02. Sz 65 and Sz 66
were observed with JWST/MIRI in Medium Resolution
Spectrometer (MRS) mode on 2024 July 11 (UT) as part
of the Cycle 2 GO program 3034 (PI: K. Zhang). The
two targets were observed within the same visit, with
a total scheduled visit duration of 3.55 hr. The MIRI-
MRS spectra were obtained using the full MRS wave-
length coverage to produce continuous spectra across
4.9–28µm. Observations used the standard 4-point neg-
ative dither pattern optimized for point sources.
Data reduction for both disks follows the standard
JDISCS procedure described in Pontoppidan et al.
(2024), which adopts the standard MRS pipeline
(Bushouse et al. 2025) up to stage 2b. An asteroid ref-
erence observation of (515) Athalia is obtained using
the same instrumental configuration and dither pattern.
The asteroid calibration provides high-quality fringing
removal and characterizes the relative spectral response
function, maximizing the S/N in channels 2–4. We use
JDISCS reduction version 9.1, which includes improved
bad-pixel correction and an updated long-wavelength
(≳ 20 µm) flux calibration.
2.2. Continuum subtraction
We follow the continuum subtraction technique de-
scribed in Pontoppidan et al. (2024). The full MIRI
spectra and the continuum estimation of the primary
and the secondary are shown in Figures 16 and 17 in
Appendix A. The continuum is obtained by iteratively
applying median filters, retaining the lower flux density
components of the spectra, and subsequently smoothing
with a second-order Savitzky-Golay filter (Savitzky &
Golay 1964). Broad molecular emission regions includ-
ing 13.4–14.1µm (C2H2 and HCN) and 14.9–15.0µm
(CO2) are excluded from continuum estimation. Follow-
ing the approach in Banzatti et al. (2025), we identify
a set of line-free regions and compare them to the con-
tinuum, then apply a final wavelength-dependent flux
offset to the continuum.

After obtaining the estimated continuum, we quantify
the strength of the 10µm silicate feature by calculating
SF10 ≡ F10/Fbg, where F10 is the silicate continuum
at 10µm and Fbg is obtained by linearly interpolating
between 7.5 and 13.5 µm; the SF10 of Class II disks
typically ranges from 1.2 to 3.5 (Kessler-Silacci et al.
2006). Figure 1 shows that the two disks have similar
silicate feature strengths (SF10 = 1 .39 for Sz 65 and
SF10 = 1 .43 for Sz 66). A reduced silicate feature is
generally associated with grain growth and vertical set-
tling (Dullemond & Dominik 2004; Lommen et al. 2010).
Therefore, the nearly equalSF10 values suggest broadly
similarlevelsofdustgrainprocessinginthebinarydisks.
3. ANALYSIS
The continuum-subtracted spectra of Sz 65 and Sz 66
in the 13–27µm range are shown in Figure 2. After scal-
ing up the flux of Sz 66 (the secondary) by a factor of 2,
the emission of CO2 (14.97 µm) and HCN (∼ 14 µm) are
very similar, while the C2H2 emission (13.6–13.8µm) is
substantially stronger in Sz 65. The H2O emission lines
of the two disks show comparable intensities between
13–18µm. Beyond 18 µm, where H2O lines have gen-
erally lower upper-level energies (Eup), the scaled spec-
trum of the secondary shows an excess in H2O emission.
In the following sections, we further examine the disk
properties of the binary based on the emission features
observed.

et al. (2025). These diagnostics provide a fast prelimi-

agnostics to provide a preliminary estimate of the hot

components of the binary. The lines involved in the di-

In conclusion, the water line-ratio diagnostics provide
a quick look at the binary’s water reservoirs, indicat-
ing a larger hot water emitting area for the primary, a
larger warm/cold water contribution and higher warm
H2O column density for the secondary, and comparable
v = 1–0 non-LTE suppression.
3.2. Slab models of molecular emission
The continuum-subtracted MIRI spectra reveal a for-
est of molecular lines. We detect H2O lines throughout
the full MIRI range at both disks, appearing primar-
ily as pure rotational lines in longer wavelengths (13–
27 µm) and as rovibrational lines in shorter wavelengths
(5.5–8.5µm). We detect significant emission from CO2
(15.0 µm), C2H2 (∼ 13.7 µm), HCN (∼ 14.0 µm), and
CO (4.9–5.15µm). The isotopologue emission of13CO2
is analyzed in Appendix F.
We retrieve the physical parameters of molecu-
lar species by fitting the MIRI spectrum with LTE
slab models, employing a Markov Chain Monte Carlo
(MCMC) approach. Although we cannot spatially re-
solve inner-disk emission, the rich set of molecular lines
with differentEup and Aul allows us to probe the molec-
ular reservoir with a wide range of temperatures and
opacities. The line transition information is obtained
from the HITRAN database (Gordon et al. 2022). In
previous studies, the slab model is typically character-
ized by three parameters: temperature (T), column den-
sity (log N), and emitting area ( log A) (Grant et al.
2023; Pontoppidan et al. 2024). In this study, we replace
log N and log A with log N −log A and log N +log A, re-
spectively. The latter term captures the observable mass
(log M) divided by the molecular mass, which remains
constrained in the optically thin regime. This choice sta-
bilizes the MCMC run by avoiding multiple degenerate
parameters.
We include the optical depth effect of overlapping lines
by adopting Gaussian line profiles with an FWHM of
∆vopacity = 4 .7 km s−1 (σ = 2 km s−1), consistent with
the values adopted by Salyk et al. (2011) and Grant
et al. (2023). The LTE model is implemented with

3.2.1. 13–27µm: Main molecular models
We divide the 13–27µm wavelength range into two in-
tervals at 18µm because of the spectral resolution gap
between MRS Channels 3 and 4 (see Figure 6 of Pon-
toppidan et al. 2024). The molecular species and the
FWHM of lines are treated separately in the two inter-
vals, whereas theχ2 values are combined in the MCMC
technique. We determine the FWHM due to instrument
resolution and Keplerian motion by fitting selected H2O
lines. For 13–18µm, the FWHM of Sz 65 and Sz 66
are ∆vobs = 150km s−1 and 121km s−1; for 18–27µm,
the FWHM are ∆vobs = 169 km s−1 and 147 km s−1.
The systemic radial velocity measured from H2O lines
is 8.4km s−1 and 5.7km s−1 for Sz 65 and Sz 66 after
converting to the LSR frame, which are close to the ra-
dial velocity fitted in AGE-PRO Trapman et al. (2025)
(Sz 65: 4.5 km s−1, Sz 66: 4.5km s−1), considering the
velocity uncertainty of MIRI (Argyriou et al. 2023). We
estimate the noise level of the continuum-subtracted
spectra using a Gaussian process following the approach
in Romero-Mirza et al. (2024); the results are summa-
rized in Appendix B. For 13–18µm, the mean noise lev-
els of Sz 65 and Sz 66 are 1.98 mJy and 1.23 mJy; for
18–27µm, the noise levels are 4.40 mJy and 2.93 mJy.
We include three H2O components to approximate the
radial temperature gradient of the disk, with tempera-
tures initialized from flat distributions spanning±50K
around 800K (Hot), 400K (Warm), and 200K (Cold).
The Hot and Warm components enter both wavelength
intervals, whereas the Cold component is restricted to
18–27µm. We also fit CO2, C2H2, HCN, and OH si-
multaneously in the MCMC runs. We represent OH
with three temperature components to approximate its
non-LTE effects as in Banzatti et al. (2025), but the pa-
rameters retrieved under the LTE assumption are not
representative of the thermalized conditions.

summarizes the best-fit parameters and the 1σ uncer-
tainties. The larger values in the binary are represented
in bold numbers. The emitting area (log A) in Table 2 is
calculated by dividing observable mass by column den-
sity and molecular mass. The uncertainties of the OH
components are not included because their parameters

distributions of the main model parameters.

3.2.2. 5.5–8.5µm: Non-LTE water emission
We analyze the rovibrational H2O emission in the 5.5–
8.5 µm range, where significant water emission exists
and does not heavily overlap with CO emissions. It
is known that rovibrational H2O lines are often sub-
thermally excited compared to the bulk pure rotational
lines (Meijerink et al. 2009; Bosman et al. 2022; Ban-
zatti et al. 2023b). For each disk, we apply our three-
component H2O model derived from the 13–27µm fit
and then scale the emission by a suppression factor. We
note that non-LTE suppression may be temperature de-
pendent (Banzatti et al. 2023b); however, we assume a
uniform suppression for simplicity within the scope of
this work.
We obtained the best-fit suppression factors by min-
imizing χ2 for each disk, yielding 5.9 and 6.9 for Sz 65
and Sz 66. Figure 9 shows that the scaled model flux
reproduces the spectra of both disks from 5.5 to 7µm
reasonably well, consistent with the result shown in Fig-
ure 5 using line-ratio diagnostics.

4. DISCUSSION
4.1. Binary comparison
Inner-disk chemical properties can be influenced by a
range of factors, including stellar age, luminosity, metal-
licity, accretion rate, radiation environment, disk struc-
ture, and gravitational interactions. Consequently, iso-
lating the impact of any single factor on disk chemistry
is challenging. Wide-separation (> 300au) binaries can
serve as laboratories with controlled variables, sharing
nearly identical stellar ages, metallicities, and environ-
ments. As a result, the remaining factors (e.g., stellar
luminosity, accretion rate, and disk properties) become
the principal variables.
Among the protoplanetary disk systems observed with
JWST/MIRI, Sz 65 and Sz 66 constitute the first wide-
separation Class II binary analyzed in detail. Previ-
ous studies have analyzed the molecular lines of several
class II binaries with relatively small projected separa-
tions, including DF Tau (9 au) (Grant et al. 2024), VW
Cha (130 au), WX Cha (143 au), and RW Aur (Kurtovic
etal.2025)( 240 au). TheDFTausystemisnotspatially
or spectrally resolved by MIRI. The separations of VW
Cha, WX Cha, and RW Aur are larger, but the sec-
ondary disks of all three systems are mostly line-poor,
which might be caused by disk truncation, low stellar
mass, or an inner disk cavity (Kurtovic et al. 2025). In
contrast, as a wide-separation binary, Sz 65 and Sz 66
show no evidence of dynamical perturbation and ex-
hibit remarkable similarities in H2O, CO2, and HCN
line emission. Therefore, we consider them ideal con-
trolled samples for studying dynamically unperturbed
disk evolution.
Comparisons of the continuum-subtracted spectra
(Figure 2) and the best-fit slab parameters (Table 2;
Figure 8) reveal close similarities between the primary
(Sz 65) and the secondary (Sz 66) in the CO2, HCN,
and hot (∼ 750 K) H2O components, with systemati-
cally scaled-down emitting areas in the secondary. This
aligns with expectations for unperturbed binaries with
the same metallicity, as the HCN/H2O flux ratio has
been proposed as a robust, temperature-insensitive in-
dicator of inner-disk C/O ratio (Carr & Najita 2011; Na-
jita et al. 2013; Anderson et al. 2021). The C2H2/H2O
flux ratio is lower in the secondary; however, this C/O
indicator might also reflect the luminosity contrast (0.87
L⊙ and 0.22 L⊙) according to Anderson et al. (2021).
The most notable difference is the cold water emission.
Both the water diagnostic plots and the best-fit param-
eters indicate that the secondary has a significantly en-
hanced cold-water mass relative to its hot water, HCN,
andCO 2. Theslabmodelresultsofthesecondarymatch
the “H2O dominated phase” described in Sellek et al.
(2025), where cold H2O is enhanced while CO2 remains
largely unchanged.
Because the binary system controls for stellar age,
metallicity, and the environment, we discuss three re-
maining factors that could drive the chemical differ-
ences. We examine stellar properties and accretion rate,
two H2O-regulating factors commonly discussed in the
literature, and explore the impact of disk structure dif-
ferences inferred from ALMA observations.
4.2. Explaining the cold H 2O excess
4.2.1. Stellar properties
The secondary (Sz 66) has lower mass and stellar lu-
minosity (0.30 vs. 0.68M ⊙; 0.22 vs. 0.87L ⊙), which
implies a closer snowline and a more compact water
emitting region. Under such a scenario, Sz 66 should
exhibit lower water masses across all three components.
Instead, we find the two sources have comparable masses
of hot water and Sz 66 actually has significantly higher
masses in warm and cold water than Sz 65, directly con-
tradicting the expected trend.
In addition, the lower stellar mass may produce higher
pebble drift velocities (Pinilla et al. 2013; Pinilla 2022),
though this effect is predicted to be significant primarily
for very low-mass stars (≲ 0.2, M⊙).

4.2.3. Disk structure
Previous studies proposed that the sublimation of ice
mantles on inward-drifting pebbles replenishes cold and
warm water in the inner disk (Ciesla & Cuzzi 2006).
Kalyaan et al. (2021) and Kalyaan et al. (2023) relate
the efficiency of pebble drift to the size of the dust disk
and the presence of gaps: compact, featureless disks ex-
perience efficient inward drift and inner-disk water en-
richment, while large, structured disks trap pebbles be-
yond the gap. The anti-correlation between cold water
enrichment and dust disk size is observationally sup-
ported by Spitzer samples (Banzatti et al. 2020) and a
relatively small JWST sample (Banzatti et al. 2023a).
In addition, Krijt et al. (2025) found a correlation be-
tween inner disk gap radius and cold water reservoir,
suggesting that close-in gaps prevent cold water enrich-
ment. Easterwoodet al. (2024)alsosuggest that close-in
deep gaps and multiple gaps tend to reduce cold water
delivery.

and a tentative signal of an inner gap at6 au, while the
secondary has a compact disk with no structure detected

might be deeper than observed due to resolution limits,

as Sz 65 has a high inclination (63◦) and the gap width
tends to be smaller at an inner radius.
The spatial extent of the disk components provides
further insight into the observed water discrepancies.
The primary exhibits a large disk size ratio of 5.3:1 (gas
at 142au vs. dust at 27au), whereas the secondary has
a smaller ratio of 3:1 (gas at 48au vs. dust at 16au),
although the latter ratio is more uncertain because the
gas disk radius of Sz 66 is less well constrained. The
large size ratio of the primary suggests that significant
radial drift occurred in its outer disk region (Trapman
et al. 2020; Sanchis et al. 2021). However, its cold wa-
ter emission is still weaker than the secondary, implying
that the inner disk substructure plays a more important
role. Studies have proposed that the inner gaps can trap
the pebbles beyond the water snowline (Kalyaan et al.
2023; Easterwood et al. 2024; Krijt et al. 2025). The
inner (6au) gap of Sz 65 might correspond to the "traf-
fic jam" regime modeled by Mah et al. (2024), which
slows the H2O supply into the inner disk. Conversely,
the secondary’s featureless disk allows pebbles to drift
efficiently across the snowline. Given the similarity be-
tween the binary disks, our study provides further evi-
dence under controlled stellar age and metallicity that
inner disk gaps regulate pebble drift, consequently shap-
ing the cold water reservoir in the inner disk.
According to AGE-PRO measurements, the millime-
tre dust masses of the primary and the secondary are
19.4 and 3.0M⊕ (Deng et al. 2025), and the gas masses
are 1.9+1.1
−0.6 ×10−3 M⊙ and 7.6+11
−4.0 ×10−4 M⊙ (Trapman
et al. 2025). The larger contrast in millimetre dust mass
is consistent with more efficient depletion of mm-sized
grains in the secondary through radial drift and dust
growth in a compact disk.
Efficient pebble drift may also increase the inner-disk
opacity, partially obscuring molecular emission from the
emitting layers (Sellek et al. 2025; Houge et al. 2025).
Such an opacity effect could contribute to the overall
lower molecular fluxes in the secondary, but it does not
naturally explain the relative enrichment of the cold
H2O component.
Of the factors considered, differences in disk structure
offer the most robust explanation for the observed fea-
tures in the secondary: the excess of cold water, the
lower dust-to-gas ratio, and the overall lower molecular
fluxes.
4.3. Uncertainties and Caveats
We stress that analyzing a single pair of binary disks
is insufficient to conclusively determine how dust disk
structures shape inner-disk chemistry. Indeed, several
uncertainties remain in this study: although we have
argued that the accretion rate difference is unlikely to
drive the cold water excess, its potential influence on
longer-term disk evolution cannot be fully excluded with
current data. In addition, two notable model underpre-
dictions in the organic wavelength region remain unex-
plained (see Appendix G), which might have resulted in
the unphysical C2H2 model parameters. While a single
pair of binaries could suffer from case-specific uncertain-
ties, future observations on more wide-separation binary
disks will greatly enhance our understanding of disk evo-
lution under controlled age and metallicity.
5. SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS
We perform a detailed comparative analysis of the
JWST/MIRI spectra of the wide-separation binary
Sz 65 and Sz 66, primarily yielding the following results:
1. The two disks exhibit remarkably similar inner-
diskmolecularemission. Inparticular, CO 2, HCN,
and the hot (∼ 750 K) H2O component have com-
parable best-fit temperatures and column densi-
ties, with the secondary (Sz 66) showing system-
atically smaller emitting areas. The non-LTE sup-
pression for H2O lines in the 5.5–8.5µm range is
also similar.
2. Both the H2O line-flux diagnostics and the LTE
slab models indicate a significant excess of warm
(∼ 450 K) and cold (∼ 200 K) water emission in
Sz 66 relative to its hot H2O, HCN, and CO2. This
is consistent with efficient pebble drift in its com-
pact, unstructured disk.
3. After subtracting the molecular slab models, we
detect atomic emission in both disks, including
[Ne II] and [ArII]. The extended [NeII] emission
in Sz 66 suggests a tentative detection of a jet.
Binaries offer naturally controlled samples of stellar
age, metallicity, and environment. Given that the pri-
mary (Sz 65) has gaps in the dust disk while the sec-
ondary (Sz 66) does not, the cold water excess in the sec-
ondary is consistent with current theories in which gaps
regulate pebble drift, and pebble drift replenishes inner-
disk water through the sublimation of ice mantles. This
study demonstrates the potential of wide-separation bi-
naries for understanding the chemistry and evolution of
protoplanetary disks.

A. CONTINUUM SUBTRACTION
We followed the continuum subtraction process presented in Pontoppidan et al. (2024) and Banzatti et al. (2025),
which removes dust continuum while keeping narrow gas lines. The empirical method assumes an emission-dominated
spectrum and excludes regions where absorption spikes are present. We obtain the continuum by iteratively applying
median filters and retaining the lower flux, followed by a second-order Savitzky–Golay filter (Savitzky & Golay 1964).
The windows of median filters are 151 and 91 for 4.9–9.2µm and 9.2–28.5µm. To avoid continuum overestimation
caused by dense clustering of lines, we exclude and interpolate over the following regions from continuum estimation:
6.538–6.603µm (H2O), 6.784–6.89µm (H2O), 13.4–14.1µm (C2H2, HCN), 14.40–14.435µm (H2O), 14.74–14.77µm
(H2O), 14.91–15.01µm (CO2), and 28–28.5µm (low S/N). We selected a set of line-free regions identified iniSLAT,
and a final offset is applied according to the flux at these regions. Figure 16 and 17 show the continuum estimation
process of Sz 65 and Sz 66.

F. ISOTOPOLOGUE DETECTIONS
We inspect two isotopologues in the binary:13CO2 and 13CCH2. After subtracting the model obtained from fitting
13–27µm, we fit the two molecules individually and evaluated the∆χ2 before and after including each molecule. The
noise level is determined using the standard deviation of a line-free region from 15.90µm to 15.94µm, following the
approach in Grant et al. (2023). The signal of13CO2 is very tentative in both disks, whereas13CCH2 is not detected.
The best-fit 13CO2 parameters are listed in Table 2. The limited residual signal and the optically-thin nature of
13CO2 lead to degeneracy of multiple parameters, so the uncertainties cannot be derived with confidence, and the
best-fit values are just coarse estimates. The temperature is relatively low compared to12CO2, which is commonly
seen in previous detections because the traced gas is deeper in the disk (Grant et al. 2023; Vlasblom et al. 2025).
The best-fit mass ratios of13CO2 to 12CO2 for the primary (Sz 65) and the secondary (Sz 66) are 1:50 and 1:10,
respectively. The secondary value appears elevated relative to the canonical ISM ratio of 1:68 (Milam et al. 2005),
but this comparison is uncertain and may be affected by optical-depth effects, as13CO2 can trace deeper layers. To

column density by the canonical ISM ratio to assess whether the isotopologue signals are consistent with expectation.

G. SLAB MODEL RESIDUALS
We identify two relatively broad residuals of our slab models at 13.55µm and 13.8µm that appear in both disks and
cannot be explained by other molecular species or atomic lines after careful examination. Figure 23 zooms in on this
wavelength region and highlights the underpredicted features.
The underpredictions at 13.55µm coincide with the wavelength of an OH quadruplet. The current three-component
OH model, which assumes LTE, does not reproduce this feature successfully enough. However, the OH prompt emission
originating from UV dissociation of H2O might explain the discrepancy. Specifically, the non-LTE prompt emission
at rotational level N = 22 contributes to the flux at 13.55µm. The OH prompt emission in protoplanetary disks is
discussed in detail in Tabone et al. (2024). Further evidence is required to confirm the origin of this underprediction.
