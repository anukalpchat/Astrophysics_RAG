ABSTRACT
The Cosmic Archipelago is an ensemble of galaxies, strongly lensed by the Hubble Frontier Fields cluster MACS J0416, showing
some of the most extreme physical properties known atz∼6.14. In this work, we combine JWST/NIRCam with deep VLT/X-Shooter
and JWST/NIRSpec IFU, to perform a joint spectrophotometric analysis from the far ultraviolet to red optical rest-frame. In particular,
we focus on CA4, a UV-faint (M UV =−17.7), isolated, and compact (r e =81±11 pc) galaxy atz=6.1446, magnified by a factor
µ=3.73. CA4 is a young, low-mass (M ⋆ =4.3×10 6 M⊙), star-forming (SFR=0.46 M ⊙/yr), and metal-poor (Z≃0.02 Z ⊙) galaxy,
efficient producer of ionizing photons (log(ξion/erg−1 Hz)≃25.5). Its properties put CA4 at the poorly explored interface between the
regimes of massive stellar cluster and dwarf galaxies at the epoch of reionization. Moreover, CA4 presents large Lyα(f Lyα
esc ∼43%)
and Lyman-continuum (f esc ∼47%) escape fraction values, consistent with the small Lyαvelocity offset (∆v∼100 km s −1) and the
extremely blue UV-continuum slope (β=−3.10). These characteristics suggest that such UV-faint, metal-poor galaxies may have a
major contribution to cosmic reionization. Additionally, we confirm five additional systems at the redshift of the Cosmic Archipelago,
magnified by factors ranging from∼1.2 to 12.5. They are all young (mass-weighted ages<11 Myr) and metal-poor galaxies
(Z<0.05 Z ⊙), spanning a wide range of stellar masses and star-formation rates. Given the large number of these bursty star-forming
galaxies in a relatively small cosmic volume, we estimated that all the currently known members of the Cosmic Archipelago result
in a significant overabundance atz∼6 (∆z≃0.08), with a derived overdensity factor ofδ gal =12.3 +6.6
−4.6. These results highlight the
Cosmic Archipelago as an unprecedented laboratory for studying the earliest groups of low-mass and low-metallicity galaxies during
the epoch of reionization.

1. Introduction
The discovery and characterization of galaxies in the first bil-
lion years after the Big Bang have provided crucial insights into

the physical processes that shaped the early stages of galaxy as-
sembly and cosmic reionization (e.g. Stark 2016; Dayal & Fer-
rara 2018). Thanks to deep imaging and spectroscopy taken by
ground-based facilities, such as the Very Large Telescope (VLT),
and space-based observatories, such as the Hubble Space Tele-
scope (HST), a large population of galaxies has been identified
atz≳6 (e.g., Bouwens et al. 2015; Finkelstein et al. 2015;
Vanzella et al. 2011; Caruana et al. 2014). These observations
revealed that galaxies across the reionization era are remarkably
different from those typically observed at later times, while also
displaying a significant diversity in their physical properties. The
Lyman-alpha (Lyα) line has long been used to trace ionized hy-
drogen and regions of escaping ionizing radiation, but its detec-
tion rate decreases atz≳6 due to the increasing neutral fraction
of cosmic hydrogen, which can easily suppress the visibility of
Lyα(e.g., Pentericci et al. 2018; Ouchi et al. 2020). Despite this
expected suppression, thanks to the advent of the James Webb
Space Telescope (JWST), with its coverage of the infrared wave-
length range and its high sensitivity, a growing number of Lyα
emitting galaxies atz≳7 have recently been observed (e.g.,
Tang et al. 2023; Bunker et al. 2023; Witten et al. 2024). Their
detections suggest that some galaxies at such early epochs must
lie within large ionized bubbles, which allow the Lyαphotons
to escape (e.g. Mason et al. 2018; Chen et al. 2024), or they
may be experiencing dynamical processes such as mergers or
interactions that modify the surrounding gas kinematics and in-
creases the transmission of Lyα(e.g., Whitler et al. 2024; Ning
et al. 2024). Moreover, with JWST rest-frame optical lines such
Hα, Hβ, and [O III] became accessible for high-zstudies, and
are routinely detected atz∼6−10, enabling robust measure-
ments of physical properties such as the star-formation rate, ion-
ization conditions, dust attenuation, and metallicity (e.g. Curti
et al. 2023; Sanders et al. 2023; Boyett et al. 2024; Matthee
et al. 2023; Castellano et al. 2022; Bunker et al. 2023; Atek et al.
2023; Messa et al. 2025). Typically, JWST-detected faint star-
forming galaxies atz≳6 present blue UV-continuumβslopes
(β≲−2.5, wheref λ ∝λ β, Calzetti et al. 1994) suggesting very
young stellar populations, low metallicity, and negligible dust
content (e.g., Bouwens et al. 2010; Bolamperti et al. 2023; Sax-
ena et al. 2024; Donnan et al. 2025). Such properties become
more pronounced towards lower luminosities, consistent with
fainter galaxies representing some of the most metal-poor and
dust-free star-forming systems in the Universe (e.g. Bouwens
et al. 2012; Austin et al. 2025; Felicioni et al. 2026), although
this trend presents a significant scatter (e.g., Morales et al. 2024;
Jecmen et al. 2026). At the same time, many galaxies at this
epoch exhibit large ionizing photon production efficiencies (ξion,
Leitherer & Heckman 1995; Bouwens et al. 2016; Emami et al.
2020), with typical values log(ξ ion)≳25.3 erg −1 Hz, signifi-
cantly higher than those inferred for more massive systems at
lower redshift (e.g., Castellano et al. 2023). Such values mod-
erately increase as well for low-luminosity galaxies, suggesting
that they may be major contributors to the cosmic reionization
(Prieto-Lyon et al. 2023; Izotov et al. 2024; Simmonds et al.
2024b).
Morphological studies revealed that typically such faint
galaxies are remarkably compact, with effective radii typically
below 1 kpc (e.g., Miller et al. 2025; Stephenson et al. 2025).
Thanks to strong gravitational lensing, whose magnification
power allows us to observe fainter and more compact systems
that would otherwise remain undetected or unresolved, we could
characterize objects with sizes of<100 pc, reaching also scales
down to a few pc on the source plane in the most magnified cases
(e.g., Rivera-Thorsen et al. 2017; Vanzella et al. 2023a). Lensed

The majority of these objects required very large magnifi-

to the lensing critical lines. Here, the lensed volume is small,
preventing any characterization of their environment on larger
scales. However, in some rare cases, observations revealed that
faint, metal-poor galaxies can occur in overdense structures,
where several systems at nearly identical redshifts are clustered
within a few tens of comoving Mpc 3. Such overdensities have
been identified both in blank and lensed fields, and represent
the progenitors of present-day galaxy groups or clusters (e.g.,
Toshikawa et al. 2012; Finkelstein et al. 2022; Morishita et al.
2024, 2025b). They provide valuable laboratories for under-
standing not only the evolution of individual galaxies, but also
the environmental processes, such as gas accretion, interactions,
and shared enrichment, which regulate their growth (e.g., Fensch
& Bournaud 2021; Feldmann et al. 2023).
In this context, we focus here on theCosmic Archipelago
(Fig. 1), a system atz∼6.14 lensed by the Hubble Frontier
Fields (HFF) cluster of galaxies MACS J0416.1−2403 (here-
after, MACS 0416). It was initially identified as an extended Lyα
arc (system 2 of Caminha et al. 2017), later enriched with differ-
ent systems at similar redshifts (e.g., Vanzella et al. 2021). As
the number of identified members in this system continues to
grow, and in anticipation of additional sources or substructures
that may be revealed by future observations, we adopt the nam-
ing scheme proposed by Messa et al. (in prep.). In this scheme,
the main systems are labeled with the prefix CA (for Cosmic
Archipelago), while individual “islands” receive the “i” prefix.
We define individual CA systems as those with a projected phys-
ical size≲1 kpc on the source plane. Accordingly, the D1, T1,
and UT1 systems (e.g., Vanzella et al. 2017; Calura et al. 2021;
Messa et al. 2025) are now renamed CA1-i1, CA1-i2, and CA1-
i3, respectively. In this work, we focus in particular on CA4,
previously known as D2 (see Sect. 3.1), a metal-poor, young,

2. Observations and data reduction
2.1. Ancillary data
The central part of MACS 0416 benefits from a rich collec-
tion of data, both photometric and spectroscopic, as it is one
of the galaxy clusters that act as lenses with the largest num-
ber of lensed background sources and a very robust strong lens-
ing model (e.g., Zitrin et al. 2013; Bergamini et al. 2023; Ri-
htaršiˇc et al. 2025). CA4 is included in the HFF pointing of
the MACS 0416 field, consisting of deep observations in seven
HST filters, namely the HST/ACS F435W, F606W, F814W, and
HST/WFC3 F105W, F125W, F140W, and F160W (Lotz et al.
2017; Koekemoer et al. 2014).
CA4 is also included in the field of view that was tar-
geted with the Multi Unit Spectroscopic Explorer (MUSE) ob-
servations pointing in the north-east region of the galaxy clus-
ter, taken between November 2017 and August 2019 (Prog.ID
0100.A-0763(A), PI: E. Vanzella), and GTO observations, taken
in November 2014 (Prog.ID 094.A-0115B, PI: J. Richard), for a
total combined on-sky integration time of 17.1 hours (see Cam-
inha et al. 2017; Vanzella et al. 2021, for a comprehensive de-
scription of the observations and for the final catalogs). The fi-
nal reduced MUSE datacube covers a field of view of 1 ′ ×1 ′,
spatially sampled with 0.2 ′′/pix, and a PSF of 0.6 ′′. The wave-
length range covers from 4700 Å to 9350 Å, with a dispersion
of 1.25 Å/pix, and a spectral resolution of 2.6 Å approximately
constant across the entire spectral range.
2.2. JWST/NIRCam
We exploited MACS 0416 JWST Near Infrared Camera (NIR-
Cam) images from the Prime Extragalactic Areas for Reioniza-
tion and Lensing Science program (PEARLS, program 1176,
PI: R. Windhorst; Windhorst et al. 2023), where the mosaics
also included data from other public archival programs (1208,
PI: C. Willott; 2883, PI: F. Sun; and 3538, PI: E. Iani). These
include observations in eight JWST filters: four in the short
wavelength channel (SW; F090W, F115W, F150W, and F200W)
and four in the long wavelength channel (LW; F277W, F356W,
F410M, and F444W), with exposure times between 15100 and
17700 s per filter. Several additional medium bands are available
(F140M, F162M, F182M, F210M, F250M, F300M, F335M,
F360M, F430M, F460M, F480M) but, given their shallowness
with respect to broadbands, we did not include them in our anal-
ysis (for completeness, we include their photometry in Fig. A.1).
The adopted data reduction procedure for the full set of mo-
saics that we use for the results in this paper is detailed in Wind-
horst et al. (2023) and Yan et al. (2023), where these steps fol-
lowed the approaches originally described by Koekemoer et al.
(2011), with relevant parts updated for JWST as needed. In sum-
mary, the data from the Mikulski Archive for Space Telescopes
were processed with the standard JWST pipeline 1 (Bushouse
et al. 2023), based on the pipeline version 1.9.4 in the calibration
context ofjwst_1063.pmap. All the different exposures were
aligned through their astrometry being registered with respect to
the public HFF products (Lotz et al. 2017), and then combined
into a final image. Each filter has been reduced by adopting a
final pixel size of 0.04 ′′/pix, and the SW filters also with a fi-
nal pixel size of 0.02′′/pix. We show the stacks of the SW (with
0.02′′/pix) and LW (with 0.04 ′′/pix) images in Fig. 2. The PSF
FWHM in individual filters ranges from 0.05 ′′ in the F090W
filter to 0.15′′ in the F444W filter. To correctly accomplish aper-
ture photometry with widely different filters, we created images
that are PSF-matched (see the details on the adopted kernels in
Merlin et al. 2022) to the F444W image.
2.3. JWST/NIRSpec
We observed CA4 with the JWST Near Infrared Spectrograph
(NIRSpec), in the Integral Field Unit (IFU) mode, as one of the
five pointings of the Cycle 1 GO program 1908 (PI: E. Vanzella).
It was observed on August 26th 2023 for 2 hours, by adopting a
small-cycling dithering pattern with eight dithers, with the high-
resolution setup composed of the G395H grism and the F290LP
filter combination.
We reduced the data making use of the Space Tele-
scope Science Institute’s (STScI) version 1.14.0 of the JWST
Pipeline (Bushouse et al. 2023), and the reference files con-
tained in the Calibration Reference Data System (CRDS) con-
textjwst_1230.pmap, used to calibrate the data. We follow the
data reduction process detailed in Messa et al. (2025). In sum-
mary, it consists of three stages. In the first stage we corrected
for effects related to the detector, e.g., we applied bias and dark
subtraction to the raw frames, linearity correction, and cosmic
rays rejection. At the end of this stage, we subtracted the median
value of each spectral column, to remove the residual 1/fnoise
signatures. In the second stage, the output products of the first
stage were wavelength, flat-field and flux calibrated. We then ex-
cluded the saturated pixels, and those labeled with a bad flat-field
solution. In the third stage, we identified and masked the defects
and the spikes associated with the detector coordinates through
the “persistence” parameter (R), defined as the ratio between the
median signal on the spaxel and its 1σmedian deviation, adopt-
ing a value ofR=5.
We tested different options for optimizing the background
subtraction (see Appendix A of Messa et al. 2025). In this work,
we adopted a spatially varying component, estimated for each
slice using±30 slices (corresponding to approximately±200 Å)
blueward and redward from the actual slice and then subtracted
it from the cube. This method is particularly effective with this
kind of pointing, where the continuum of the target is not de-
tected. We checked that this procedure does not significantly af-
fect the estimated fluxes for the observed emission lines, that are

2.4. VLT/X-Shooter
CA4 has been observed with VLT/X-Shooter (Vernet et al.
2011) for a total integration time of 27 hours, between August
2023 and October 2024 (program 111.253B.001, PI: A. Bo-
lamperti), with clear sky, optimal seeing conditions (typically
ranging from 0.5 ′′ to 0.8′′), and with airmass≲1.4. X-Shooter
is a multi-wavelength spectrograph, composed of three prism-
cross-dispersed échelle spectrographs optimized for the UV-
Blue (UVB, 300-559.5 nm), Visible (VIS, 559.5-1024 nm) and
Near-IR (NIR, 1024-2480 nm). X-Shooter slits are 11′′ long, and
we adopted a slit width of 0.9 ′′, with the K-band blocking fil-
ter. This setup corresponds to a spectral resolution of 8900 and
5600 in the VIS and NIR arms, respectively. The slit was ori-
ented at 167◦ North to East. We adopted the nodding technique,
which is the most efficient for subtracting lines in the NIR arm
where the sky background is stronger. We adopt a nod throw
of 4′′, and a jitter box of 0.4 ′′. In order to maximize the result-
ing signal-to-noise ratio (S/N), we adopted NIR exposure times
longer than the standard ones for the first 15 observing blocks
(OBs), namely 1700 s in the UVB and VIS arms and 1800 s in
the NIR arm. After December 2023, we relaxed this condition to
make the scheduling of our observations easier, and observed 6
OBs with 1200 s exposures in the three arms, with observations
divided in two cycles (ABBA scheme). Two additional OBs were
observed with 1200 s exposures in the single cycle scheme.
We reduced the resulting observations with the X-Shooter
pipeline (Modigliani et al. 2010), through the ESO Recipe Ex-
ecution Tool (EsoRex; ESO CPL Development Team 2015),
which allows more control over the parameters used in data re-
duction, particularly important when reducing data of such faint
objects and in such lowS/Nregime, as the case of CA4. The
pipeline identifies non-linear (bad) pixels, computes the master
bias (UVB and VIS), master dark (NIR), and master flat frames,
and accurately traces the order centers. Then it produces recti-
fied and wavelength calibrated frames, making use of arc lamp
lines. We checked that the wavelength positions are fully consis-
tent with what is derived from MUSE. Finally, we corrected the
wavelength calibration from air to vacuum, and for barycentric
correction, as observations taken several months apart showed
velocity shifts up to∼40 km s −1 due to the Earth motion around
the Sun. We executed this procedure for all the OBs separately,
obtaining 22 flux and wavelength calibrated 2D spectra. We
added them by aligning their wavelength and sky positions, per-
forming a 3σclipping rejection for each pixel. The Lyαline is
marginally detected in each individual OB (S/N∼2), allow-
ing us to test the goodness of the alignment. We evaluated the
uncertainties by creating a 2D error spectrum where each pixel
value is the root-mean-square (rms) of its respective values in
the 22 OBs. The resulting 2D spectrum spans from∼3000 Å to
21000 Å, with a pixel size of 0.16 ′′, 0.16 ′′, 0.21 ′′ and a wave-
length sampling of 0.2 Å/pix, 0.2 Å/pix, and 0.6 Å/pix for the
UVB, VIS, and NIR arms, respectively.
2.5. Lensing model
We adopted the strong lensing model of MACS 0416 presented
in Bergamini et al. (2023). This model is based on photomet-
ric observations taken with HST within the Cluster Lensing And
Supernova survey with Hubble (CLASH; Postman et al. 2012,
16 filters) and HFF (Lotz et al. 2017, 7 filters) surveys, and spec-
troscopic observations taken with the VLT-VIsible MultiObject
Spectrograph (VIMOS, presented in Balestra et al. 2016) and
MUSE (see Sect. 2.1 and Vanzella et al. 2021). The model fi-
nally makes use of Chandra X-ray observations (Bonamigo et al.
2018), used to constrain the hot gas content.
In summary, the MACS 0416 strong lensing model exploits
237 spectroscopically confirmed multiple images, at 0.9<z<
6.6, from 88 background sources. The rms difference between
the observed and the model-predicted positions of the multiple
images is 0.43 ′′. The projected total mass profile of the deflec-
tor cluster of galaxies is parametrized with three extended halos,
representing the cluster-scale halos mainly due to dark matter
(DM), a hot gas component, and 212 cluster member galaxies,
whose mass profile has been linked through scaling relations cal-
ibrated on 64 cluster members with stellar kinematics measured
from MUSE (Bergamini et al. 2021).
When studying strongly lensed objects, such as CA4 and the
entire Cosmic Archipelago, it is key to rely on a robust lens-
ing model (e.g., Rivera-Thorsen et al. 2017; Adamo et al. 2024;
Vanzella et al. 2023b; Claeyssens et al. 2025; Meštri´c et al. 2022;
Furtak et al. 2025), because the intrinsic properties of the lensed
objects are inferred from the observed ones making use of the
local deflection and magnification maps. Given that CA4 lies
fairly distant from the lensing critical lines atz=6.14, it is
not significantly distorted, and thus in this work we use the total
magnification factor. We measuredµ=3.73 +0.07
−0.08 considering the
median, 16th and 84th percentile, respectively, of the distribution
of the magnification factors within a circular region (r=0.3 ′′)
including CA4 in 500 different realizations of the strong lensing
model, randomly extracted from the final Monte Carlo Markov
Chain (MCMC).
3. Analysis of CA4
3.1. Previous studies on CA4
CA4 (RA, dec=4:16:10.32,−24:03:25.96) was included in the

tinuum (m=28.33±0.09 in the HST F150W filter, Castellano
et al. 2016; Merlin et al. 2016). Successively, Livermore et al.
(2017) included CA4 in theirz∼6 sample used to investigate
the faint end of the UV luminosity function, and measured an
intrinsicM 1500 magnitude of−17.28 +0.41

3.2. Photometric properties
Despite CA4 being isolated, we found that the measured magni-
tudes could significantly vary between different extraction meth-
ods, due to slightly different sky estimates and possible flux
losses in the extended tails. We thus measured the photome-
try with three different approaches: i) we performed aperture
photometry and subtracted the sky background estimated as a
sigma-clipped median in an annular region, included within cir-
cles with radii of 0.35 ′′ and 0.6′′; ii) as in the previous method,
but with the sky background extracted from empty regions 1−2′′
from CA4; iii) we extracted the (total) isophotal magnitudes
(MAG_ISO) with SExtractor (Bertin & Arnouts 1996). Uncer-
tainties, for the three methods, are directly propagated from the
sigma images, and are modified to keep into account correlations
between pixels introduced in the drizzling (with theASTRORMS
Python module 2). For methods i and ii, we considered differ-
ent circular apertures centered on CA4, fromr=0.12 ′′ to
0.30′′, and both from the original images and from those PSF-
matched. Given that the CA4 system is regular and isolated, we
did not find any significant difference between them, and thus
in the following we will refer to the results obtained with an
aperture ofr=0.2 ′′ (shown in Fig. 2), intended to include
≳80% of the total flux in the UV rest-frame, and with PSF-
matched images. We applied a corresponding aperture correc-
tion of−0.322 (assuming PSF-like sources), and galactic red-
dening corrections (Cardelli et al. 1989; Schlafly & Finkbeiner
2011; O’Donnell 1994), ranging from 0.05 in the F090W fil-
ter to 0.003 mag in the F444W filter. The comparison between
the measured magnitudes is described in Appendix A. In sum-
mary, even if the different methods give rise to slightly differ-
ent magnitudes, only marginally consistent in some filters, the
derived spectro-photometric properties (e.g., photometric rest-
frame equivalent widths of the lines, log(ξion) and the properties
derived from SED fitting in Sect. 3.3) are robust and consistent
within the uncertainties. In the following, we take as fiducial the
photometry obtained with method ii, as the band excesses (corre-
sponding to emission line fluxes) estimated with this method best
agree with those obtained from spectroscopy (Sect. 3.4). The re-
sulting photometry is shown in Fig. 3, and compared with the re-
sults obtained with the other methods in Fig. A.1. We measured a
magnitude in the F115W filter, tracing the rest-frame∼1500 Å,
ofm F115W =27.58±0.05, which corresponds to an absolute UV

et al. 2010; Bouwens et al. 2010; Cullen et al. 2023; Topping
et al. 2022). Such blue values, already serendipitously found
with HST atz∼6−7 (e.g., Bouwens et al. 2010; Labbé et al.

et al. 2023), are systematically detected with JWST up toz∼10,
showing also that UV-fainter galaxies tend to have bluerβslopes
(e.g., Topping et al. 2022; Nanayakkara et al. 2023; Austin et al.
2025; Cullen et al. 2023; Cullen et al. 2024; Saxena et al. 2026;
Yanagisawa et al. 2025).

is convolved with the spectroscopic output, and three parame-

of the wavelength in our NIRSpec observational setup.

3.4. NIRSpec spectroscopic properties
In order to study the spectroscopic properties of CA4, we ex-
tracted and summed the spectra of all the spaxels within the
aperture shown in Fig. 2, consisting of a circular aperture with
radius of 0.3 ′′. We do not detect any stellar and nebular contin-
uum emission, but we identified strong Hα, Hβ, [OIII]λ5007 and
[OIII]λ4959 emission lines. We centered the circular aperture on
the peak of the narrow-band image obtained by collapsing the
cube around the detected lines. The final 1D spectrum is shown
in the bottom panels of Fig. 2. We also extracted the spectrum
from a smaller circular aperture (r=0.2 ′′, intended to include
only the nucleated central region), finding no significant differ-
ences.
We fit the four lines detected in NIRSpec with Gaussian
profiles. We fixed the width and position of the Hβline from
that of Hα, after correcting for the different instrumental reso-
lution, while leaving uninformative priors on the other parame-
ters. We derived a joint spectroscopic flux-weighted redshift of
zspec =6.1446±0.0003, in agreement with the previous Lyα
measurements (Vanzella et al. 2017; Meštri ´c et al. 2022). We
will discuss the small shift between the Lyαpeak and the sys-
temic velocity in Sect. 3.5. The main spectroscopic results are
reported in Table 2. The measured line fluxes correspond to a
Hα/Hβflux ratio of 2.34±0.44, consistent within 2σwith the
expected value of 2.86 relative to the case B recombination for
a 10 4 K gas with no extinction (e.g., Storey & Hummer 1995;
Dopita & Sutherland 2003), and a [O III]λ5007/[OIII]λ4959 flux
ratio of 2.97±0.53, consistent with the 2.98 value expected from
atomic physics (e.g., Storey & Zeippen 2000). We also obtained
a tentative detection of the Hγline, with aS/N∼2. The mea-
sured Hα/Hγratio of 7.3±1.5 is consistent with the case B re-
combination scenario (Storey & Hummer 1995), which we will
adopt in the following. However, it is interesting to highlight that
the slightly lower value of the Hα/Hβflux ratio could also be
compatible with modest departures from case B conditions, ex-
pected with largerT e,n e, low-metallicity nebulae or in extreme
star-forming systems where radiative-transfer effects may mod-
ify the intrinsic Balmer ratios (e.g., Reddy et al. 2023; Scarlata
et al. 2024; Sandles et al. 2024; McClymont et al. 2025).
We checked the consistency between our NIRCam and NIR-
Spec observations by comparing the fluxes of the observed
emission lines with the photometric excesses. We assumed in
both cases that the level of stellar continuum, which is not
detected in NIRSpec, is represented by the medium-band fil-
ter F410M, whose wavelength range is completely included in
the NIRSpec coverage, but does not include any of the de-
tected emission line. Thus, we compared the NIRSpec Hαand
Hβ+[OIII]λλ4959,5007 fluxes with the excesses in the NIR-
Cam F444W and F356W filters, respectively. To obtain more
robust comparisons, we make use of the measured fluxes of
the brightest lines, Hαand [O III]λ5007, and measured the Hβ
and [OIII]λ4959 fluxes by assuming the line ratios from Storey
& Hummer (1995) and Storey & Zeippen (2000). We mea-
sured an excess in the F444W filter ofF F444W exc. =(1.69±
0.32)×10 −18 erg s−1 cm−2, consistent withF Hα =(1.84±
0.17)×10 −18 erg s−1 cm−2 and an excess in the F356W filter
ofF F356W exc. =(4.01±0.55)×10 −18 erg s−1 cm−2, consistent
withF Hβ+[OIII] =(3.32±0.54)×10 −18 erg s−1 cm−2. Such photo-
metric excesses correspond to rest-frame line equivalent widths
(EW0) of (1679±203) Å for the Hαand (2613±242) Å for
the Hβ+[OIII]λλ4959,5007, consistent with the values inferred
from the measured line fluxes, reported in Table 2. We discuss
how such values change among the recovered photometry using
different methods in Appendix A.

3.5. The 1D X-Shooter spectrum
We made use of the final 2D spectra from X-Shooter to optimally
extract the 1D spectrum of CA4. We adopted an aperture of 1.4′′
centered on the Lyαline, clearly detected at∼869 nm. Such
aperture, showed in Fig. 4, is designed to maximize the flux of
CA4, which is a compact source.
Given the broad wavelength coverage of X-Shooter, several
lines usually adopted in similar studies are included in our ob-
servations atz=6.1446. The UVB arm covers a rest-frame in-
terval which is bluer than the Balmer break (λ <912 Å). The
VIS arm, covering the rest-frame interval up to∼1450 Å, in-
cludes the Lyαand N Vλ1240 lines, while the NIR arm (rest-
frame∼1450 Å< λ <2950 Å) includes the C IVλλ1548,1550,
HeIIλ1640, OIII]λ1666, and CIII]λ1908 lines. Among them, only
the Lyαis detected. In the following, we will first discuss the
Lyαline, and then present upper limits on the undetected lines
and their interpretation, in particular for the HeIIλ1640 line. We
show their 1D and 2D spectra in Fig. 4.
The Lyαline is the strongest tracer of ionized hydrogen in
systems hosting young stellar populations (Partridge & Peebles
1967) and is often detected in the high-zUniverse (e.g., Zitrin
et al. 2015; Caminha et al. 2023; Nakane et al. 2024). As a res-
onant line, Lyαphotons experience numerous scattering events
before being able to escape, depending on the H I column den-
sity, geometry, kinematics (Adams 1972; Dijkstra 2014), and on
quantum mechanical probabilities (Stenflo 1980). This informa-
tion is encrypted in the surface brightness and spectral profile
of the observed Lyα(Dijkstra 2014). In the following, we will
discuss the Lyαemitted by CA4, from the X-Shooter 1D spec-
tra described above and from the MUSE datacube, extracted
within an aperture ofr=0.5 ′′ centered on the Lyαpeak. The
Lyαis characterized by a redshifted component, shifted with re-
spect to the systemic redshift by 111±20 km s −1 (from MUSE)
and 90.6+3.4
−3.5 km s−1 (from X-Shooter). Such low shift suggests
that the Lyαphotons escape through low neutral column density
channels, withN HI ≲10 18.5cm−2 (e.g., Verhamme et al. 2015).
We measured a total Lyαflux of (6.72±0.21)×10−18 erg s−1 cm−2
from MUSE data. This corresponds to a Lyα/Hαratio of 3.77±
0.38. If we compare it with the expected value in case B re-
combination of 8.7 (e.g., Brocklehurst 1971; Hayes et al. 2010),
this would correspond to an escape fraction for the Lyαof
f Lyα
esc =(43.3±4.3)%. Whilef Lyα
esc <10% are commonly mea-
sured in galaxies atz∼2−3 (e.g., Hayes et al. 2010; Steidel et al.
2011; Iani et al. 2021), larger Lyαescape fractionsf Lyα
esc >30%
have been reported for dust-poor systems similar to CA4, such
as high redshift powerful Lyαemitters (LAE) (e.g., Hayes et al.
2011; Steidel et al. 2011; Saxena et al. 2023), in particular for
faint systems and with very blueβslopes (Tang et al. 2024;
Chen et al. 2024), and are predicted by simulations for dust-free
LAE (e.g., Dijkstra et al. 2016; Choustikov et al. 2024). This
means that∼40% of the emitted Lyαphotons escape and reach
us, while the remaining fraction is either lost and dispersed by
the numerous scattering events, or absorbed by the intergalactic
medium (IGM).
Given that the Lyαradiative transfer process is non trivial
and analytic and exact solutions are available only for a very
limited number of simplified cases, Monte Carlo radiative trans-
fer codes are typically employed. To interpret the physical in-
formation from the Lyαprofile, the “shell model" is usually
adopted (e.g., Ahn et al. 2000; Verhamme et al. 2006). Even
if this model represents a simplified version of the reality and
can be affected by many degeneracies (e.g., Gronke et al. 2016;
Li & Gronke 2022), it offers a clear interpretation and allows
to decrypt the information in the Lyαline. We fit our 1D Lyα
spectrum of CA4 using the publicly available packagezELDA
(Gurung-López et al. 2022, 2025), which is based onFLaREON
(Gurung-López et al. 2019). We fit the line with the Monte Carlo
Markov Chain methodology ofzELDA, which represents the typ-
ical approach (e.g., Gronke et al. 2015). We adopted a homoge-
neous expanding thin shell model, described by six free parame-
ters: the expansion velocity (vexp), the HI column density (NHI),
the dust optical depth (τ), the intrinsic FWHM of the Lyαemis-
sion (FWHMLyα
i ), its equivalent width (EWLyα
i ), and the systemic
redshift (z sys). We fixed the CA4’s redshift, and added a gaus-
sian prior to FWHM Lyα
i to match the measured FWHM of Hα
and its uncertainty. We used uninformative priors on the remain-
ing free parameters. We ran 200 burn-in MCMC steps, followed
by 300 steps to find the best-fit values of the parameters. We
then used such values to randomly generate 10 5 models, using
a bootstrapping technique. We obtainedv exp =(50±5) km s −1,
NHI =10 (18.7±0.1) cm−2,τ <0.05, and EW Lyα
i =(21.1±2.3) Å.

5.2. An overabundance of bursty galaxies atz∼6.14
To assess whether the systems in the Cosmic Archipelago trace
a larger structure in the early Universe, we estimated the galaxy
overabundance aroundz∼6.14, through the overdensity factor
δgal =(N obs −¯n)/¯n(e.g., Chiang et al. 2013), where ¯nis the ex-
pected number of galaxies within the same volume in the field.
We estimated ¯nby integrating the luminosity function (LF) de-
fined in Bouwens et al. (2021) atz∼6, using their evolution of
the LF parameters to infer their values atz=6.14. We integrate
the LF down toM UV =−16.7, corresponding to our faintest ob-
ject. We then consider an area within a circular aperture with
R=70 kpc (physical), which includes all of our objects once
ray-traced on the source plane (Fig. D.1), and a redshift range
within 6.05 and 6.17, corresponding to approximately 7 Mpc
(physical). We excluded CA9 from this estimate, as it lies in the
outer parts of the galaxy cluster, where the magnification factor
is∼1 and in a region only partially covered with photometric
and spectroscopic observations, which would bias our results.
We obtained an expected number of galaxies of ¯n=0.6±0.1.
With our 8 spectroscopically confirmed galaxies within the con-
sidered volume, we foundδ gal =12.3 +6.6
−4.6, where the uncertain-
ties represent the Poisson noise and the uncertainties on the LF
parameters. This value places the Cosmic Archipelago in a range
for which its identification as a protocluster is ambiguous (Chi-
ang et al. 2013), and is in line with overdensity factors found for
similar galaxy overabundances atz∼5−8 (Toshikawa et al.
2012; Helton et al. 2024; Fudamoto et al. 2025), while is smaller
than those found for larger overdensities at the same redshifts
(e.g., Arribas et al. 2024; Morishita et al. 2025b; Witten et al.
2025). Typically, galaxies that evolve in overdense environments
present older stellar populations, larger metallicities, and higher
dust contents with respect to field galaxies, as they form in nodes
of the cosmic web where the gas accretion from the filaments
can drive early star-formation episodes and an accelerated evo-
lution (e.g., Thomas et al. 2005). For the Cosmic Archipelago,
we observe instead opposite properties, and with the available
data there is no evidence that its different islands will merge into
a common extended dark matter halo. This, together with the
low mass of our galaxies and the moderate overdensity factor,
suggests that the Cosmic Archipelago will more likely evolve
today into a smaller group of dwarf galaxies, rather than into a
massive galaxy cluster. On the other hand, we also remark that
our overdensity factor estimate likely represents a lower limit.
In fact, we counted the entire CA1 system as a single galaxy,

CA1-i3 (previously labelled as D1, T1, and UT1) are subregions
of a common dark matter halo (Calura et al. 2022). However,
Messa et al. (2025) showed that they lie approximately 0.5 to
1 kpc apart on the source plane, further than the typical galaxy
sizes atz∼6 (e.g. Stephenson et al. 2025), and Pascale et al.
(2025) showed that they are separate systems likely to include a

suggest that the overdensity factor quoted above likely represents
a lower limit. Further data and completeness analysis would be
needed to fully characterize the full large-scale structure, which
is besides the aims of this work.
5.3. Metal-poor galaxies with different physical properties
All the galaxies analyzed in this work are found to be very metal-
poor, with gas metallicities from 0.02 to 0.05 Z ⊙. These val-
ues place them among the lowest metallicity systems at high-
zcurrently confirmed (e.g., Vanzella et al. 2023b; Nakajima
et al. 2026; Cai et al. 2025; Morishita et al. 2025a; Vanzella
et al. 2026, and see Fig. 7) and candidate from photometry
(Trussler et al. 2026). Despite their similar chemical properties,
the systems in the Cosmic Archipelago presented in this work
span a wide range of physical properties, with stellar masses
from approximately 4×10 6 to 2×10 8 M⊙, SFRs from 0.06 to
10.2 M ⊙ yr−1, extinctions withA V ∼0 to 0.75, and ages from
less than 1 to 11 Myr. These properties suggest they have dif-
ferent stellar populations, consistent with the divergingβslopes
varying from moderately red (β >−2) to extremely blue (down
toβ∼ −3). On average, they are all powerful emitters of ionizing
photons, with efficiency ranging from log(ξion)=24.8 to 25.5,
consistent with the relation between log(ξ ion) andM UV (Prieto-
Lyon et al. 2023). We remark here that the Cosmic Archipelago
also hosts more extreme cases with log(ξ ion)>26 erg −1 Hz for
CA2-i3 (T2c in Vanzella et al. 2024), and on the opposite with
log(ξion)<24.2 erg −1 Hz (CA3-i1 and CA3-i2 in Messa et al.,
in prep.), relative to UV-bright galaxies with no detected Hα.
The coexistence of such diversity of stellar population prop-
erties with similar metallicities in galaxies lying relatively close
to each other suggests that the environment plays the major role
in defining the chemical enrichment of such systems, rather than
their individual evolutionary histories (e.g., Sánchez Almeida
et al. 2014; Muratov et al. 2017; Feldmann et al. 2023). Inflow of
pristine or near-pristine material continuously dilutes the ISM,
while metal-enriched gas is efficiently expelled by feedback-
driven outflows (e.g., Mandelker et al. 2017; Tamburello et al.
2017; Fensch & Bournaud 2021; Calura et al. 2022). These com-
bined processes naturally maintain galaxies near the observed
0.02–0.05 Z ⊙ regime even after undergoing brief but intense
bursts of star formation.
Altogether, these properties indicate that the Cosmic
Archipelago systems are composed of young, metal-poor stel-
lar populations embedded in low-metallicity gas, producing hard
ionizing spectra and in many cases very blue UV slopes. The ob-
served spread in SFR, age, and attenuation, reflects the different
phases of short starburst episodes in which such galaxies are ob-
served.
6. Conclusions
In this paper, we have exploited new high-resolution
JWST/NIRSpec IFU, NIRCam (PEARLS program), and
VLT/X-Shooter observations to characterize CA4, previously
known as “D2” (Vanzella et al. 2017), and of five new systems,
named from CA5 to CA9, belonging to theCosmic Archipelago
(Vanzella et al. 2024; Messa et al. 2025), lensed by the cluster
of galaxies MACS 0416.
From PEARLS imaging we found CA4 to be compact
(re =81±11 pc) and we measured an extremely blue UV-
continuum slope ofβ=−3.10±0.19, consistent with a very
young, metal-poor and dust-free stellar population. The NIR-
Spec spectrum revealed strong nebular emission (Hα, Hβ, and
[OIII]λλ4959,5007 lines), enabling us to refine the systemic red-
shift of CA4 (z=6.1446±0.0003), and to derive robust phys-
ical properties. CA4 is metal-poor (Z=0.02 Z ⊙), dust free
(as indicated by both the case-B Hα/Hβratio and its extremely
blueβslope) and a highly efficient producer of ionizing pho-
tons (log(ξ ion)=25.5 erg −1). A joint SED fit of spectroscopy
and photometry yielded a stellar mass of 4.3×10 6 M⊙, a mass-
weighted age of∼4 Myr, and a SFR of 0.46 M ⊙ yr−1. These
properties place CA4 among lowest-mass, most efficiently star-
forming galaxies currently known at the reionization epoch, at
the boundary between a compact dwarf galaxy and a massive
stellar clump forming in a larger halo. The uniquely deep X-
Shooter coverage (27 hours) for CA4 provided high-resolution
spectra (R∼5600−8900) from 450 to 2950 Å rest-frame, cov-
ering all the main rest-UV lines. Aside from strong Lyα, no other
lines are detected, as expected at such low metallicity. The non-

We have then analyzed additional systems of the Cos-

all spectroscopically confirmed betweenz=6.05 and 6.23.
They are all covered by PEARLS photometry and NIRSpec
spectroscopy. Although they are uniformly metal-poor (Z≲
0.05 Z⊙), they span a broad range of stellar masses (4×10 6 to
2×10 8 M⊙), star-formation rates (0.06–10.2 M⊙ yr−1), dust con-
tents (A V ∼0–0.75), and ages (from<1 to∼11 Myr). This
diversity suggests that their chemical enrichment histories are
significantly affected by large-scale environmental gas accretion
rather than by internal evolutionary processes.
Finally, given the large number of systems in a narrow red-
shift range, we investigated whether the Cosmic Archipelago can
be interpreted as an overabundant group of young star-forming
galaxies. We measured an overdensity factor ofδ gal =12.3 +6.6
−4.6,
within a 70 kpc (physical) aperture and∆z=0.12. This value,
consistent with those found for similar galaxy overabundances
but smaller than those found for larger overdensities, is still am-
biguous in terms of future virialization. However, because of
spectroscopic incompleteness and the exclusion of faint systems
or sub-components, this estimate likely represents a lower limit
of the true value.
While MUSE remains very efficient for blindly discovering
faint Lyαemitters over wide areas, this work demonstrates the
power of combining gravitational lensing with deep multi-band
JWST photometry and high-resolution spectroscopy to physi-
cally characterize the faintest building blocks of galaxies in the
reionization era. The complementary coverage of JWST and X-
Shooter provided robust constraints on the physical conditions of
these systems, though our results also show that long X-Shooter
exposure times are needed for such faint galaxies atz>6 (our
27 X-Shooter hours, with a magnification factor of 3.73 would
correspond to∼376 hours on a non-lensed source). The synergy
of different facilities to characterize different physical properties
will be key in the next future with new instruments. For instance,
ELT/MORFEO–MICADO will allow us to resolve sub-parsec
scales in systems similar to the Cosmic Archipelago, and will al-
low us to further resolve possible substructures and detect fainter
compact objects, but will require JWST follow-up to probe ex-
tended ionized gas and the diffuse host galaxy structure.

Appendix A: Comparison between photometries extracted with different methods
We described the procedure used to extract the photometric properties of CA4 in detail in Sect. 3.2. In summary, we performed
aperture photometry within different circular apertures, with radii from 0.12′′to 0.3′′. They all produced consistent results, and we
thus assumed a circular aperture ofr=0.2 ′′, chosen as a good compromise between the observational PSF (this aperture includes
≳80% of the total flux, if a PSF profile is assumed) and the inclusion of additional noise with larger apertures. We then applied
this aperture both to the original images (with a filter-dependent aperture correction ranging from−0.181 in the F090W filter to
−0.322 in the F480M filter) and to images that have been PSF-matched to the F444W image. In both cases we additionally corrected
for the galactic reddening. Also in this case, we found consistent results, and we thus decided to proceed with the PSF-matched
images. With this setup, we used three different methods to estimate the CA4 magnitudes: i) we performed aperture photometry and
subtracted to the aperture flux the sky background estimated as a sigma-clipped median in an annular region, included within circles
with radii of 0.35′′ and 0.6′′; ii) as in the previous method, but with the sky background extracted from empty regions 1−2 ′′ from
CA4; iii) we extracted the (total) isophotal magnitude (MAG_ISO). Despite CA4 being an isolated and regular object, these methods
gave rise to consistent but yet different magnitudes. They are shown in Fig. A.1. We additionally estimated the total magnitude of
CA4 by performing surface brightness fits withGALFITin each individual filter. We found consistent results and a consistent trend,
with theGALFITmagnitudes being systematically∼0.11 mag brighter.

Appendix C: The Lyαemission of the systems of the Cosmic Archipelago
In Sections 3 and 4, we discussed the properties of the Lyαemission from the systems of the Cosmic Archipelago, and reported
them in the bottom of Tables 2 and 3. We extracted the spectra from the MUSE datacube considering circular regions with radii of
0.5-0.8′′, intended for including the full spatial emission. For CA6, given its elongated shape, we adopted an elliptical region with
semi-axes of approximately 0.8 ′′and 0.3 ′′. CA7 and CA9 are not shown, as they are not included in the field of view of MUSE.
The resulting Lyαlines are showed in Fig. C.1, that we modeled with a skewed Gaussian profile and a uniform background. We
measured the velocity shift with respect to the systemic redshift (measured from rest-optical lines, when detected), the Lyαflux,
rest-frame equivalent width EW0, the FWHM, and the Lyαasymmetry. The continuum below the Lyαused to measure EW 0 was
estimated extrapolatingM UV at the relative wavelength according to theβslope and correcting for the extinction (all quantities are
reported in Tables 1 and 3). The FWHM was corrected for instrumental broadening. The red-blue asymmetry of Lyαis quantified
through theA RB parameter, defined asA RB =(F red −F blue)/FLyα, whereF red andF blue are the Lyαfluxes measured red-ward and
blue-ward from the line peak, respectively (red and blue regions in Fig. C.1).
