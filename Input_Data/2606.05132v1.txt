ABSTRACT
Protoplanetary disks are rotating structures of gas and dust surrounding young stars, serving as
the birth places of planets. Understanding the chemical evolution of organic materials in these disks
is key for tracing the origins of organics in planetary systems. Formaldehyde (H 2CO) is the most
commonly detected organic molecule in protoplanetary disks. In this study, we investigate the emission
of H2CO and its link to disk properties, using a sample of 20 Class II disks in the Lupus and Upper
Sco star-forming regions spanning over 1-6 Myr. We analyze the H 2CO lines at 218.222 and 290.623
GHz observed as part of the AGE-PRO ALMA Large Program (Zhang et al. 2025). Within this
sample we achieve a detection rate of H 2CO of 45% (9/20), and set robust upper limits for the
non-detections. We measure the excitation temperature and column density of the H 2CO gas in
the sources with H 2CO detections. We combine our sample with 13 additional disks with archival
H2CO detections and search for correlations between H 2CO properties and disk parameters. Notably,
we find strong correlations between H 2CO line luminosity and dust radius, gas radius, dust mass, gas
mass, stellar mass, and stellar luminosity. This suggests that H 2CO emission is brighter for extended
massive dust disks where H 2CO can form via CO ice hydrogenation on grain surfaces. We find that
the H2CO excitation temperature is also correlated with stellar mass and stellar luminosity, so more
massive and luminous stars could increase H 2CO excitation.

1.INTRODUCTION
The composition and potential habitability of exo-
planets are strongly dependent on the processes that
lead to their formation. Planets form in protoplane-
tary disks, which are disk-like structures of gas and dust

that rotate around young stars (see review by Williams
& Cieza 2011; Manara et al. 2023). As disks evolve,
their chemical compositions may dramatically change
over time due to the evolving radiation field, as well
as the density and temperature structures (e.g., Zhang
et al. 2020; ¨Oberg & Bergin 2021). In particular, com-
plex organic molecules are essential for life, and therefore
understanding the origins of organic chemistry in pro-
toplanetary disks is key for our understanding of how

habitable planets form (Booth et al. 2021; Krijt et al.
2022; Zhang 2024; Ligterink et al. 2024).
Formaldehyde (H 2CO) is a small organic molecule
that is considered a stepping stone to more complex
organics. H 2CO can form through two processes: (1)
neutral-neutral gas-phase chemistry (e.g., Fockenberg &
Preses 2002; Atkinson et al. 2006) and (2) grain-surface
chemistry via CO ice hydrogenation (e.g., Hiraoka et al.
1994, 2002; Watanabe & Kouchi 2002; Hidaka et al.
2004; Watanabe et al. 2004; Fuchs et al. 2009). Neutral-
neutral gas-phase chemistry occurs more frequently in
the inner disk because these reactions occur faster where
the temperature and density are higher. In contrast,
the CO ice hydrogenation occurs past the CO snowline,
where CO freezes out onto grain surfaces. H2CO formed
through CO ice hydrogenation is expected to be the first
step towards forming oxygen-bearing, complex organic
molecules (COMs). COMs are carbon-bearing molecules
with six or more atoms (Herbst & van Dishoeck 2009).
COMs are difficult to detect in protoplanetary disks be-
cause they typically have low abundances in the gas-
phase and many transitions, so the emission from each
line is weaker (Walsh et al. 2014). H 2CO, however, is
the most commonly detected organic molecule in pro-
toplanetary disks (e.g., Pegues et al. 2020). Therefore,
understanding the abundances and spatial distribution
of H 2CO in disks is the foundation for understanding
the more complex organic chemistry that is difficult to
observe directly.
The distribution of H 2CO in protoplanetary disks re-
veals how the two chemical formation pathways operate
in different disk regions. Observations of the TW Hya
disk detected two H 2CO emission lines that provided
key constraints on its spatial distribution and origins
( ¨Oberg et al. 2017). The emission was found to be cen-
trally peaked, indicating some gas-phase formation of
H2CO in the warm inner disk, and most of emission is
from the outer disk originated at intermediate heights
(0.2< z/r <0.4), consistent with production via CO
ice hydrogenation on grain surfaces. Theoretical models
similarly reproduce this pattern, with gas-phase chem-
istry dominating in the inner regions and grain-surface
formation in the colder outer disk where CO remains
frozen long enough for hydrogenation to occur (Willacy
& Woods 2009; Walsh et al. 2014). However, the rela-
tive importance of these two pathways remains debated.
Hern´ andez-Vera et al. (2024) modeled spatially resolved
H2CO emission in HD 163296 and found that gas-phase
reactions contribute significantly even in the outer disk,
and Terwisscha van Scheltinga et al. (2021) argued that
gas-phase formation could be the dominant pathway in
TW Hya based on the emitting height and ortho-to-para
ratio of H 2CO.
H2CO formation in disks could be accelerated by the
presence of substructures. Substructures in disks can
appear in many forms, such as axisymmetric rings and
gaps or asymmetric spirals and crescents, with rings
being the most common form of substructure (Long
et al. 2018; Bae et al. 2023). These substructures can
cause dust trapping by creating pressure bumps where
dust accumulates as it radially drifts inward (Ligterink
et al. 2024). The dust grains can be vertically trans-
ported to warmer surface layers in the disk, resulting
in a good environment for the formation of organic
molecules. As a result, disks with substructures can
have efficient H 2CO formation via CO ice hydrogena-
tion on dust grains due to dust trapping. In the absence
of dust traps, molecules are likely to stay trapped in ices,
making them undetectable (Ligterink et al. 2024). Sub-
structures are therefore a probable contributor to high
levels of H2CO emission in disks.
H2CO has been detected in more than twenty proto-
planetary disks, within both T Tauri and Herbig disks.
(e.g., Loomis et al. 2015; Carney et al. 2017; ¨Oberg et al.
2017; Pegues et al. 2020; ¨Oberg et al. 2021; Booth et al.
2023). H 2CO has also been detected in protoplanetary
disks around M-stars by Pegues et al. (2021). This study
analyzed five disks around M4-M5 stars, and detected
H2CO toward one line in two disks. Both of these disks
showed central depressions in the H 2CO emission and
peaks in H 2CO emission around or beyond the edge of
the pebble disk (Pegues et al. 2021).
Rotational diagram analyses have been done for vari-
ous sources with multiple H 2CO line detections, includ-
ing IRS 48, HD 142527, PDS 70, and HD 100546 (van
der Marel et al. 2021; Temmink et al. 2023; Rampinelli
et al. 2024; Evans et al. 2025). The results revealed a
wide range of excitation conditions:T ex ranges from
∼20 K in HD 142527 to over 100 K in IRS 48, and
column densities from 2.0×10 13 cm−2 in PDS 70 to
2.1×10 14 cm−2 in HD 142527. The inferred dominant
formation pathway also varies: grain-surface chemistry
is favored in IRS 48, where a dust trap can loft ice-
coated grains to warmer layers (van der Marel et al.
2021), while gas-phase chemistry is favored in PDS 70
and HD 142527 - the latter despite hosting a dust trap,
because its CO snowline lies beyond the trap (Temmink
et al. 2023; Rampinelli et al. 2024).
So far, the largest population study of H2CO in proto-
planetary disks was by Pegues et al. (2020), which used
a sample of 15 disks with stellar mass between 0.5-2.0
M⊙ and age between 1-15 Myr. The 15 disks are T
Tauri and Herbig Ae disks, with six disks in the Taurus-

Auriga region, five disks in the Upper Sco region, one
disk in the Lupus region, and one disk in the Ophiuchus
region. Five of these disks (IM Lup, GM Aur, AS 209,
HD 163296, and MWC 480) are analyzed in more detail
in ( ¨Oberg et al. 2021). With this sample, Pegues et al.
(2020) detected H2CO in 13/15 disks and tentatively in
one additional disk. These detections showed diverse
emission morphologies, including centrally peaked, cen-
trally depressed, and ring-like structures. In the four
disks with detections toward multiple H 2CO lines, they
found excitation temperatures between 11-37 K and col-
umn densities between 1.9-21×10 12 cm−2. However,
this sample of 15 disks was inhomogeneous, with a wide
range of stellar masses (∼0.5−2.0M ⊙) and an uneven
age distribution (e.g., most of the old sources are the
sources with the highest stellar masses). As a result,
no conclusions were drawn about the dependence be-
tween H2CO line flux and disk and stellar properties.
In this paper, we use a more homogeneous sample with
disks from the AGE-PRO sample (Zhang et al. 2025)
that have a narrow range of stellar masses but a wide
span of ages. The AGE-PRO sample also allows us to
test dependencies with gas mass for the first time. We
aim to evaluate how H2CO emission is connected to disk
properties.
This paper presents a detailed study of the
H2CO emission in 20 Class II disks around 0.3-0.8 M ⊙
stars and tests how H 2CO emission changes with disk
parameters such as gas mass, gas radius, dust mass, dust
radius, stellar luminosity, and stellar mass.§2 describes
our disk sample, the observations, and measurements
of line fluxes, excitation temperature, and total column
density.§3 presents our results on detection rate, line
fluxes, and correlations with disk properties. In§4, we
discuss potential explanations for the correlations we
find with disk properties, including the implications on
H2CO evolution and chemistry.§5 summarizes our find-
ings and discusses future research directions for organics
in protoplanetary disks.
2.METHOD
2.1.Disk Sample and Data calibration
Our sample composites of 20 Class II disks which were
part of the the ALMA Survey of Gas Evolution of PRO-
toplanetary Disks (AGE-PRO), a Cycle 8 Large Pro-
gram of the Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter
Array (ALMA) (Zhang et al. 2025). The AGE-PRO
program was designed to trace the evolution of gas disk
masses and sizes, which selected 30 disks from three
nearby star-forming regions of different ages: Ophi-
uchus (embedded disks, 0.5-1 Myr), Lupus (middle age,
1-3 Myr), and Upper Sco (the end of gas disk lifetime,
2-6 Myr) (Zhang et al. 2025). This work focuses on the
20 Class II disks from the Lupus and Upper Sco regions.
See Table 1 for the stellar properties of the 20 sources.
The AGE-PRO sample was selected with four main
criteria: (1) sources with known stellar spectral type be-
tween M3-K6, roughly corresponding to a stellar mass
(M⋆) of 0.3-0.8 M ⊙, see Table 1. (2) sources without
known companions or that are in wide-separation bina-
ries (>600 AU), as close binaries may evolve differently
due to tidal interactions. (3) For Lupus and Upper Sco
regions, Class II sources were selected to exclude debris
disks. (4) sources were selected from previous detections
of mm continuum and CO line emission. Ten disks were
selected from each region that covers the spread of con-
tinuum luminosities in the region (Ruiz-Rodriguez et al.
2025; Deng et al. 2025; Agurto-Gangas et al. 2025).
The analysis presented here is based on the AGE-PRO
fiducial images, for two H 2CO lines in Bands 6 and 7.
The angular resolution is∼0. ′′35 for Band 6 and∼0. ′′70
for Band 7 (Deng et al. 2025), and the velocity resolu-
tion is 0.2 and 1.552 km/s for H2CO in Band 6 for Lupus
and Upper Sco sources, respectively and 1.164 km/s for
H2CO in Band 7 (Deng et al. 2025; Agurto-Gangas et al.
2025). Further information including the observational
logs, line frequencies, and full details of the data calibra-
tion and imaging are described in Zhang et al. (2025);
Deng et al. (2025); Agurto-Gangas et al. (2025). Two
H2CO lines are covered by our spectral setups: p-3 03-
202 at 218.222 GHz and p-4 04-303 at 290.623 GHz (in
this paper, we abbreviate these lines as p-3-2 and p-4-3,
respectively). The line information is shown in Table 2.
2.2.Analysis
Here we describe the methods used to measure the
H2CO line fluxes and create the radial profiles. We
first generate moment-zero maps for the H 2CO lines
of 20 disks by applying Keplerian masks to channel
maps. For the Lupus sources, we use the Keplerian
mask parameters for individual disks listed in Deng et al.
(2025). For the Upper Sco sources, we generate Keple-
rian masks based on the inclination and position angles
measured from 1.3 mm continuum emission by Vioque
et al. (2025). The best-fitting masks are decided by eye
evaluation of masks in individual channel maps. For
sources without 3σH 2CO line detections, we generate
masks based on 12COJ=2-1 line emission. The Keple-
rian mask parameters for the Lupus and Upper Sco disks
are listed in Appendix Table A1. Our line fluxes for the
Upper Sco disks are consistent with those obtained us-
ing the Keplerian mask parameters from Agurto-Gangas
et al. (2025), but we use a slightly larger Keplerian mask
to be more conservative for the fainter Upper Sco disks.

3.RESULTS
3.1.H 2CO detections
Both H2CO lines are detected at the 3σlevel in inte-
grated fluxes in five disks (Lupus 2 and 10, and Upper
Sco 1, 3, and 8). Only H2CO line p-3-2 is detected in Up-
per Sco 9 and Upper Sco 10, but no H 2CO p-4-3 obser-
vations were taken for these two disks. Only H 2CO line
p-4-3 is detected in Lupus 3 and Upper Sco 7. In to-
tal, there are five disks with two H 2CO detections and
four disks with one H 2CO detection. This means that
we recover a detection rate for the p-3-2 line of 20% for
Lupus and 50% for Upper Sco, and a detection rate for
the p-4-3 line of 30% in Lupus and and 40% in Upper
Sco. A significant detection requires both a 3σline flux
measurement and visible emission in the channel maps.
Very faint H 2CO emission is seen toward Upper Sco 2
and Upper Sco 4 for both lines, toward Lupus 3 and
Upper Sco 7 for H 2CO line p-3-2, and toward Lupus 7
for the p-4-3 line. However, the signal-to-noise ratios are
not sufficient for 3σdetections. The total line fluxes and
upper limits for non-detections are displayed in Table 3.
The line fluxes vary by more than two orders of mag-
nitude: ranging from 13.6 mJy km s −1 in Lupus 7 to
2269.4 mJy km s−1 in Lupus 10 for line p-4-3. Lupus 10
has the largest line fluxes toward both lines. However,
Lupus 10 is a very large disk that is not representative of
a typical disk (Trapman et al. 2025a). With the excep-
tion of Upper Sco 1, the line flux is larger for p-4-3 line
than for the p-3-2 line for all disks with two detections.
Looking at the radial profiles in Figure 2, H2CO emis-
sion generally peaks towards the center of the disk and
decreases radially outward. For the p-4-3 line, the beam
size is large (∼0. ′′8) and therefore the radial profiles
are not well resolved. The p-3-2 line observations have
smaller beam sizes (∼0. ′′3) and thus we focus on the
radial features seen in the p-3-2 lines. Notably, Lupus
2, Upper Sco 1, and Upper Sco 8 show a central depres-
sion. This could be indicative of an inner cavity in the
disk, although while Upper Sco 1 shows an inner cav-
ity in the CO emission, Lupus 2 and Upper Sco 8 do
not (Agurto-Gangas et al. 2025; Deng et al. 2025; Sierra
et al. 2024). For the few disks with central depressions,
the p-3-2 H 2CO emission peaks around 150 au for Lu-
pus 2, 80 au for Upper Sco 1, and 60 au for Upper Sco
1. It is possible that for some of these disks, continuum
subtraction is creating central depressions in H 2CO by
hiding H 2CO emission in high velocity channels, thus
producing a cavity in the H 2CO emission that is not
seen in the CO emission. This has been seen before in
RW Aur A (Kurtovic et al. 2024). Other possibilities
include the presence of optically thick dust that blocks
central H2CO emission, which is proposed to cause the
central depression of H2CO in HD 163296 (Carney et al.
2017; Hern´ andez-Vera et al. 2024), or that lower energy
H2CO lines are more sensitive to cold gas (Guzm´ an et al.
2021). Notably, Upper Sco 1 and Upper Sco 8 show cen-
tral depressions in the dust radial profiles in Figure 2.
The size of the H 2CO in R 90 is consistent between
Lupus and Upper Sco, ranging from 120 au to 230 au,
with the exception of Lupus 10, which is the largest
disk in the whole AGE-PRO sample with an H 2CO R90
of 860 au. All R 90 values are listed in Appendix Table
A2.

correlations with disk properties. However, the H2CO p-
4-3 line does have more detections than the H2CO p-3-2
line both in the AGE-PRO data and in the literature.
It is possible that the tighter correlations with the p-4-3
line could just be a result of the larger sample size.

4.DISCUSSION
4.1.Possible causes of the correlation between
H2CO luminosities and disk properties
With the new detections of H 2CO in 9 AGE-PRO
disks, we are able to examine correlations between
H2CO line luminosities with disk properties across a sig-
nificantly expanded parameter space. As shown in Fig-
ure 4, the AGE-PRO sources add a population of smaller
and lower-mass disks that were underrepresented in pre-
vious samples (e.g. Pegues et al. 2020). The broader
coverage in disk sizes and masses likely contributes to
our ability to identify statistically significant correla-
tions that were not evident in prior work. A summary
of the correlations we found is shown in Table 5. These
correlations suggest that H2CO emission is influenced by
a combination of disk structure, mass content, and stel-
lar irradiation. Below, we discuss several physical and
chemical processes that may contribute to these trends.
First, H2CO line luminosities show the strongest cor-
relations with the gas and dust disk sizes. A natural first
guess might be that larger disks provide a more extended
emitting area, thereby increasing the integrated line lu-
minosity. However, we find that the H 2CO emitting
radii are only marginally correlated with the gas and
dust disk sizes, which argues against the idea that emit-
ting area alone is the dominant driver. Alternatively,
the grain-surface pathway of H 2CO formation expects
H2CO to be abundant in the cold outer disk regions.
Larger dust and gas disks may provide greater reservoirs
of ices and cold CO gas which promote H2CO formation
and survival.
The strong correlation with dust disk mass further
supports the role of dust grains in H 2CO formation.
H2CO is believed to form efficiently through hydrogena-
tion of CO ice on grain surfaces. Disks with higher dust
masses may host more grain surface area and larger ice
reservoirs, enabling more efficient H2CO production. In-
terestingly, the H 2CO p-4-3 line shows a strong corre-
lation with gas disk mass, whereas the p-3-2 line shows
a more moderate correlation. This could simply be a
result of a difference in sample size. Additionally, the
upper limit for Upper Sco 9 falls well below the trend
line for the p-3-2 line, but we do not have data for the
Upper Sco 9 p-4-3 line, so that outlier does not affect the
correlation coefficient for gas mass and the p-4-3 line.
The observed correlation with stellar mass could arise
from several contributing factors. The dust disk mass
is known to scale with stellar mass (e.g., Pascucci et al.
2016; Andrews et al. 2018), and therefore the stellar
mass correlation could indirectly reflect the correlation
with dust disk mass. In addition, more massive stars

ner disk, our results support the presence of a strong
grain-surface component. In the next section, we will
explore how the substructures in our sample also lend
to this theory.
4.2.Substructures and H 2CO Detections
Substructures in protoplanetary disks can lead to dust
traps that allow for efficient H 2CO formation via CO
ice hydrogenation. Many of the disks in the AGE-PRO
sample show evidence of substructures, which implies
that dust trapping will effect the H 2CO emission in our
sample. Substructures have been detected in the az-
imuthally symmetric radial profiles for seven disks in
our sample: Lupus 2 and 10, and Upper Sco 1, 7, 8,
9, and 10. Upper Sco 1, 7, and 8 also show inner dust
cavities (Vioque et al. 2025).
The set of disks with substructures compares well to
the set of disks in which we detect H 2CO. In the Lupus
region, we detect two H 2CO lines toward only Lupus 2
and Lupus 10, which are the two Lupus disks with re-
solved substructures. We note that 7/10 Lupus disks
did not have enough resolution to detect substructures,
so there could be additional substructures that are un-
detected in the Lupus region (Vioque et al. 2025). In
the Upper Sco region, we detect two H2CO lines toward
Upper Sco 1, 3, and 8. Upper Sco 1 and 8 show sub-
structures, while Upper Sco 3 does not. The remaining
Upper Sco disks with substructures, Upper Sco 7, Upper
Sco 9, and Upper Sco 10, have H 2CO detected toward
a single line. Overall, all seven disks in the sample with
definite substructures show significant H2CO detections.
The two disks with the greatest H 2CO line luminosi-
ties (Lupus 10 and Upper Sco 1) show complex sub-
structures that likely contribute to H 2CO formation in
dust traps. Lupus 10 and Upper Sco 1 are also both
on the larger side of the sample, with Lupus 10 being
the largest disk in both gas and dust and Upper Sco 1
being the third largest disk (Deng et al. 2025; Agurto-
Gangas et al. 2025). Lupus 10 is an extremely struc-
tured disk, with several rings and gaps and potentially
a spiral arm (Vioque et al. 2025). In Figure 2, both the
H2CO and the dust radial profiles show small bumps
between 100 au and 300 au, particularly in the H2CO p-

3-2 emission. Upper Sco 1 has a large inner dust-cavity
and a ring structure, as well as a spiral arm structure
(Vioque et al. 2025; Sierra et al. 2024). Upper Sco 1’s
dust ring is around 80 au, and as shown in Figure 2, this
corresponds with the peak of the H 2CO emission. We
see that the dust substructures and H 2CO are spatially
linked in these disks, and we expect the substructures to
exist outside the mid-plane CO snowline (Zhang et al.
2017). Through both the presence of substructures and
size of the dust-disk, we see that H 2CO formation via
CO ice hydrogenation is likely to be more efficient in
disks such as Lupus 10 an Upper Sco 1, resulting in the
larger H2CO line luminosities in these disks. The par-
allel between substructures and H 2CO detections sup-
ports the existence of dust traps in the disks with sub-
structures that can accelerate H2CO formation on grain
surfaces.
4.3.Potential evolution of H 2CO?
Figure 8.H 2CO line fluxes, excitation temperatures, and
total column densities grouped by star-forming region. The
square represents the median value for the disks in each
region. We note that in the top panels, Lupus 10’s large
H2CO flux is an outlier that skews the location of the me-
dian point for the Lupus region.
In our sample, we detect H2CO in 3 disks in the young
Lupus star forming region and in 6 disks in the rela-
tively older Upper Sco star forming region. As shown in
Figure 8, the median value of the line luminosities for
both H2CO lines decreases from the Lupus region to the
Upper Sco region. However, Lupus 10 likely serves as
an outlier, and we do detect H 2CO in more relatively
older Upper Sco disks than younger Lupus disks. More
data points will be needed to draw a conclusion on how
H2CO emission varies with age. Similarly, the average
total column density increases and the average excita-
tion temperature decreases from the Lupus region to the
Upper Sco region. Again, too few data points are avail-
able to confirm these relationships. With only five disks
with significant H2CO detections in both lines, we can-
not come to a clear conclusion on how H 2CO evolves
with disk age.
5.CONCLUSION
In this paper, we present an analysis of H 2CO toward
20 Class II disks in the Lupus and Upper Scorpius star-
forming regions and measure line luminosities of two
H2CO lines. Both H 2CO lines are robustly detected in
five disks, and one H 2CO line is detected in four addi-
tional disks. The rotational diagram method is used to
calculate the excitation temperature and total column
density for the disks with two H2CO line detections, and
these values are corrected for optical depth. We use the
linmixPython package and a Spearman-ρcorrelation
test to search for correlations between the line luminos-
ity, excitation temperature, and total column density
and various disk parameters. The following summarizes
our conclusions:
•We find significant H 2CO detections in 9 of the
20 AGE-PRO disks in our sample. The p-3-2
H2CO line is detected at a rate of 20% in the Lu-
pus region and 50% in the Upper Sco region. The
p-4-3 H2CO line is detected at a rate of 30% in the
Lupus region and 40% in the Upper Sco region.
•We calculate excitation temperatures and total
column densities for five disks, finding values be-
tween 8.4-23.4 K and 0.9-44.5×1012 cm−2, respec-
tively.
•We find the R 90 of H 2CO line emission between
121-830 au for the Lupus region and between 134-
229 au for the Upper Sco sample.
•We find that the H 2CO line luminosity is strongly
correlated with gas disk radius, dust disk radius,
dust disk mass, stellar luminosity, stellar mass,
and gas mass for both the H 2CO p-3-2 and the
H2CO p-4-3 transitions.
•The H 2CO line luminosity has the strongest corre-
lation with the gas and dust disk radii, suggesting
that larger gas and dust disks have larger reser-
voirs of ices and CO gas where H 2CO can form.
The strong correlation with dust disk mass sug-
gests that the presence of more dust in the outer
regions of the disk could allow for more efficient
surface-grain chemistry as a formation pathway for
H2CO.

16Chevalier et al.
•The H 2CO line luminosities are strongly correlated
with the line fluxes of J=2-1 CO, 13CO, and C18O
lines, but not with CO abundance ([n CO/nH]).
This suggests that H2CO emission depends on the
total amount of CO gas but is not overly sensitive
to the CO abundance.
•The excitation temperature of H 2CO is found to
be strongly correlated with the stellar mass and
luminosity. More massive and luminous stars po-
tentially increase H2CO excitation.
•The disks with substructures are more likely to
have significant H2CO detections, supporting that
dust traps in these substructures can lead to more
H2CO formation via CO ice hydrogenation.
•More data is needed to determine the relationship
between H2CO emission and disk age. Improving
the precision of our age estimates for protoplane-
tary disks would allow more robust analysis of the
evolution of H2CO over time.
Overall, we find that larger protoplanetary disks with
more massive and luminous stars will likely have more
H2CO emission. The disks with the largest H 2CO line
luminosities also have clear substructures, suggesting
that dust traps may form in these disks and acceler-
ate H2CO formation on dust grains. Future work could
attempt to detect more complex organic molecules to
determine if these detections follow the same trends
that we find for H 2CO. Additionally, while there are
not enough disks with significant H 2CO detections
in our sample to determine the relationship between
H2CO emission and disk age, future research could add
to this sample by continuing to study H 2CO systemat-
ically in disks that span the lifetime of protoplanetary
disks.
