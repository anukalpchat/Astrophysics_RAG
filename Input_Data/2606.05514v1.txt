Ultra-high energy cosmic rays (UHECR) can produce relativistic cascades that emit radio-
frequency (RF) pulses in the 0.05-1 GHz bandwidth via two distinct effects: the geomagnetic effect,
and the Askaryan effect. The geomagnetic effect occurs when the magnetic field of the Earth causes
cascade charges to form a transverse current that radiates linearly polarized radiation aligned with
the Lorentz force direction. The Askaryan effect is caused by the net negative charge excess in
the cascade that radiates linearly polarized radiation along the Cherenkov cone. When UHECR
cascades enter solid, RF transparent matter at altitudes where the cascade develops, Askaryan ra-
diation can propagate through the solid matter to RF detectors. The Askaryan Radio Array (ARA)
at the South Pole has observed 13 UHECR candidates in precisely this fashion. We present an
analytical model that confirms the events are UHECRs. The model includes Askaryan radiation
and the ARA RF channel response. The coherently summed waveforms (CSWs) from the UHECR
candidates match our model with correlation coefficients between 0.69 and 0.86, and with minimal
fractional power differences. Finally, we demonstrate how to use the analytic formalism to obtain
the Askaryan ⃗E-field, and that it matches the results from ARA.
Keywords: Ultra-high energy neutrino; Askaryan radiation; Mathematical physics
I. INTRODUCTION
UHECR cascades produce RF pulses via the geomag-
netic and Askaryan effects, and these pulses have been
used to study UHECR properties [1, 2]. Detectors with
RF channels sensitive to RF pulses from UHECRs add
complementary data for event reconstruction to tradi-
tional ground-based muon and fluorescence detectors.
UHECR candidate events have now been presented by
the Antarctic Ross Ice Shelf Antenna Neutrino Array
(ARIANNA), ARA, and Radio Neutrino Observatory
Greenland (RNO-G) collaborations [3–5]. Due to the
unique location of ARA at the South Pole, the contri-
bution from the Askaryan effect dominates the observed
RF pulses. The South Pole is 2800 meters above sea
level, and the thin atmosphere allows UHECR cascades
to develop near the altitude of the ARA RF detection
channels. As charged particles from the UHECR cascade
enter the ice, the negative excess charge radiates collec-
tively in the 0.1-1 GHz bandwidth.
Within the genre of calculations that predict the prop-
erties of RF pulses from UHECRs and ultra-high en-
ergy neutrinos (UHE-ν), there are three classes of mod-
els. First, Monte Carlo programs may be used to track
each particle and corresponding RF emission within the
cascade. These are known as full Monte Carlo, orfull-
MC calculations[6–8]. Second, there are formalisms in
which the analytic vector potential corresponding to the
radiating charge is convolved with the simulated cascade
profile. These are calledsemi-analytic calculations, and
have been used to predict the RF pulses from UHE-ν

nally, there arefully analyticcalculations that derive the
UHECR or UHE-νsignal from classical electrodynam-

(RB2001) [12], Hanson and Connolly (HCRB2017) [13],
and Hanson and Hartig (HHH2022) [14].
One key advantage of fully analytic Askaryan calcula-
tions is that they can be fit to raw data from Askaryan-
class detectors. A prediction for the voltage trace from
an RF detection channel is produced by convolving the
analytic Askaryan model with an accurate model of the
RF channel response. Analytic voltage traces can be
matched to the observed voltage traces by maximizing
the correlation coefficient from cross-correlation while
tuning parameters like the Askaryan pulse width. Re-
cently, Hanson and Hartig presented evidence that the
HH2022 model, when convolved with an RF channel
model, produces analytic voltage traces that match volt-
age traces from UHE-νsignals in NuRadioMC. This tech-
nique can be used to reject several years of thermal
noise backgrounds in detectors like ARA and RNO-G
(HH2026) [11, 15]. Further, once the match between
observed and analytic voltage trace is optimized, the
Askaryan electromagnetic pulse can be extracted based
on the fit parameters. In this work, we show that the
HH2026 procedure matches the voltage traces from 13
UHECR candidate events observed by ARA [4].
The remaining sections of this work are organized as
follows: in Sec. II, we define units, functions, operations
on functions, and notational conventions. In Sec. III,
we review the design of the Askaryan Radio Array de-
tector, including the properties of the RF channels. In
Sec. IV, we present the match between our theoretical
voltage traces and the ARA data. Finally, in Sec. V, we
summarize our results and indicate future directions of
the research.

III. THE ASKARYAN RADIO ARRAY
The Askaryan Radio Array consists of five stations de-
ployed near the South Pole designed to detect UHE-νand
UHECR events via the Askaryan effect in Antarctic ice
[16–19]. The A5 station utilizes a dedicated RF trigger
on the CSW output of a phased array of seven vertically
polarized dipoles. The RF phased array trigger boosts
the SNR of UHE-νand UHECR events, and allows for
increased sensitivity to rare fluxes of these particles [20].
The ARA UHECR candidates used in this analysis were
recorded on the A5 station via the RF phased array trig-
ger. A schematic of the A5 detector is shown in Fig. 1,
and the A5 detector is summarized in [16].
The UHECR candidate events were detected with the
RF phased array trigger in A5. The proposed event ge-
ometry is shown in Fig. 2. The UHECR candidates
would normally create both geomagnetic and Askaryan
radiation, and geomagnetic radiation usually dominates
the total electromagnetic pulse when the detector lies
far beneath the cascade [3]. The same claim is made
in recent UHECR candidate detections via RNO-G [5].
Though RNO-G and ARA are both situated at high al-
titudes, the latitude of RNO-G is about 72 degrees N,
while ARA is at 90 degrees S. At 90 degrees S, geomag-
netic radiation is expected to be horizontally polarized
due to the vertical orientation of the local magnetic field.

IV. RECONSTRUCTION ANALYSIS
Our cross-correlation analysis between Eq. 7 and the
CSWs from the UHECR candidates from A5 begins by
making two assumptions. The first is that thef 0 and
γparameters are identical across the A5 phased array
trigger string. While each channel may not haveidentical
parameters, that is the intent of the design shown in Fig.
1. The second assumption we make is that calibration
pulse events resemble Askaryan pulses. Though that is
the purpose of the calibration pulser system design in
ARA [16, 18], the original pulser voltage is transmitted
through an ARA dipole, propagates through ice to the
A5 trigger string, and is converted to the observed trace.
None of these effects, however, should alterf 0 orγ, as
they are properties of the RF antennas themselves.
Given these assumptions, we measuredf 0 andγfor the
A5 trigger string using a data set of events triggered by
the A5 calibration pulser. Given that the lower edge of
the RF channels is of order≈0.1 GHz, and that calibra-
tion pulser events are of order≲100 ns wide, we hypoth-
esized thatf 0 ≈0.1 GHz andγ≈0.01 GHz. Given these
initial guesses, we calculated the CSW for each event, and
maximized the correlation coefficient for each CSW,ρ, by
tuningσ t. We scanned the [f 0, γ] parameter space until
the global averageρ ave was maximized. We found that
ρave = 0.79±0.02 was maximized whenf 0 = 0.19±0.01
GHz, andγ= 0.02±0.01 GHz. Thoughσ t was allowed
to vary for each CSW, the maximumρwas reached when
σt = 0.6±0.1 for each CSW individually. This is not a
coincidence, but encouraging, for the calibration pulser
properties tend to remain constant over time. These re-
sults are summarized in Tab. I.

example, the authors of HH2026 [15] showed that UHE-

would produce a distribution of correlation coefficients
peaked atρ≈0.9 but spread between 0.4 and 1.0. If
adding or removing the reflected pulse from the fit re-
ducedρbelow 0.4, for example, this would suggest a
shift in interpretation.

cade observation. Consider Eq. 5 of [15]:

cascade energy was between 10 and 100 PeV gives the
correct range for ∆θ.
There are two logical steps for this work in the future.
First, a separate fit for ∆θwould allow the measrure-
ment of theaparameter, which is tied to the UHECR
cascade energy. This parameter ∆θmay be constrained
by using the spectral cutoff of the voltage traces, which
are inversely correlated to ∆θ[13]. Second, it is interest-
ing to note that our model fors(t) in Eq. 1 corresponds
to theoff-cone(θ̸=θ C) result from HH2022 [14]. The
authors of [14] also include anon-coneversion ofs(t)
that is used when ∆θ= 0, and can be used as ∆θ→0.
The on-cone model could provide a better fit for events
with ∆θ≲1 degree [14]. Finally, it is our hope that one
day our model will be used to fit UHE-νcandidates, in
addition to UHECR candidates. We would like to thank
