ABSTRACT
The complex radio light curves of tidal disruption events (TDEs) challenge our understanding of
the properties of both the outflows and the circumnuclear medium (CNM) surrounding supermassive
black holes. In this work, we explore outflow-CNM interactions across a broad parameter space using
three-dimensional hydrodynamic simulations, adopting a broken power-law CNM density profile with a
transition near the Bondi radius. The outflow-CNM interaction inside Bondi radius produces an early
radio flare (≲2 yr) once the emitting region becomes optically thin. A second radio rebrightening can
appear a few years later if the outflow decelerates beyond Bondi radius. We also find that either a
very dense inner CNM, which causes rapid deceleration, or a rarefied outer CNM suppresses the late
rebrightening that will produces a single early-peaked flare. In contrast, a rarefied CNM inside the
Bondi radius suppresses the early flare and yields a single late-peaked event. For the case of very dense
CNM at large radii, the interaction will trigger a sharp late-time rise as observed in some TDEs. We
further explore the interaction of a relativistic jet with a broken power-law CNM, which can reproduce
the characteristic light curves as observed in jetted TDEs without invoking complex jet structure.
Keywords:Hydrodynamical simulations(767), Tidal disruption (1696), Radio transient sources(2008)
1.INTRODUCTION
A tidal disruption event (TDE) occurs when a star
enters the tidal radius of a supermassive black hole
(SMBH) in a galactic nucleus (e.g., Rees 1988; Evans
& Kochanek 1989; Gezari 2021), producing a luminous,
multi-wavelength flare (e.g., Alexander et al. 2020; van
Velzen et al. 2020; Lu & Bonnerot 2020). The peak
fallback rate of the disrupted stellar debris typically ex-
ceeds the Eddington limit (e.g., Strubbe & Quataert
2009, 2011; Lodato & Rossi 2011), leading to the for-
mation of an accretion disk that radiates primarily in
soft X-rays (e.g., Ulmer 1999; Roth et al. 2016; Stone
& Metzger 2016; Auchettl et al. 2017; Dai et al. 2018;
Pasham & van Velzen 2018; Curd & Narayan 2019; Sax-
ton et al. 2020). However, most TDEs are discovered
through optical surveys, and their optical/UV emission
mechanisms remain uncertain. Two leading models have
been proposed to explain this emission: (1) reprocessing
of X-ray photons by an optically thick envelope, and (2)

shock heating from collisions among debris streams (e.g.,
Guillochon & Ramirez-Ruiz 2013; Jiang et al. 2016; Pi-
ran et al. 2015; Roth et al. 2016; Lu & Bonnerot 2020;
van Velzen et al. 2020; Liu et al. 2021; Steinberg &
Stone 2024). Recent wide-field surveys further indicate
that TDEs preferentially occur in compact, green-valley
galaxies, which suggests that TDE rates are closely reg-
ulated by the nuclear environments of their host galaxies
(e.g., Hammerstein et al. 2021; Yao et al. 2023; Wang
et al. 2024).
The radio survey of TDEs remains incomplete, but
rapid follow-up observations have yielded over 50 ra-
dio detections (e.g., Alexander et al. 2020; Cendes
et al. 2024; Zhou et al. 2026). Several jet-dominated
events have been identified in recent years, includ-
ing Sw J1644+57 (e.g., Bloom et al. 2011; Zauderer
et al. 2011; Levan et al. 2011; Metzger et al. 2012),
Sw J2058+05 (e.g., Cenko et al. 2012), Sw J1112−82
(e.g., Brown et al. 2015), and AT 2022cmc (e.g., An-
dreoni et al. 2022). The origin of the radio emission
of other TDEs is still in hot debate, which typically
appears tens to hundreds of days after the optical/X-
ray flare (e.g., Alexander et al. 2016; Krolik et al. 2016;

Pasham & van Velzen 2018; Alexander et al. 2020; Lu
& Bonnerot 2020; Stein et al. 2021). The explanations
for the delayed radio emission include circumnuclear
medium (CNM) interaction with an on-axis or off-axis
jet (e.g., Cendes et al. 2021b; Horesh et al. 2021a; Mat-
sumoto & Piran 2023; Teboul & Metzger 2023; Zhou
et al. 2024; Sfaradi et al. 2024; Sato et al. 2024), non-
relativistic winds (e.g., Alexander et al. 2016; Lu & Bon-
nerot 2020; Mou et al. 2022; Bu et al. 2023; Matsumoto
& Piran 2024; Zhuang et al. 2025), unbound debris (e.g.,
Krolik et al. 2016; Lei et al. 2016; Yalinewich et al. 2019;
Spaulding & Chang 2022), or delayed jet/outflow forma-
tion during the late decaying phase in a low accretion
state (e.g., Horesh et al. 2021a; Sfaradi et al. 2022; Cen-
des et al. 2022; Matsumoto & Piran 2023).
The CNM in galactic centers is likely shaped by the ac-
cretion history of the central SMBH. In AGNs, gas and
dust are abundant, forming a toroidal obscuring struc-
ture in the equatorial plane, where the dense gas regions
that emit broad lines and more extended less dense gas
that emits narrow lines as described by the AGN unifica-
tion model (e.g., Antonucci 1993; Urry & Padovani 1995;
Netzer 2015). In contrast, low-luminosity AGNs and
quiescent galaxies often lack such cold components based
on absence of the broad emission lines and mid-infrared
signatures, where, however, hot gas can be directly re-
solved on parsec scales via high-resolution X-ray imaging
(e.g., Wang et al. 2013; Russell et al. 2015; Wong et al.
2014). Therefore, the CNM evolves significantly over
the evolutionary history of the host galaxy. However,
owing to limited angular resolution, detailed CNM den-
sity profiles remain poorly constrained for most galax-
ies. The CNM density profile normally differs inside and
outside the Bondi radius, where the gravitational poten-
tial is dominated by the SMBH and by the host bulge,
respectively (e.g., Bondi 1952; Quataert 2004; Cough-
lin & Begelman 2014; Generozov et al. 2017; Gillessen
et al. 2019). For example, the gas density often fol-
lows a power-law profile inside the Bondi radius, while
it flattens near the Bondi radius and eventually follows
a power-law decline beyond kpc scales (e.g., M87; Rus-
sell et al. 2015). TDEs provide an opportunity to probe
the CNM distribution by fitting radio light curves and
spectral energy distributions produced by outflow-CNM
interaction (e.g., Mou et al. 2022; Lei et al. 2024; Mat-
sumoto & Piran 2024; Zhuang et al. 2025).
With the growing sample of radio TDEs, the observed
radio light curves have revealed a remarkable diversity
that challenges simple analytical models. For instance,
some sources exhibit radio delays of several years after
the optical peak (e.g., PS16dtm; Cendes et al. 2024),
others show extremely steep flux rises exceedingt 3 (e.g.,
AT2018hyz; Cendes et al. 2022), and some events dis-
play double-peaked radio light curves (e.g., ASASSN-
15oi; Horesh et al. 2021a). Although recent analytical
work has attempted to interpret some of these complex
features (e.g., Teboul & Metzger 2023; Matsumoto &
Piran 2024; Sato et al. 2024; Cendes et al. 2025), a uni-
fied framework remains lacking. Moreover, the possi-
ble hydrodynamic instabilities and the evolving geome-
try of the outflow can further influence the actual radio
emission, motivating a multidimensional hydrodynamic
treatment beyond one-zone analytical frameworks. In
this work, we investigate the resulting radio emission
using three-dimensional hydrodynamic simulations in
which we vary the outflow properties and the CNM den-
sity profiles inside and outside the Bondi radius. Our
models can roughly reproduce many key features of ob-
served TDE radio light curves, including single early or
late peaks, double-peaked structures, and sharply rising
late-time flares. Section 2 outlines the numerical setup;
Section 3 presents the simulation results; Section 4 dis-
cusses the correspondence between simulated and ob-
served light curves; and Section 5 summarizes the main
conclusions.
2.NUMERICAL SETUP
We model the outflow as a uniform, axisymmetric
outflow launched from the inner boundary and simu-
late its interaction with the CNM. When a star is dis-
rupted and accreted by an SMBH, the resulting super-
Eddington accretion flow can launch a fast wind or a
relativistic jet (e.g., Dai et al. 2018; Curd 2021; Qiao
et al. 2025; Bu et al. 2026). As these outflows propagate
outward, they interact with the surrounding CNM and
drive shocks that accelerate nonthermal electrons and
produce synchrotron emission. We eject a TDE out-
flow with a total mass ofM ej from the inner boundary
over the interval 0< t < t ej. Within the polar cone
θ < θ ej/2, the material is ejected with a constant mass-
outflow rate ˙Mej at velocity ofv ej during the ejection
durationt ej = 0.1 yr (e.g., Li & Guo 2024; Hayasaki &
Yamazaki 2023). To avoid numerical instabilities in the
simulations, the ejecta temperature is set toTej = 108 K,
which roughly do not affect the evolution of shocks and
the radio light curves.
During the interaction between the outflow and the
CNM, variations in the density profile can significantly
modify the resulting radio light curve. To investigate
how the CNM structure influences the radio emission,
we adopt a simplified broken-power-law density profile
with a transition at the Bondi radius,

We employ the publicly available codeAthena++(e.g.,
Stone et al. 2020) to perform three-dimensional hy-
drodynamic simulations of the interaction between the
outflow and the CNM. The simulation domain spans
r∈[0.01R B,10R B],θ∈[0, π/2], andϕ∈[0,2π], us-
ing a logarithmically uniform grid in the radial direc-
tion withN r ×N θ ×N ϕ = 6144×64×256 cells. Out-
flow boundary conditions are imposed in ther-direction,
polar boundary conditions in theθ-direction, and peri-
odic boundary conditions in theϕ-direction. The sim-
ulation is evolved until the outermost shocked material
reaches the outer boundary at 10R B, which occurs at
∼5×10 3 days for our fiducial models. The governing
equations and the synchrotron-emission calculations are
summarized in Appendix A.
3.SIMULATION RESULTS
3.1.The dynamic evolution
We simulate the interaction between an outflow and
a broken-power-law CNM, adopting a fiducial density
at the Bondi radius ofn B = 200 cm −3 withk i = 2.5
andk o = 0 describing the power-law slopes inside and
outsideR B, respectively. To capture geometric effects,
we adopt an opening angleθ ej = 120 ◦ to represent a
wide wind withM ej = 0.02M ⊙ andv ej = 0.1c.
Figure 1 presents snapshots of the number density and
the scaled entropy during the wind-CNM interaction. In
the early phase (panels a and a’), the wind expands at
nearly constant velocity, forming a dense shell with a
small fraction of shocked material accumulated at the
leading edge. As the forward shock reaches the Bondi
radius (panels b and b’), it continues to sweep up am-
bient material. Once the swept-up mass becomes com-
parable to the ejecta mass, the flow transitions into the
Sedov-Taylor phase (e.g., Taylor 1950; Sedov 1959) and
begins to decelerate (panels c and c’). When the density
of the expanding ejecta drops below that of the CNM,
a Rayleigh-Taylor (RT) instability is triggered; this in-
stability disrupts the wind structure and enhances de-
celeration. The RT-driven disruption and deceleration
develop further in panels d and d’.
3.2.Basic Radio Light Curve
We simply calculate the synchrotron emission for an
observer located along the polar axis, adopting typi-
cal parametersϵ e = 0.1,ϵ B = 0.02, andp= 2.5 (see
Appendix A.2 for details). The radio light curves are
shown in Figure 2(a). The radio emission exhibits two
distinct flares, peaking at∼10 2 and∼10 3 days. The
first flare is strongly frequency-dependent (higher fre-
quencies peak earlier), whereas the second flare is nearly
frequency-independent. The early peak arises from the
transition of the emitting region from optically thick to
optically thin (see Figure 2(a)), which accounts for its
frequency dependence. After the first peak, the lumi-
nosity decays approximately asνL ν ∝t −1.8 until the
freely expanding ejecta reach the Bondi radius. The
light curve shows a break att break ≃700 days, coinci-
dent with the wind crossingR B. Beyond this time, the
radio luminosity rises asνL ν ∝t 1.7 because the flat-
ter outer CNM increases the mass-sweeping efficiency.
The second peak occurs att 2nd ≃1750 days, when the
swept-up mass becomes comparable to the ejecta mass.
Thereafter, the emission declines asνL ν ∝t −1.4. The
second peak is generally dimmer than the first one be-
cause less kinetic energy is dissipated at that stage. The
spectral peak frequencyν p, which roughly corresponds
to the emission-weighted average of the synchrotron self-
absorption frequenciesν a of individual shocked cells,
evolves asν p ∝t −1.4 before the outflow crosses the
Bondi radius and transitions to a slower decline of
νp ∝t −1.1 beyond this radius (bottom-left panel of Fig-
ure 2). Notably,ν p remains approximately constant for
several hundred days after crossingR B. Our simulation
results are broadly consistent with the analytical pre-
dictions of the spherical thin-shell model (Matsumoto
& Piran 2024). The simulations provide more detailed
results, where the non-negligible kinetic energy dissipa-
tion prior to the nominal deceleration radius, multidi-
mensional geometric effects (such as lateral spreading)
and RT instabilities modify the dynamical evolution and

3.3.Impact of the CNM Density Profile
In Section 3.2 we show that outflow-CNM interac-
tions naturally produce double-peaked radio light curves
when the deceleration radius greatly exceeds the Bondi
radius, corresponding to relatively little mass loaded
within the Bondi radius (i.e.,M ej/MB ≫1). In this
regime the light-curve shape is primarily controlled by
the radial CNM density profile (see also Matsumoto &
Piran 2024, for analytical work). To quantify this depen-
dence, we define the decay slope inside the Bondi radius
byνL ν ∝t si and the rise slope of the second flare beyond
the Bondi radius byνL ν ∝t so; both slopes are measured
between the 80% and 20% flux levels. To better mea-
sure the slope of light curve, we adoptM ej = 0.2M ⊙
for the simulations shown in Figure 3, althoughs i is
largely insensitive to the ejecta mass. A shallower CNM
profile insideR B produces a flatter decline of the first
flare because less shocked gas is generated. Fork i = 1.0,
the light curve withinR B can remain in a slowly rising
phase. The slope of the second flare correlates strongly
with the outer density slope: a rapidly declining CNM
(ko ≳1) suppresses the second peak, whereas a flat or
locally rising profile (k o ≲0) yields a brighter, more
steeply rising late-time flare. The right panels of Fig-
ure 3 illustrate the relation between the CNM slopes and
the measured light-curve slopes. While the simulations
agree well with analytic expectations for the first flare,
the measured second-flare slopes are systematically shal-
lower than the analytical prediction. This discrepancy
is primarily due to the broader spatial expansion of the
outflow and the non-negligible kinetic-energy dissipation
that occurs prior to reaching the nominal deceleration
radius in the simulations. Overall, reduced gas mass

3.4.Fast and Narrow Jet Scenario
In addition to the wide-angle, low-velocity wind mod-
els, we also explore the interaction between a relativis-
tic, narrow jet and the CNM. The fiducial jet param-
eters areM ej = 3×10 −3 M⊙,v ej = 0.9c(correspond-
ing to kinetic energyE k ∼4×10 51 erg), and an open-
ing angleθ ej = 10 ◦. We fix the CNM properties to
RB = 7.5×1016 cm,n B = 103 cm−3,k i = 2.5 andk o = 0.
The dynamical evolution is broadly similar to the wind
case (Figure A1), although the narrower opening angle
suppresses RT instabilities and limits lateral mixing.
The light curves of the jet-CNM interaction are shown
in Figure 5(a). Because the jet propagates much faster
than the wind, it reaches and crosses the Bondi radius
shortly after producing the first peak at higher frequen-
cies (87 GHz), yielding a secondary rise near∼50 days.
The jet’s lower ejecta mass also leads to earlier deceler-
ation than in the wind case, producing an initial decline
followed by a second peak at∼100−200 days. No-

4.DISCUSSION
To qualitatively compare our simulations with obser-
vational results, we summarize the main features of TDE
radio light curves in Figure 6 and Table 1. These fea-
tures are simply grouped into five categories: double-
peaked flares; early-peaked flares (<2 yr); late-peaked
flares (>2 yr); sharp-rise flares (e.g., steeper thanνL ν ∝
t5); and jetted TDEs. We note that this classification
may be incomplete because of limited radio coverage.
In the following subsections we describe each category,
compare them with our simulation results, and discuss
the corresponding physical interpretations.
4.1.Double-peaked Flare
Several TDEs show clear double-peaked radio light
curves, which include ASASSN-15oi, AT2020vwl and
AT2024tvd (e.g., Goodwin et al. 2025a; Hajela et al.
2025; Sfaradi et al. 2025). In our simulations, this shape
can be naturally reproduced when the CNM radial pro-
file transitions near the Bondi radius (see also Mat-
sumoto & Piran 2024, for analytical work): the first
peak arises from the outflow-CNM interaction inside
RB, while the second peak originates from the inter-
action beyondR B. Such distinct CNM distributions
inside and outside the Bondi radius are expected, be-
ing governed primarily by the SMBH potential and the
host bulge potential, respectively. Notably, the early
peaks of AT2024tvd and ASASSN-15oi exhibit both
steep rises and steep decays, implying large inner density
slopesk i in their host galaxies. While the steep slope of
AT2024tvd may be related to its special off-nuclear en-
vironment, this preference of double-peaked events for

4.2.Single-peaked Flare
A substantial fraction of radio TDEs show a single ra-
dio peak in the currently available datasets (see Figure 6;
e.g., Alexander et al. 2020; Cendes et al. 2024), although
sparse late-time coverage (particularly beyond∼10 3
days) leaves the possibility of undetected rebrighten-
ings. The observed single peak is commonly interpreted
as synchrotron emission from the interaction between
the TDE-driven outflow and a single power-law CNM
(e.g., Alexander et al. 2020, 2025; Hu et al. 2025; Mou
2025). Several radio TDEs exhibit peak times shorter
than two years, or even within∼10 2 days. Such early
peaks naturally arise from the outflow-CNM interaction
inside the Bondi radius in our simulations, and the po-
tential second peak may be weak or abscent if the CNM
gas is tenuous beyond the Bondi radius (e.g.,k o ≳1;
see the bottom-left panel of Figure 3). Furthermore, if
the deceleration radius is comparable to or smaller than
the Bondi radius (M ej/MB ≲1), either because of a
high inner CNM density or a low ejecta mass, the sec-
ond peak should be strongly suppressed, leaving only a
single early-peaked flare.
In recent years, several TDEs have exhibited ex-
tremely late-time radio flares, emerging two years or
more after the initial optical/X-ray discovery (e.g.,
Horesh et al. 2021a,b; Cendes et al. 2022; Goodwin et al.
2023b; Cendes et al. 2024). These late flares are primar-
ily produced by the interaction of the outflow with the
CNM beyond the Bondi radius. A deficit of gas inside
the Bondi radius, either because of a lower density or a
shallow inner profile (ki ≲1.5), suppresses the first peak
and naturally yields a single late-time peak in our sim-
ulations. Such a shallow inner environment, that con-
sistent with X-ray observations of nearby galactic nuclei
including M87, NGC 3115, and NGC 1600 (Wong et al.
2014; Russell et al. 2015; Runge & Walker 2021), also
tends to produce steeper late-time rises. The particu-
larly steep rises as observed in AT2018zr and ASASSN-
14ae may reflect local density bumps near the Bondi
radius withk o ≳0.0. An alternative explanation is a
delayed outflow launch during a low-activity state of the
SMBH, although the physical origin of such delays re-
mains unclear (e.g., Horesh et al. 2021a; Sfaradi et al.
2022; Cendes et al. 2022, 2025; Sato et al. 2025). No-
tably, very long delays can produce apparently steeper
light curves as a consequence of an offset in the as-
sumed time origin (e.g., Cendes et al. 2022, 2025; Mat-
sumoto 2025). An off-axis jet can also produce a de-
layed onset, but it typically predicts a much steeper
rise (e.g., Lei et al. 2016; Matsumoto & Piran 2023;
Sfaradi et al. 2024). We further discuss this scenario
in Section 4.3. Finally, our distinction between “early”
and “late” peaks, based on a timescale of two years,
is a crude classification that roughly separates the ob-
served and simulated double-peak timescales (Cendes
et al. 2024), and will require more observations to vali-
date.
4.3.Sharp-rise Flare
AT2018hyz and AT2018cqh were all detected in the
radio band more than a thousand days after their TDE
discoveries (Cendes et al. 2022; Sfaradi et al. 2024;
Zhang et al. 2024; Cendes et al. 2024). Unlike the single
late-time peaks discussed above, these sources exhibit
extremely steep rises, in some cases even steeper than
νLν ∝t 5. One explanation invokes interaction between
the outflow and a dense gas structure at large radii, such
as clumpy cold clouds or a dusty torus (e.g., Mou et al.
2022; Bu et al. 2023; Lei et al. 2024; Matsumoto & Pi-
ran 2024; Zhuang et al. 2025; Mou & Shu 2025; Yang
et al. 2025), where the sharp rise reflects a sudden in-
crease in ambient density. This behavior is reproduced
in our simulations (see Figure 4). The sharp-rise events
with extremely steep late flares typically lack early radio
emission, which may be caused by the preference for a
shallow inner profile (ki ≲1.5). An alternative explana-
tion is an off-axis relativistic jet initially misaligned with
our line of sight: relativistic beaming suppresses early
emission, producing a sharp radio rise as the jet decel-
erates and the beaming cone widens into view (e.g., Lei
et al. 2016; Matsumoto & Piran 2023; Sfaradi et al. 2024;
Sato et al. 2024; Lu et al. 2024; Matsumoto 2025). Note
that the required jet energy and Lorentz factors may be
extreme for some events (e.g., Sfaradi et al. 2024; Sato
et al. 2024), and the low observed incidence of jetted
TDEs disfavors this scenario as a universal explanation
for all sharp-rise flares (e.g., Teboul & Metzger 2023).
4.4.Jetted TDE
Jetted TDEs, although still limited in number, consti-
tute an important subclass of radio TDEs (e.g., Zaud-
erer et al. 2011; Berger et al. 2012; Kelley et al. 2014; De
Colle & Lu 2020; Andreoni et al. 2022). Their radio light

10
curves often exhibit pre-peak bumps or shoulder-like
shapes (see the bottom-left panel of Figure 6; e.g., Cen-
des et al. 2021b; Rhodes et al. 2025). At sufficiently high
frequencies, some jetted events (e.g., Swift J1644+57)
show distinct double peaks (e.g., Eftekhari et al. 2018;
Cendes et al. 2021b). Previous studies have interpreted
these features with two-component jet models composed
of a narrow, fast cocoon and a wider, slower sheath,
whose interaction with a stratified CNM can roughly
reproduce the observed jetted-TDE light curves (e.g.,
Wang et al. 2014; Mimica et al. 2015; Liu et al. 2015;
Teboul & Metzger 2023; Zhou et al. 2024; Sato et al.
2025). In our simulations, both the low-frequency shoul-
der features and the high-frequency double peaks (typi-
cally≳20 GHz) are consistent with observations. Vari-
ations in the CNM radial structure further modulate the
light-curve shape (see the right panels of Figure 5; e.g.,
De Colle et al. 2012; Generozov et al. 2017), and our
models are broadly consistent with the observed behav-
ior of Swift J1644+57 and AT2022cmc. Jet precession
or angular structure can further distort the timing and
shape of the radio emission (e.g., Stone & Loeb 2012;
Teboul & Metzger 2023; Calder´ on et al. 2024; Lu et al.
2024; Wang et al. 2025; Yuan et al. 2026). A detailed,
quantitative comparison that constrains the CNM will
require a broader parameter survey, which we plan to
pursue in future work.

5.SUMMARY
The increasing complexity of TDE radio light curves
motivates a detailed modeling beyond analytical approx-
imations. This work presents three-dimensional hydro-
dynamic simulations of TDE outflow-CNM interactions
using a broken power-law density profile, and computes
the resulting radio emission, and provides analytical es-
timates to aid interpretation. We find that different
shape of radio light curves reflect different properties of
the CNM and outflow. We summarize the main obser-
vational features of TDE radio light curves and present
corresponding hydrodynamic simulations that capture
their key physical features. The principal results are as
follows.
1.Double-peaked flare:Interaction between an
outflow and a broken power-law CNM naturally
produces double-peaked radio light curves, with
characteristic peaks at∼several hundred days and
∼several thousand days respectively. The simu-
lations are qualitatively consistent with analytical
expectations.
2.Early-peaked flare:The late flare is weak or
absent when the outflow is strongly decelerated
within the Bondi radius or when the CNM beyond
the Bondi radius is tenuous.

11
3.Late-peaked flare:A low-density CNM inside
the Bondi radius suppresses the early radio flare,
while a late radio flare can arise from the sub-
sequent interaction between the outflow and the
CNM beyondR B.
4.Sharp-rise flare:Interaction between the out-
flow and dense CNM (for example, dense clouds
or a dusty torus) produces rapid accumulation of
shocked material and a steep rise in radio lumi-
nosity; a shallow inner profile (k i ≲1.5) further
weakens the early flare and makes an apparently
single late sharp rise more likely.
5.Jetted TDE:A fast, narrow jet interacting with a
broken power-law CNM produces a low-frequency
shoulder (a bumpy rise) and double peaks at suf-
ficiently high observed frequencies.
Therefore, the radio emission provides a valuable di-
agnostic of nuclear gas distributions and outflow prop-
erties.

Mej, a steeper inner profile (largerk i) gives a later break time because the outflow sweeps up more mass before reaching
the Bondi radius.

density profile. In the optically thin regime, Equation (A8) predicts (νL ν)thin ∝t 3 andν a ∝t 0.3, which explains the
bumpy evolution ofν p seen in our simulations. However, strong deceleration and the finite thickness of the shocked
region smooth the transition, resulting in a shallower rise (νL ν ∝t 1.7) that deviates from the idealt 3 scaling.

B.APPLICATION TO AT2022VWL
We apply our model to a TDE candidate of AT2020vwl, which is found in a galaxy SDSS J153037.80+265856.8/LEDA
1794348 at z = 0.0325 on October 10, 2020 (e.g., Hodgkin et al. 2020; Hammerstein et al. 2021; Yao et al. 2023). Radio
observations started at around 140 days after the optical flare, where the radio was detected in the declining phase
(e.g., Goodwin et al. 2023a). Atν >2 GHz, the luminosity falls until∼400 days, and the light curve can be explained
by the interaction between the wind and the CNM. After 400 days, the light curve enters a second flare lasting 1265
days (e.g., Goodwin et al. 2025a), which may arise from the density profile transition at the Bondi radius.
To reproduce the light curve of AT2020vwl, we perform hydrodynamical simulations of the outflow-CNM interaction
with the wind structure (θ ej = 120◦), motivated by its second radio flare which is notably weaker than the first. The
Bondi radius of this source isR B ∼2.3×10 16cm (e.g., Bondi 1952; Matsumoto & Piran 2024), withM BH ∼10 5.6M⊙
(e.g., Yao et al. 2023). Based on the observed spectral energy distribution and light-curve slope, we approximately
estimate thek i = 2.5 andp= 2.8 (e.g., Goodwin et al. 2023a, 2025a). For simplicity, we assume a flat CNM profile
outside the Bondi radius, i.e.,k o = 0.0, and setn B = 2000 cm −3 (e.g., Goodwin et al. 2025a). We find that the
total swept-up mass within the Bondi radius isM B ∼2.6×10 −4 M⊙. Observationally, a distinct double-flare feature
requires the total outflow mass to satisfyM ej ≳a few timesM B. Based on observational data, the second radio flare
is expected to begin between 432 and 577 days, so we can estimate the outflow velocityv ej ≳0.022c. We present the
fitting of the radio light curves for AT2022vwl in the left panel of Figure A2, where we choose a set of appropriate
parameters:M ej ∼7.4×10 −4M⊙,v ej ∼0.025c,ϵ e = 0.06 andϵ B = 0.05. In the right panel of Figure A2, we present
the spectral fitting at different epochs. Our model successfully reproduces the slow rise at late times (from∼400
days), which is not well captured by alternative models (e.g., Zhuang et al. 2025; Matsumoto & Piran 2024), with only
slight deviations observed in two cases between 800 and 1000 days. This steep variation over a period of approximately
100 days may result from the interaction of the outflow with another steep density profile, such as a torus or clouds
(e.g., Zhuang et al. 2025; Lei et al. 2024).
