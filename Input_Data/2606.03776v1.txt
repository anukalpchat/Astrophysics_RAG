ABSTRACT
Next-generation gravitational-wave detectors such as the Einstein Telescope and Cosmic Explorer will be able to detect binary
black-hole mergers out to the cosmic dawn. Mergers observed in the local Universe represent a mixture of systems formed across
the entire cosmic history, spanning a wide range of astrophysical environments. Iron-group elements govern metallicity effects on
stellar evolution, making metallicity a key tracer that leaves a strong imprint on the black-hole population. We introduce the concept
of a “target” redshift, zt, defined as the epoch at which more than 90% of stars form with metallicity Z< 0.1 Z⊙. This provides a
straightforward way to isolate mergers originating from metal-poor environments. The determination of zt relies on the reconstruction
of the cosmic star-formation rate density as a function of iron abundance. This reconstruction is not unique, as it depends on the
combination of different empirical scaling relations. Consequently,zt spans a broad range, from zt ∼ 4 to zt > 10, depending on the
adopted model variation. We present a statistical framework that enables rapid tests of astrophysical predictions against forecasted
observations from next-generation gravitational wave detectors. By quantifying variations in the binary black-hole merger-rate density
between the target redshift and the local Universe, our approach maps evolutionary trends across parameter space and estimates the
detection statistics required to distinguish genuine astrophysical variations from statistical fluctuations.

1. Introduction
Current ground-based gravitational wave (GW) detectors LIGO,
Virgo, and KAGRA (LVK) constrain the redshift distribution of
merging binary black holes (BHs) most strongly up to z∼ 0.2;
credibility intervals grow rapidly at higher redshifts and become
uninformative for z≳ 1 (Fishbach et al. 2018; Callister et al. 2020;
Schiebelbein-Zwack & Fishbach 2024; Abac et al. 2025; Tenorio
et al. 2025). Next-generation facilities such as Einstein Telescope
(ET; Abac et al. 2026b) and Cosmic Explorer (CE; Evans et al.
2021) will detect binary BH mergers beyond z∼ 20, unveiling
a whole new segment of the binary BH population (though ac-
curately constraining whether binaries truly sit at those redshifts
will be somewhat challenging, cf. Ng et al. 2022; Mancarella
et al. 2023). At those redshifts, the potential existence (and hence
detection) of primordial BHs of cosmological origin presents a
tantalizing opportunity for new probes of fundamental physics
(e.g. Iacovelli & Maggiore 2024).
From a stellar-physics perspective, extending the observa-
tional reach of the binary BH population to higher redshifts will
be transformative. The population of GW sources observed in the
local Universe is a mixture of systems formed across the entire
cosmic history in different environments. This reflects the wide
range of possible time delays between progenitor star formation
and the eventual compact-binary merger (O’Shaughnessy et al.
2010; Dominik et al. 2012; Mandel & de Mink 2016; Lamberts
et al. 2016; Giacobbo & Mapelli 2018; Vitale et al. 2019; El-
dridge et al. 2019; Neijssel et al. 2019; du Buisson et al. 2020;
Safarzadeh et al. 2020; van Son et al. 2022; Chru´sli´nska 2024).
Sources detected at a given redshift zt originate from progenitor
stars formed over a narrower range of earlier redshifts (>z t) and,
consequently, from a more constrained set of environments. Dif-
ferent “target redshifts” zt will be required to effectively select
populations that formed under specific environmental conditions
in GW data from next-generation detectors.
The key tracer that evolves over cosmic time and leaves strong
imprints on the binary BH merger population is the environment
metallicity, which is the focus of this work. Metallicity in massive
stars impacts their mass-loss rates via stellar winds (Heger et al.
2003; Vink et al. 2011; Ziosi et al. 2014; Mapelli 2016; Giacobbo
et al. 2018; Mapelli et al. 2020; Chowdhury & Santra 2024). Stars
in high-metallicity environments can lose a substantial fraction
of their initial mass before undergoing core collapse, while stars
in low-metallicity environments experience much weaker winds
and therefore retain a significantly larger fraction of their mass
throughout their evolution (Kudritzki et al. 1987; Vink et al. 2001;
Giacobbo et al. 2018). This directly translates into the masses of
the resulting BHs. The dependence of the maximum BH mass
on metallicity is a robust prediction of stellar evolution models,
with more massive remnants being systematically produced in
more metal-poor environments (Mapelli et al. 2009; Belczynski
et al. 2010a; Fryer et al. 2012; Mapelli et al. 2013; Spera et al.
2015). Metallicity also influences the opacity and nuclear burning
processes inside the stars, which in turn affect its radii at various
stages of their evolution, especially during post-main-sequence
phases (Klencki et al. 2020; Xin et al. 2022; Farrell et al. 2022;
Romagnolo et al. 2023). The complex interplay of metallicity-
dependent stellar winds and stellar radii propagates through the
evolutionary processes that form merging binary BHs.
Despite the large variety of proposed binary BH formation
channels—ranging from isolated to dynamical scenarios, with
various subtypes within each class (Mapelli 2021; Mandel &
Farmer 2022)—the overall trend is a significant increase in binary
BH formation efficiency at lower metallicities (Chruslinska et al.
2019; van Son et al. 2025; Klencki et al. 2018; Giacobbo &

Mapelli 2018; Neijssel et al. 2019). This inextricably links the
GW event rate, the binary BH mass distribution, and the redshift
distribution. Disentangling such features from the set of binary
BHs currently observed by the LVK interferometers is proving
challenging (Rinaldi et al. 2024; Lalleman et al. 2025; Tenorio
et al. 2025; Heinzel et al. 2025a). We need to observe further out,
but how much further?
In this paper, we present a statistical argument aimed at quan-
tifying the level of variation in the binary BH merger rate across
mass and redshift that can be inferred with ET and CE, and link
it to the astrophysical environments one wishes to probe. How
precisely must the binary BH population be measured at a given
target redshift zt to determine whether its properties differ from
those inferred at z∼ 0? Using state-of-the-art estimates for the
metallicity-dependent star-formation history (SFH) calibrated
on observations, we illustrate how to make astrophysically in-
formed choices for the target redshift with forecasted binary BH
observations from next-generation GW detectors.
2. Goals and methods
2.1. T arget redshift
Our goal is to quantify the measurement requirements for a con-
clusive comparison of binary BH mergers observed at a targetz t
and a reference redshift zref ∼ 0, where zt is such that the selected
sub-population originates from an environment significantly dif-
ferent (e.g., in metallicity) from the one observed at zref. This
comparison aims to isolate the impact of the physical conditions
under which the progenitors formed and evolved on the result-
ing BH merger population. Time delays between formation and
merger imply that the observable population of compact binaries
contains systems formed throughout the entire cosmic history.
Selecting sources at a given zt is therefore the most straightfor-
ward way to ensure that the observed mergers originated from
stellar progenitors that formed and evolved at redshifts >z t. In an
idealized scenario, different choices ofzt correspond to distinct
astrophysical environments, and therefore probe different regimes
of stellar physics.
To illustrate this concept with a case study, we focus on the
metal-poor regime. For the purpose of this work, we define the
target redshift zt as the redshift at which > 90% of the stars
form with a metallicity Z< 0.1 Z⊙, where Z⊙ denotes the solar
metallicity. Although our approach does not require making any
assumption about how binary BH properties evolve, the target
redshift zt depends on how the cosmic star-formation rate varies
with redshift and metallicity. The latter is subject to considerable
uncertainties (Chruslinska & Nelemans 2019; Broekgaarden et al.
2022; Chru´sli´nska 2024; Chru´sli´nska et al. 2025), which propa-
gate to our zt selection. In Sec. 3.1, we use the recent estimates
by Chru´sli´nska et al. (2025) to infer that zt ≳ 4 within current
uncertainties, though values as high asz t ≳10 may be required.
For reference in what follows, it is worth keeping in mind
that ET should see ∼ 102 yr−1 BH mergers with ∼ 10 M⊙ and
∼ 35 M⊙ at z∼ 4. This estimate is based on Fig. 3.5 by Abac
et al. (2026b), which uses bins with∆z =0 .2,∆ logm 1/M⊙ =0 .1,
the mass distribution model by Abbott et al. (2023), a Madau-
Dickinson SFH (Madau & Dickinson 2014), and a ∝t −1 delay-
time distribution, and considers a single ET instrument in a 2L
configuration with 15 km arm length.

3. Results
3.1. Selecting metal-poor progenitors
As described in Sec. 2.1, we tie our target redshift to the epoch at
which at least 90% of cosmic star formation occurred at metal-
licities an order of magnitude below solar. Such conditions were
characteristic of the early Universe, which was dominated by
young galaxies that had not yet undergone substantial chemical
enrichment through stellar nucleosynthesis. In contrast, star for-
mation in the present-day Universe spans a much broader range
of chemical compositions
To determine how early in cosmic history these conditions
prevailed, and thus to estimate zt, we use the suite of metallicity-
dependent cosmic SFH models by Chru´sli´nska et al. (2025), who
extend and validate the observation-based framework by Chruslin-
ska & Nelemans (2019) and Chru ´sli´nska et al. (2021) against
more recent empirical constraints. Within this framework, the
metallicity-dependent cosmic SFH is obtained by combining em-
pirical relations linking galaxy properties (star-formation rate,
gas-phase oxygen abundance, and stellar mass) with their number
statistics. Chru´sli´nska et al. (2025) introduce a set of model vari-
ations that bracket the dominant uncertainties in these relations
and propagate them to the final result. These variations explore
different assumptions regarding: (i) the low-mass slope of the
galaxy stellar mass function and its redshift evolution; (ii) the
slope and redshift evolution of the star formation rate-mass rela-
tion; (iii) the normalization and high-redshift (z> 3) evolution of
the relation connecting galaxy gas-phase oxygen abundances to
their properties (masses, star formation rates); and (iv) the form
of the relation between the oxygen-to-iron abundance ratio and
the specific star formation rate of galaxies. The latter relation
(iv), introduced by (Chru ´sli´nska et al. 2024), encapsulates the
delay in enhanced iron enrichment by Type Ia supernovae (SNIa)
relative to the prompt enrichment by core-collapse supernovae
from massive progenitors. The uncertain delay time distribution
of SNIa is one of the key factors shaping the relation. Chru´sli´nska
et al. (2025) apply the relation (iv) to convert gas-phase oxygen
abundances into iron abundances and derive the iron-dependent
cosmic SFH. This step is crucial for a meaningful determina-
tion of zt, as iron-group elements set the metallicity dependence
of stellar evolution by regulating stellar opacities and radiation-
driven winds (Iglesias & Rogers 1996; Vink et al. 2001; Sander &
Vink 2020; Pauli et al. 2025), and thereby influence the properties
of stellar-origin compact objects and related transients (Woosley
et al. 2002; Langer & Norman 2006; Belczynski et al. 2010b;
Perley et al. 2016; Klencki et al. 2018; Schulze et al. 2018; Broek-
gaarden et al. 2022; Romagnolo et al. 2023; Iorio et al. 2023;
Chru´sli´nska 2024; Vink et al. 2024; Hirschi et al. 2025). Accord-
ingly, throughout this work, we use the term “metallicity” to refer
to iron abundance. Figure 3 illustrates the set of model variations
from Chru´sli´nska et al. (2025) in the context of our target-redshift
definition. Each curve shows the fraction of stellar mass formed
with metallicity below 0 .1 Z⊙ between redshifts z and 10, and
thus defines a corresponding target redshift via its intersection
with the 90% horizontal line. Each model results from differ-
ent combinations of the ingredients (i-iv) used to construct the
observation-based, iron-dependent cosmic SFH.
The model variations in Fig. 3 span a wide range of possible
target redshifts. We define two representative thresholds, corre-
sponding to the lower and upper bounds on zt implied by the
metallicity-dependent cosmic SFH models. These are referred to
as optimistic and pessimistic, respectively, reflecting the detec-
tion statistics required to accurately constrain variations in the
merger rate between zt and the local Universe. Even with next-

3.2. T argeted features in the mass spectrum
We focus our discussion on two bins representing typical “low
mass” (∼ 10 M⊙) and “high mass” ( ∼ 35 M⊙) BHs in systems
merging at low redshift, motivated by the observed features in
the mass spectrum identified by LVK (Abac et al. 2025, 2026a;
Banagiri et al. 2025; Guttman et al. 2026). The peak in the pri-
mary mass distribution at around 10 M⊙ is currently the most
robustly detected feature. A broader overdensity (with a merger
rate density of ∼ 1/50 of that measured in the “low mass” peak)
is also present at ∼ 35 M⊙, though the statistical significance
of this feature is model dependent (Farah et al. 2024; Heinzel
et al. 2025b; Sridhar et al. 2025; Tenorio et al. 2025) and may
instead reflect fluctuations due to limited sample sizes (Corelli
et al. 2026). At higher masses the BH merger rate density declines
more steeply.
Stellar evolution, binary interactions, and metallicity all im-
print on the binary BH merger mass distribution, complicating the
interpretation of these features and shaping their evolution with
redshift (e.g. Abac et al. 2025). The low-mass peak is predomi-
nantly attributed to BH mergers formed through the evolution of
isolated binaries, via both common-envelope evolution and stable
mass transfer channels (e.g. Mandel & Farmer 2022), but may
also include a contribution from dynamically assembled mergers
in dense stellar clusters (e.g. Ye et al. 2026).

The origin of the high-mass overdensity is heavily debated.
The appealing interpretation as a pile-up caused by (pulsational)
pair-instability supernovae (Moriya & Langer 2015; Yoshida et al.
2016; Marchant et al. 2019; Stevenson et al. 2019; Woosley &
Heger 2021), which can map stellar progenitors spanning a wide
range of masses onto a relatively narrow range of BH masses,
is difficult to reconcile with current models, which generally
predict such a feature at higher masses (e.g. Hendriks et al. 2023).
Multiple formation channels, including isolated binaries evolving
through stable mass transfer (e.g. Xu et al. 2025) or chemically
homogeneous evolution (e.g. de Mink & Mandel 2016), as well
as dynamically assembled systems and hierarchical mergers in
dense stellar environments (e.g. Barber & Antonini 2025), can all
contribute to the formation of BH mergers in this mass range.
The low- and high-mass BH mergers likely arise from differ-
ent (combinations of) formation channels, each characterised by
distinct metallicity dependencies and delay time distributions. For
example, the stable mass-transfer channel typically yields longer
delay times than the common-envelope channel (Gallegos-Garcia
et al. 2021; Klencki et al. 2021, 2026; Marchant et al. 2021; van
Son et al. 2022), and both channels may become inefficient at
high metallicity. Chemically homogeneous evolution is expected
to operate primarily (if not exclusively) at low metallicity and
to preferentially produce high-mass (≳ 25,M ⊙) BH. In addition,
independent of the formation channel, more massive BHs are
expected to form more efficiently in metal poor environments due
to the strong dependence of wind mass loss rates of their stellar
progenitors on metallicity (e.g. Mapelli et al. 2009; Belczynski

6
et al. 2010a; Spera et al. 2015; Di Carlo et al. 2020; Vink et al.
2021; Romagnolo et al. 2024; Merritt et al. 2025). Therefore,
the redshift evolution of the BH merger rate is highly uncertain,
but expected to vary across the mass spectrum, and the role of
metallicity in shaping this evolution likely differs between our
“low mass” and “high mass” bins.
Motivated by these considerations, we define “low” and “high”
mass bins centered at 10 M⊙ and 35 M⊙, respectively, based on
the primary mass of the binary. To present the merger rate density
ratio results, we fix the secondary BH mass by assuming constant
mass-ratio (q) systems. We choose q based on results by Abac
et al. (2025): BHs with masses ∼ 10 M⊙ preferentially merge
with lighter companions ( q∼ 0.8), while those with masses ∼
35 M⊙ tend to merge with more equal-mass partners (q∼ 1). This
simplified setup is intended to illustrate the key features of our
method and its ability to enable rapid, informative comparisons
between merger rates at different redshifts.
3.3. Constraining the merger-rate density ratio
We now present the results from our merger rate density ratio esti-
mator. We fix the reference redshift tozref =0 .2, which allows us
to anchor our estimates to the mass-specific merger rate measured
by LVK and avoid extrapolating the uncertain redshift evolution
of binary-BH population properties. The expected number of de-
tections in the reference bin, Nref, over a given observing time
Tobs then follows from the assumed sensitivity of next-generation
detectors. The results discussed in this section assumeTobs =1 yr,
and the corresponding number of events Nref associated with the
“reference” bin is fixed to 10 (estimated from Fig. 3.5 by Abac
et al. 2026b at zref =0 .2). The confidence interval of the merger
rate density ratio depends on both the number of detections falling
within the considered bins and their associated spacetime-volume.
To show the behavior of Eq. (14) in different scenarios, we pro-
duced two sets of plots: we show both the Nt/Nref vs. zt plane
(Fig. 4) assuming a fixed zref; and zt vs. zref plane (Fig. 5) assum-
ing a fixedN t/Nref.

4. Discussion
There is no unique target redshift at which low-metallicity envi-
ronments can be isolated. Even with our modest requirement of a
low metallicity of 10% of the solar iron abundance, current mod-
els span a wide range, from zt ∼ 4 to zt > 10. If the “pessimistic”
scenario (fast Fe enrichment) holds, the relevant redshift window
shifts to such high values that, despite the excellent sensitivity
of future detectors, uncertainties in source parameters may hin-
der the identification of specific subpopulations. The ability of
next-generation detectors to probe stellar physics will therefore
also rely on external constraints on the SFH. While the reach of
ET+CE is sufficient to observe massive binary BHs out to the
cosmic dawn, Figs. 4 and 5 shows that it may remain challenging
to determine conclusively whether BH formation physics differs

The delay-time prescriptions considered here also bracket a
range of expected merger timescales: stable mass-transfer chan-
nel can produce characteristic delays of order ∼ 1 Gyr, common-
envelope channel can yield much shorter delays, and other chan-
nels may populate the intermediate regime. We point out that
delay times of ∼ 1 Gyr are comparable to the age of the Universe
at zt, and therefore yield R(zt)/R(zref) ≈ 0 regardless of η, as illus-
trated by the dotted line in Fig. 7. For short delay times, instead,
R(zt)/R(zref) ≳ 1, with larger values if η is enhanced below a
low metallicity threshold (solid and dashed lines in Fig. 7). The
realistic BH-merger population is likely to arise from a mixture
of channels, with different delay times and formation efficiencies,
and with different relative contributions in the low- and high-mass
bins (see Sec. 3.2). Therefore, distinguishing among most current
formation models, and identifying differences between the mass
bins at zt, would require constraining R(zt)/R(zref) to within a
factor of a few.
In Sec. 3.3, we showed, using the estimator D of Eq. (15),
that achieving the detection statistics required to establish a min-
imum merger rate variation within individual mass bins is not
sufficient to demonstrate a differential evolution across masses.
In this context, our framework provides a fast way to assess the
detection requirements needed to test stellar evolution predictions
for the binary BH merger rate density. While matching the relative
number of detections across masses and redshifts to theoretical
expectations can be formally reduced to applications of Eq. (9),
we argue that such ratios should be interpreted in light of the
minimum detection thresholds identified in Fig. 4, i.e., the re-
quirements needed to establish a rate evolution between given
redshifts in the first place. Furthermore, we find that achieving
an uncertainty onDat the level of∼50% (∼10%), based on its
95% credible interval, requires∼200 (∼4000) detections in the
reference bin,N ref.
The analysis presented here focuses on mass bins centered
at 10 M⊙ and 35 M⊙, motivated by the features identified with
current LVK data. Future applications could target different mass
ranges. The choice of the reference redshift is also important, as
Nref sets the width of the confidence regions in Figs. 4 and 5. Our
framework currently compares a single pair of bins, but it can be
extended to incorporate multiple locations in parameter space.
Our framework relies on a simplified counting approach that
neglects individual parameter-estimation uncertainties. Fully in-
corporating these effects requires computationally intensive hier-
archical analyses (Mandel et al. 2019; Vitale et al. 2022) that can
be adapted to select events in a given mass or redshift range (e.g.
Kishore Roy et al. 2025). Nevertheless, our approximation is ade-
quate for rapid forecasting studies aimed at building the science
case and informing the design of next-generation interferometer
networks (Evans et al. 2021; Abac et al. 2026b).
5. Summary
Next-generation GW detectors, such as ET and CE, will extend
the observational horizon for stellar-mass BH mergers far beyond
current limits, revealing previously unexplored BH populations.
This increased reach opens new opportunities for stellar astro-
physics: by selecting sources at specific redshifts, one can probe
remnants originating from progenitors formed in early cosmic
environments, thereby isolating the impact of low metallicity.
In this work, we presented a statistical framework to quantify
the precision required to compare binary BH populations at a
target redshift zt with the one observed at zref =0 .2. Specifically:
– We developed an approach based on Bayesian inference
(alongside a likelihood-ratio test) to estimate the merger rate
density ratio between two mass–redshift bins, accounting for
selection effects through the differential space-time volume.
– To illustrate the method, we focused on binary BH subpopula-
tions with primary masses of 10 M⊙ and 35 M⊙, correspond-
ing to overdensities observed in the BH mass spectrum with
LVK data.
– Using updated models of the metallicity-dependent cosmic
SFH, we showed that ensuring the majority of progenitors
form in low-metallicity environments (Z< 0.1Z⊙) requires
target redshifts of zt > 4, potentially extending to zt > 10
depending on model uncertainties.
While physically motivated choices of the target redshift
can help isolate BH progenitors formed in specific environ-
ments—thereby enhancing the power of GWs as probes of stellar
evolution—constraining merger-rate variations with high preci-
sion may remain challenging. Within this framework, accurate
characterization of the local BH population will continue to play
a central role in determining Rt/Rref. The simplicity of the ap-
proach makes it a reusable tool for rapid bin-to-bin comparisons
in future population studies, as well as for testing stellar evolution
model predictions against observations from next-generation GW
detectors.

not satisfied. In this regime, the Bayesian approach is preferable,
as it does not rely on asymptotic assumptions and depends only on
the choice of prior for the Poisson rate parameter (see Sec. 2.3.2)
associated with each bin.
The results in this paper are presented using the Bayesian
approach. We note that Eq. (14) is formally defined for integer
values of Nt and Nref, which we have extended to real numbers.
We have verified that this approximation has a negligible impact
on the results presented here.
