ABSTRACT
Long-period transients (LPTs) challenge our knowledge of the mechanism producing radio periodic pulsations in compact objects.
Some LPTs have been associated with systems hosting a white dwarf and a low-mass star in a detached binary. Recently, a new
LPT (ASKAP J174508.9-505149) has been classified as an accreting cataclysmic variable (CV). In the present letter, we report on
the detailed study of the X-ray variability of ASKAP J174508.9-505149 as observed byXMM-NewtonandEinstein Probebetween
September 2025 and May 2026. Simultaneous optical and radio observations are also presented. We studied the timing variability
of the source, and estimated an X-ray periodicity ofP=4868(22) s, consistent with radio and optical periods. We also observe the
same periodicity in the hardness ratio extracted from theXMM-Newtonobservation, peaking at the minimum of the modulation. A
long-term modulation is also present in the X-rays and in the B-band photometry, but it is poorly constrained by the current data set.
Spectral X-ray analysis shows the presence of a black-body component (∼0.1 keV), a collisionally ionized plasma (∼15 keV), and
an absorption feature at 0.77 keV (possibly due to Oxygen-VII). This is the third LPT detected in the X-ray band, the second with a
detected X-ray periodicity and variable X-ray emission, and the first conclusively recognised as an accreting magnetic CV .

1. Introduction
Long-period transients (LPTs) are a class of recently identified
radio sources characterised by periodic radio bursts, with peri-
odicities spanning from minutes to hours (for a review, see Rea
et al. 2026). They show bright (up to a few tens of Jy) radio
pulses with strong linear and circular polarizations (up to 100%;
see e.g. Caleb et al. 2024; Bloot et al. 2025), hinting at a coher-
ent radio emission process, possibly involving magnetic fields.
Rea et al. (2026) identified 12 LPTs (see their Table 1), but the
number is rapidly growing1.
The nature and the emission mechanism of LPTs are puz-
zling. The high polarization and the coherent emission ini-
tially pointed towards isolated, strongly magnetised neutron stars
slowed down by fall-back accretion at birth (see e.g. Hurley-
Walker et al. 2022; Ronchi et al. 2022). However, the long pe-
riodicity would put LPTs in the so-called "death valley" of the
P− ˙Pdiagram, where no coherent emission is expected by a typ-
ical radio-pulsar pair-production process (Hurley-Walker et al.
2023; Suvorov & Melatos 2023; Rea et al. 2024). The observed
1 For an updated list, seehttps://vast-survey.org/LPTs/
spin periods and expected population are more consistent with
white dwarf (WD) emitters, but the higher luminosities and po-
larisation in LPTs are at odds with the known AR Sco-like ob-
jects (Marsh et al. 2016; Buckley et al. 2017; Pelisoli et al. 2024;
Castro Segura et al. 2025). More recently, two LPTs have been
recognized as WD-M dwarf binaries (Hurley-Walker et al. 2024;
de Ruiter et al. 2025; Rodriguez 2025; Rodriguez et al. 2026),
thus models involving binary systems hosting a magnetised WD
and a low-mass companion have gained significant traction (see
e.g. Horváth et al. 2026, and references therein). The detection
of X-ray counterparts to LPTs ASKAP J1832-0911 (Wang et al.
2025) and ASKAP J144834-685644 (Anumarlapudi et al. 2025),
and the variability and periodic X-ray modulation of the former,
has opened the interesting possibility of at least some LPTs be-
ing accreting systems (CVs; for reviews, see Warner 1995; Page
& Shaw 2022; Scaringi et al. 2026), but no LPT-like radio emis-
sion was conclusively detected from an accreting CV until now.
Accreting CVs with strongly magnetic WDs are typically di-
vided in two main classes. When the WD magnetic fieldB≳
107−8 G, the WD spin and orbit of the order of hours are syn-
chronised, and material is accreted along magnetic field lines as

no accretion disc is formed. These systems are usually referred
to as "polars". On the other hand, whenB≃10 5 −10 7 G the
spin (∼minutes) and orbit (∼hours) are not (yet) synchronised.
These systems are known as intermediate polars (IPs). In such
cases, an accretion disc/ring generally forms, but is truncated at
the WD magnetospheric radius (Ferrario et al. 2015).
ASKAP J174508.9-505149 (ASKAP J1745) was discovered
during an untargeted search for circularly polarised sources
with the Australian SKA Pathfinder radio telescope (ASKAP;
Hotan et al. 2021). It is the first LPT whose periodicity
P=4841.89 +0.12
−0.14 s has been detected in the radio (ASKAP,
MeerKAT), optical (SOAR), and X-ray band (Einstein Probe,
EP), and the first LPT conclusively identified as an accreting
WD-M dwarf binary (Rose et al. 2026). Here we report the X-
ray analysis of ASKAP J1745 as observed by EP andXMM-
Newton, between September 2025 and May 2026. Furthermore,
we present simultaneous radio (ASKAP) and B-band (XMM-
Newton/OM) observations.
2. Observations and data analysis
We requested a series of target of opportunity observations en-
compassing X-ray, optical, and radio bands (Table A.1) to mon-
itor the multi-wavelength flux, timing and spectral evolution of
ASKAP J1745. The reported X-ray net exposure times take into
account flare filtering. The details of the data reduction are re-
ported in Appendix A. Unless otherwise stated, the errors re-
ported in this letter correspond to 1σ(68.3%) confidence lev-
els. To compute the unabsorbed luminosities, we considered a
distanced≃0.57 kpc, estimated throughGaiaparallax and
supported by the M-dwarf companion magnitude (Rose et al.
2026). We note, however, that there is still some uncertainty on
ASKAP J1745 distance, which according to photogeometric es-
timates can be up tod≃6.5 kpc (Rose et al. 2026).
2.1. X-ray data analysis
In Fig. B.1, we show the FXT1, FXT2, and FXT3 light curves,
while in the left panel of Fig. 1 we show theXMM-Newtonlight
curve in the 0.3–10 keV band. The modulation is clearly visible
in all light curves, especially in the longXMM-Newtonexposure.
In the middle panel of Fig. 1, we show the power density spec-
trum (PDS) in the 0.3–10 keV band of theXMM-Newtondata, in
which a peak is visible at a frequency of≃2×10 −4 Hz.
We report the details of the timing analysis in Appendix B.
Through phase-fitting, we estimated a periodP=4868(22) s. In
the right panel of Fig. 1, we show the foldedXMM-Newtonlight
curve in the 0.3–10 keV band and the ASKAP folded light curve.
The EP folded light curve is shown in Fig. B.3. All the light
curves are folded with the same timing solution (P=4868 s,
reference epoch MJD 60952). The radio and X-ray profiles show
a 180◦ shift in phase. For theXMM-Newtonobservation, we es-
timate a pulsed fraction (defined as the semi-amplitude of the
sinusoid divided by the source average count rate) ofPF XMM =
23(1)%. For the combined EP/FXT observations, we estimate
a pulsed fractionPF EP =22(3)%. In Fig. 1, we also show the
folded hardness ratio of theXMM-Newtondata, defined as the
ratio of the hard (1.5–10 keV) counts over the soft (0.3–1.5 keV)
counts. The hardness ratio shows hardening at the minimum of
the flux modulation. By folding the soft and hardXMM-Newton
light curves, we derived a pulsed fraction ofPF soft =28(1)%
andPF hard =17(1)%, respectively.
TheXMM-Newtonlight curve also shows flux modulation on
a longer timescale. To describe this additional component, ten-
tatively reported by Rose et al. (2026) at timescalesP long,radio ≃
8 hr≃29 ks, we modelled the light curve with two sinusoidal
functions (left panel of Figure 1). We considered a constant
component (to account for the mean count rate) and two sinu-
soidal components, one with period fixed at the phase-fitting
periodP=4868 s, and one to model the long-term modula-
tion observed in the light curve. For the latter, which signifi-
cantly improves the fit (significance>5σ), we derive a period
Plong =52(2) ks, approximately twiceP long,radio. However, we
note thatP long is very close to the net exposure time (≃44.7 ks).
To analyse the X-ray spectra of ASKAP J1745, we used
XSPEC v12.15.0 (Arnaud 1996). We adopted the element abun-
dances and cross sections of Wilms et al. (2000) and Verner
et al. (1996), respectively. The uncertainties reported for the
spectral parameters correspond to 90% confidence ranges. We
adopted the pseudo-modelcfluxto compute the unabsorbed
fluxes. Using the 3D NH-Tool 2 (Doroshenko 2024), we found
that along the line of sight of ASKAP J1745 the Galactic ab-
sorption grows from 1×10 21 cm−2 to 2×10 21 cm−2 between
0.5 and 6.5 kpc. We therefore assumed the average value 1.5×
1021 cm−2. Given the lower statistics of the EP/FXT data, we
modelled the spectra from observations FXT1, FXT2, FXT3,
FXT4, and FXT5 with a simple absorbed power-law model
(tbabs×powerlawin XSPEC syntax), with the absorption fixed
atN H =1.5×10 21 cm−2. The fit is satisfactory in all five obser-
vations (χ2/dof≃1−1.2).
To model theXMM-Newtonenergy spectrum, we considered
aconstantcomponent to take into account inter-calibration dif-
ferences between the cameras, freezing the PN constant to 1.
We included atbabscomponent to take into account the Galac-
tic absorption. The hardness evolution shown in Fig. 1 indicates
an intrinsic absorption effect of local matter whose partial cov-
ering changes during the period. Therefore, we also included a
tbpcfcomponent. The spectrum is well-described by anapec
plusbbodyradmodel. There are structured residuals at energies
≃0.8 keV (panel b of Fig. 2), thus we added agabscomponent.
In Fig. 2, we show the unfolded spectrum from theXMM-
Newtonobservation, together with the best-fit model and residu-
als. We report the values of the parameters of our best-fit model
in Table C.1. For the absorption line at low energies, we estimate
a centroid energy of 0.77(1) keV , consistent with absorption from
O VII. We also find a plasma temperature for theapeccom-
ponentkT apec =15 +4
−3 keV and a blackbody temperature for the
bbodyradcomponentkT bb =0.14 +0.03
−0.01 keV . We estimated a 0.5–
10 keV unabsorbed fluxF0.5−10 keV =2.5(3)×10 −12 erg cm−2 s−1,
showing a re-brightening relative to FXT3, and indicating fluc-
tuations in the flux evolution (Figure 3).

2.3. Radio data analysis
We detected a faint, polarised radio source at the location of
ASKAP J1745 in the time- and frequency-averaged ASKAP im-
age, with the peak flux densityF rad =0.23±0.04 mJy beam −1.
This is slightly lower than the continuum ASKAP detections re-
ported by Rose et al. (2026), though comparable to flux densi-
ties reported in that work for longer observations. For the dis-
tance range reported in Rose et al. (2026), this corresponds to
the specific luminosities ofL ν,rad =10 16–1019 erg s−1 Hz−1. We
conducted Lomb-Scargle analysis using the time series data and
identified periodic variability in the ASKAP data (see the inset in
the middle panel of Figure 1), despite the lack of any significant,
bright pulses during the observation. This results in a maximum
power peak atP=4775(470) s, which is consistent with the
X-ray period and the radio period in Rose et al. (2026).
3. Discussion and conclusions
We have reported our radio, optical, and X-ray campaign of
ASKAP J1745, an LPT recently discovered in archival ASKAP
data and recognized as the first accreting CV showing bright po-
larized radio pulses (Rose et al. 2026). We reported on the signif-
icant detection of the 4868-s X-ray period in theXMM-Newton
and EP data, confirming ASKAP J1745 as an accreting source.
Rose et al. (2026) identified ASKAP J1745, the first LPT for
which the radio periodicity is detected also in X-ray and opti-
cal data, as a binary system composed of a WD and an M dwarf.
The presence of such a modulation in the X-ray data points to a
magnetised WD.
If interpreted as the spin period, such a long period sug-
gests that ASKAP J1745 is either a polar or a slowly rotating
IP. Unfortunately, the length of ourXMM-Newtonobservation
does not allow us to constrain the nature of the long-term trend,
which would allow us to correctly classify ASKAP J1745. As-
suming thatP long ≃52 ks is a lower limit on the beat period
andP≃4868 s is the spin period, we can derive an upper
limit on the orbital period of the systemP UL <5370 s, mak-
ing ASKAP J1745 an asynchronous polar. If ASKAP J1745 is
instead an IP, the 4868-s period would reflect the spin motion of
the WD, while the orbital period would be much longer, possibly
Plong ≃52 ks. These long periodicities fall on the long end of the
spin and orbital period ranges of IPs (see, e.g., Coti Zelati et al.
2016; Halpern et al. 2018).
The ASKAP J1745 flux during FXT4 and FXT5 (performed
∼6 months after FXT3) is consistent with that observed dur-
ing our 2025 campaign (Figure 3), suggesting no large changes

in the X-ray emission level. A strictly synchronous polar is ex-
pected to show a more drastic on-offbehaviour (see e.g. the re-
views by Cropper 1990; Schwope 2025). A few asynchronous
polars and IPs, on the other hand, have shown steadier fluxes
(see e.g. Rea et al. 2017; Dutta & Rana 2022).
The energy-dependent pulsed fraction, together with the
hardness ratio showing a harder emission at the minimum of
the pulse profile, is consistent with phase-dependent local ab-
sorption caused by an accretion curtain, as photoelectric absorp-
tion suppresses soft X-rays more efficiently than hard X-rays. A
smooth sinusoidal modulation of the hardness ratio at the rota-
tional period is not a characteristic of polars but rather of IPs and
asynchronous polars (Mukai 2017). Our spectral analysis also
supports an IP or asynchronous polar interpretation. The spec-
tral decomposition is consistent with the standard magnetic CV
framework, with the hot, optically thin component associated
with shock-heated plasma in the post-shock accretion column
above the WD magnetic pole. The soft blackbody component,
instead, can be interpreted as thermalised emission from the WD
surface beneath the accretion column. The inferred emitting ra-
dius is smaller than the WD radius and can be associated with
accretion-heated polar regions (Bernardini et al. 2012; de Mar-
tino et al. 2020). We must note, however, that with the current
uncertainty on the distance of ASKAP J1745 any estimate of the
radius of the blackbody component is to be treated with caution.
The temperatures we derive for the spectral components are con-
sistent with those observed in other IPs, although the blackbody
temperature is particularly high (see e.g. Page & Shaw 2022).
The absorption feature at 0.77 keV is also consistent at 3σwith
absorption from O VII (0.74 keV), detected in other IPs (see e.g.
Bernardini et al. 2012, and references therein).
In the left panel of Fig. E.1, we show the position of
ASKAP J1745 in theL radio −L X phase space. ASKAP J1745 is
in the region of (magnetic) CVs. In the right panel, we show the
position of ASKAP J1745 in theP orb −P spin plane. Given the
uncertainties on the nature ofP long, we show both possibilities:
a)P long is a (lower limit) on the orbital period; b)P long is a beat
period between the orbital and the spin period. ASKAP J1745
is consistent either with (asynchronous) polars or IPs with long
orbital periods. In summary, the available data support the classi-
fication of ASKAP J1745 as a magnetic CV . Both the timing and
spectral analysis point towards an IP or an asynchronous polar,
but further observations are needed for a more definitive classi-
fication. In particular, longer uninterrupted X-ray observations,
accompanied by high-cadence optical observations, will allow
us to obtain a better estimate of the short-term period and to as-
certain whether the long-term periodicity is caused by a beat.

Appendix A.1: XMM-Newton
We asked for anXMM-Newton(Jansen et al. 2001; Schartel et al.
2022) observation via Director Discretionary Time (DDT: Ob-
sID 0973390301; PI: Rea). We considered data coming from
the EPIC-pn camera (Strüder et al. 2001), the EPIC-MOS cam-
eras (Turner et al. 2001), and the Optical Monitor (OM; Mason
et al. 2001). Reflection Grating Spectrometer (RGS; den Herder
et al. 2001) data were not considered for our analysis, since the
low number of detected photons (≃500) in the background-
subtracted, first-order spectra did not improve our spectral analy-
sis. We performed data reduction and source events extraction in
the 0.3–10 keV band for timing and spectral analysis using SAS
(Gabriel et al. 2004) v22.1.0 with the latestXMM-Newtoncali-
brations and standard data reduction procedures. For the data re-
duction and event extraction of the EPIC-pn (EPIC-MOS) data,
we used theepproc(emproc) task and selected only events with
PATTERN≤4 (PATTERN≤12). We identified intervals of high-
background particle flares by extracting the light curves of the
entire field of view at energiesE>10 keV , filtering out intervals
during which the pn (MOS) background count rate was>0.4
(0.35) cts/s. For source event extraction, we considered a cir-
cular region with a radius of 30 ′′. A nearby, source-free circu-
lar region with radius of 50 ′′ on the same CCD was used for
background extraction. We used the taskepiclccorrto pro-
duce background- and vignetting-corrected light curves, while
we created the response matrices and ancillary files using the
tasksrmfgenandarfgen, respectively. We rebinned the energy
spectra to have at least 25 counts per energy bin to applyχ 2
statistics.
Data from the XMM Optical Monitor (OM) were acquired
both in Fast and Image modes with the B filter (effective wave-
length 450 nm, width 105 nm). We used both theomfchainand
theomichaintasks for the data reduction in the optical band.

Appendix A.2: Einstein Probe
The Follow-up X-ray Telescope (FXT; Chen et al. 2021, 2025;
Zhang et al. 2025) on board EP observed the source location
of ASKAP J1745 for three times in 2025 (PI: Rea, ObsIDs
06800000900, 08500000410, 08500000411) and twice in 2026
(PI: Wang, ObsIDs 11900670464, 11900711808), following the
detection of its counterpart in the first FXT observation (Rose
et al. 2026) . In this letter, we refer to EP ObsIDs 06800000900,
08500000410, 08500000411, 11900670464, and 11900711808
as observations FXT1, FXT2, FXT3, FXT4, and FXT5, respec-
tively. The data were reduced with thefxtchaintool available
within the FXT Data Analysis Software Package (FXTDAS;
Zhao et al. 2025). For timing analysis, we use the data collected
in the full 0.3–10 keV band of EP/FXT. However, for spectral
analysis, we restricted our analysis to the 0.5–10 keV band to
avoid calibration issues. EP/FXT consists of two identical mod-
ules, FXT-A and FXT-B. During the five observations, both mod-
ules were set in the Full Frame mode (FF), with a time resolution
of 50 ms. A circular source region with a radius of 60 ′′ and an
annular background region with inner and outer radii of 120 ′′
and 240′′ were adopted.
Appendix A.3: ASKAP
We requested a target of opportunity observation with ASKAP
(SBID 77513) to be observed quasi-simultaneously with the
XMM-NewtonDDT. We observed ASKAP J1745 for 8 hr at a
central frequency of 1365 MHz, with 144 MHz bandwidth3, be-
ginning at 02:58 UTC on October 5 2025. The data were cal-
ibrated with the standard ASKAP pipeline (see Hotan et al.
2021). We extracted model-subtracted dynamic spectra and
formed folded radio lightcurves using the DStoolspackage
(Pritchard 2025), following the procedure described in (Rose
et al. 2026).

the 5×10 4 realisations, which is consistent with the previously
determined significance of the signal. A similar separate search
on the EP/FXT data from observations FXT1, FXT2, and FXT3

EP/FXT light curve (Figure B.1), resulting in a windowing ef-

fined theXMM-Newtonbootstrapping value through epoch fold-
ing and phase-fitting with the EP/FXT data, obtaining a period
P=4868(22) s. The 1σuncertainty derived by phase-fitting is
0.2 s. The long baseline (approximately 20 days) between the
first EP/FXT observation and theXMM-Newtonobservation was
crucial to derive the phase-fitting solution. However, the associ-
ated error is most likely underestimated, dominated by the base-
line itself and not a reflection of the true uncertainty associated
with this measure. Therefore, we chose to retain the more con-
servative error derived from bootstrapping.
