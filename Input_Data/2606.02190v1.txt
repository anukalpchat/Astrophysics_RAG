Abstract
We report the detection of type-II X-ray bursts in a neutron star low mass X-ray binary MXB 1730-335 (also known as Rapid
Burster) observed with Insight-HXMTsatellite. We found significant variations in the burst properties along with the decay phases
of its 2017 and 2020 outbursts, such as switches between different burst modes. We investigated the statistical distributions of
burst parameters (peak fluxF peak, fluenceE, burst durationt dur, recurrence time∆t) and studied their correlations. We confirm the
relaxation oscillator behavior (E∝∆t α), and find that for mode-2 bursts the indexαis around 1. For mode-1 bursts,αvaries with
∆tand theE−∆t α relation can be described by a broken powerlaw model. We also confirm thatF peak is correlated withEbelow a
critical value and is independent of∆t.

1. Introduction
Low mass X-ray binaries (LMXBs) are celestial systems
composed of a compact object and a donor star with a mass≲
1 solar mass (M⊙). The compact star can be either a black hole
or a neutron star, dividing LMXBs into two categories. In such
systems, mass is transferred from the Roche-lobe-filling com-
panion star to an accretion disc, before subsequently accreting
onto the compact object. Numerous X-ray bursts have been ob-
served in neutron star LMXBs, characterized by a sudden and
significant increase in X-ray intensity. These bursts can be fur-
ther categorized into two types based on their energy sources
(for reviews see, e.g., Lewin et al., 1993; Cumming, 2004;
Strohmayer and Bildsten, 2006; Galloway and Keek, 2021): 1)
type-I X-ray bursts occur when matter accretes and accumulates
on the neutron star surface, triggering unstable thermonuclear
explosions; 2) type-II X-ray bursts probably originate from the
release of gravitational energy when the accreting matter sud-
denly falls onto the neutron star (Hoffman et al., 1978), al-
though there are still no widely accepted models that can fully

explain the behavior of type-II X-ray bursts. To date, type-
I X-ray bursts have been detected in more than 100 sources,
whereas only two sources, the Bursting Pulsar (BP, also known
as GRO J1744-28, Kouveliotou et al., 1996) and Rapid Burster
(RB, also known as MXB 1730-335, Lewin et al., 1976), exhibit
type-II X-ray bursts.
In this study, we focus on the Rapid Burster (hereafter RB),
a source situated in the globular cluster Liller 1 at a distance
of∼7.9 kpc (Valenti et al., 2010). Its name comes from the
repetitive bursting behavior (Masetti, 2002). Compared with
the Bursting Pulsar (BP), which presents only type-II bursts,
the RB displays both type-I and -II bursts. In some cases, type-I
bursts are predominant and no type-II bursts are observed when
the source is bright. Conversely, there are outburst states char-
acterized by the dominance of type-II bursts with only a few
type-I bursts. Type-II bursts show a variety of morphologi-
cal modes that may suddenly switch between each other: 1)
mode-0 is characterized by long, intense, and flattened bursts;
2) mode-1 features a series of short, rapidly recurring bursts,
followed by a relatively long and energetic burst at the end; and
3) mode-2 consists entirely of short, rapidly recurring bursts

(Guerriero et al., 1999; Marshall et al., 1979; Lewin et al., 1993).
After long bursts in mode-0 and mode-1, the persistent emis-
sion rises to a higher level before the next burst, forming the
so-called “hump" feature, which has never been observed fol-
lowing short bursts (Bagnoli et al., 2015). The most signifi-
cant property of type-II bursts in RB is theE−∆trelation (also
known as the relaxation oscillator relation), whereEis the burst
fluence and∆tis the recurrence time. This relationship im-
plies a mass storage mechanism, suggesting that after a stronger
burst, a longer recurrence period will be needed to trigger the
subsequent burst, due to the emptied reservoir that needs to be
refilled with mass. The specific triggering mechanism is still
unknown although several models have been proposed based
on magnetospheric and thermal instabilities (e.g., Lewin et al.,
1993; Spruit and Taam, 1993; Bagnoli et al., 2015). The broad-
band spectra of type-II bursts have been studied usingInsight-
HXMT observations performed in August 2017 (Chen et al.,
2021). In this study, using the same dataset and additional ob-
servations in 2020, we aim to investigate their statistical prop-
erties. This paper is structured as follows: In Sections 2, 3
and 4 we describe on the data analysis and present the results.
We compare with previous observations in Section 5.
2. Observation and data reduction
In this study, we studied twoInsight-HXMT datasets ob-
tained in 2017 and 2020. The detailed information is sum-
marized in Table 1. RB is a transient source exhibiting out-
bursts with a duration of several tens of days (Guerriero et al.,
1999). Figure 1 presents 15-50 keV long-term lightcurves mon-
itored bySwift/BAT, which indicates that our observations in
both 2017 and 2020 were conducted during the decay phase of
outbursts at different flux levels. This allows us to study burst
behaviors along with outburst evolutions at different accretion
rates. In the following, we define these observations as Epochs
A-J, and type-II bursts were only found in A-B and E-I.
Insight-HXMT is equipped with three slat-collimated in-
struments operating in the energy range of 1–250 keV (Zhang
et al., 2014, 2020), including the high energy telescope (HE;
20–250 keV), the medium energy telescope (ME; 5–30 keV)
and the low energy telescope (LE; 1–10 keV) (Liu et al., 2020;
Cao et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2020). Since bursts are more sig-
nificant in the low energy band, we used LE only in this paper.
The data reduction is conducted using the official software
(HXMTDAS1) v2.06 with the calibration database CALDB 2.07.
The good time intervals (GTI) were selected according to the
following criteria: (1) an elevation angle exceeding 8 ◦; (2) a
geomagnetic cutoffrigidity threshold>6 GV; and (3) exclu-
sion of South Atlantic Anomaly (SAA) passages to avoid in-
strumental noise contamination. Compared with the previous
study by Chen et al. (2021) who used the default GTI selection
(an elevation angle>10 ◦; a geomagnetic cutoffrigidity thresh-
old>8 GV and exclusion of additional 300 s of data before and
after the SAA passages), our filtering criteria are much looser.
This is due to the fact that our aim is to obtain a more complete
burst sample while the accurate estimation of the instrumental
background is not necessary. A side effect of non-standard GTI
filtering is that at passages close to SAA or the bright Earth
limb, some artificial flares can be produced by the high back-
ground at the start or end of consecutive GTI segments, which
appear similar to truncated bursts. Therefore, in this study, we
excluded all these suspicious flares and only studied bursts with
complete shapes. The total exposure was approximately 20 ks
and 60 ks for the observations in 2017 and 2020, respectively.
We note that there is another persistent and luminous source
4U 1728-34 (also known as the Slow Burster; SB), close to our
target with an angular separation of∆θ≈0.5 ◦. Because the
field of view (FoV) ofInsight-HXMT/LE is larger than∼1 ◦,
these two sources cannot be distinguished straightforwardly. In
practice, the on-axis source was 4U 1728-34 for the 2017 ob-
servations, and type-II bursts emitted from RB were detected
serendipitously (Chen et al., 2021). Fortunately, the LE con-
sists of collimators with three different orientations and FoVs
(see Figure 2). Two sources located at different positions within
each FoV are associated with different effective areas. This al-
lows us to reconstruct the temporal information for each source

3. Burst identification and classification
We first extracted background-subtracted lightcurves in the
energy range of 2-10 keV with a bin size of 1 s, where the back-
ground was estimated by using the toollebkgmap. We searched
for bursts according to the criterion that the burst peak flux
is significantly larger than the average persistent emission at
a confidence level of 5σ. The persistent emission was calcu-
lated as follows. For each GTI, we first calculated the mean
count rate of all data points, and then flagged points with count
rates exceeding this value at a 5σconfidence level as burst can-
didates. We excluded all flagged burst points, and repeated
the above steps until all burst-related data points had been re-
moved. The average count rate of the remaining points was

defined as the persistent level. We note that since the bin size of
lightcurves we used was 1 second, sub-second bursts might be
missed. We defined the burst starting (T start) and ending (T end)
times as the first and last points when the lightcurve reached
the persistent level within 1σerror. In this study, we focused
only on type-II bursts and excluded type-I bursts that can be
identified according to their typical fast-rising and exponential
decay profiles. Actually, five type-I bursts were found in the
dataset, and all of them originated from 4U 1728-34 instead of
the Rapid Burster. In total, 299 type-II bursts were identified in
2017, including 297 bursts that exhibited complete evolution-
ary profiles (i.e., with both rising and decay phases) and two
additional events that were incomplete due to interrupted ob-
servations. Using the same method, we observed 461 type-II
bursts in 2020, including 13 incomplete bursts. In 2017, only
regular short bursts (known as mode-2) were detected, whereas
in 2020, some long-duration bursts following a series of shorter
bursts (mode-1) appeared in Epoch E and F (see insets of Fig-
ure 1). As mentioned above, the persistent emission cannot
be accurately determined. This prevents us from distinguish-
ing between long and short bursts based on the presence of the
“hump" feature. In practice, we defined bursts that occurred at
the end of a burst sequence and had a longer duration as long
bursts.

4.1. Distributions of burst parameters
In Figures 4 and 5, we present the distributions of burst
properties for events detected in 2017 (Epochs A, B) and 2020
(Epochs E–I), respectively. Because the burst morphology changes
along with the outburst evolution, we show them as separate
samples with different colors. For bursts in 2017, the peak
flux distribution in Epoch B is centered around 120 cts/s, sig-
nificantly lower than those in Epoch A. For 2020 bursts, their
peak fluxes are comparable to those of 2017 bursts, and present
a consistent decreasing trend over time. We find that peak fluxes
of nearly all bursts are below the Eddington limit (the vertical
line in the top panel of Figure 5), with the exception of several
long bursts in Epoch E.
The burst fluence distributions also show clear differences
between epochs. For example, the distribution for Epoch B
peaks at 300 cts, while for Epoch A the fluence spans primar-
ily from 400 cts to 3000 cts (the middle panel of Figure 4). For
2020 bursts, those in Epochs E and F exhibit a much broader
fluence distribution than others, because they occurred in mode-
1 which have more diverse morphologies.
The burst duration distributions are shown in the third pan-
els of Figures 4 and 5. For the 2017 data, all burst durations in
Epochs A and B are shorter than 35 s, with a large fraction of
Epoch B bursts lasting less than 6 s. For the 2020 data, burst du-
rations in Epochs E and F (mode 1) span from a few seconds to
over 50 s, while durations in Epochs G–I (mode 2) are centered
around 12 s.
The burst recurrence time distributions are shown in bottom
panels of Figures 4 and 5. In 2017, recurrence times range from
20 s to 250 s, with samples in Epoch B significantly lower than
those in Epoch A. In 2020, recurrence times span a wider range
for those in Epochs E and F.
In summary, we find that from Epoch A to B bursts become
shorter in duration and less energetic but more frequent. For
bursts in 2020, those in Epochs E and F (mode 1) show signifi-
cant diversity in all parameters except the peak flux, while burst
properties in Epochs G–I (mode 2) are consistent with those ob-
served in Epochs A and B.

peak flux in 2017 increases with the fluence in the low fluence
range, while the increasing rate slows significantly onceF peak
reaches 137±9 cts/s. This suggests a saturation behavior for
the flux larger thanF peak, which corresponds to approximately
46% of the Eddington luminosity. We also studiedFpeak −Ere-
lations for 2020 bursts, and found that they could be described
by a powerlaw model without the need for a more complex BPL
model.

5. Discussion and Summary
We performed a thorough search for type-II bursts in Rapid
Burster withInsight-HXMT data observed in 2017 and 2020.
Compared with the previous report by Chen et al. (2021), we
applied looser GTI selection criteria with an advanced identifi-
cation method to optimize burst detections. In total, we iden-
tified 299 and 461 bursts in the decay phases of the 2017 and
2020 outbursts, respectively. Along with outbursts, these bursts
exhibit significant variations in burst properties, such as the
peak fluxF peak, fluenceEand recurrence time∆t. For instance,

5.1. Comparison with previous studies
Bagnoli et al. (2015) analyzed a large sample of bursts ob-
served withRXTE, and studied burst parameter distributions
and their mutual correlations. Our results are generally consis-
tent with their findings, including the positiveE−∆t,t dur −∆t,
tdur −Ecorrelations, as well as the absence of a significant cor-
relation betweenF peak and∆t.
The main improvement of this work is that we investigated
these relations in individual epochs at different outburst phases,
rather than combining all bursts as a whole sample. This allows
us to study subtle features without being affected by systematic
scatterings between different epochs. For instance, for mode-
1 bursts in Epochs E and F, theE−∆tandt dur −∆trelations
can be well fitted by a BPL model, significantly better than a
single power-law model at significance levels of 3σand 5σ,
respectively. Actually, such a break can be also hinted if one
carefully examines the large sample result (the middle panel
of Figure 8 in Bagnoli et al. (2015)). As shown in Figure 6,
most bursts with∆t>t b are classified as long bursts. This
suggests that there is some difference between long and short
bursts. In literature, the discrepancy between long and short
mode-1 bursts in theE−∆tdiagram was reported by Guerriero
et al. (1999). But, unlike our results, in their samples theE−∆t
relation was observed only for long bursts andEappeared to be
independent of∆tfor short bursts.
On the other hand, when comparing bursts obtained from
different Epochs, offsets ofE−∆trelations were found, as
shown in Figure 6. Similar offsets have been reported by Guer-
riero et al. (1999), interpreted as the factor that the energy re-
leased (or mass accreted) per unit time decays as the outburst
fades, which in turn leads to the increasing of the recurrence
time to accumulate enough matter for bursts. For 2017 bursts,
we did not find such an offset, which can be explained by there
are no significant changes in the accretion rate between epochs
A and B. This is plausible given the short gap (less than 2 days)
between the two epochs. In addition, in our sample the in-
dexαof mode-2 bursts in different epochs is always approx-
imately 1, which is distinct from previous report by Guerriero
et al. (1999), who found that theαvalue varied in the range
0.43–0.94. The underlying reason for theαvariation remains
unclear.
For 2017 bursts, we find thatF peak increases steeply withE
until it reaches a critical value corresponding to a luminosity of
46% Eddington luminosity. Bagnoli et al. (2015) also reported
a saturation behavior ofF peak, but this effect occurred near the
Eddington luminosity. This discrepancy may partially origi-
nate from the systematic uncertainty introduced by the assumed
broadband spectral models. However, as mentioned above, as-
suming different models would only introduce a systematic er-
ror of 15%, which is not enough to account for the discrepancy.
Therefore, we speculate that this discrepancy is more likely to
reflect the intrinsic scattering of the saturation flux obtained
from different observations. For example, the saturation value
discovered by Bagnoli et al. (2015) was primarily dominated by
mode-1 bursts, whereas the bursts we studied in 2017 were of
mode-2, which usually exhibit lowerF peak values.
5.2. F ormation mechanism of the E−∆t relation
To date, the trapped-disc model is the most widely accepted
physical model for type-II bursts from the RB. In this scenario,
the neutron star’s magnetic field truncates the accretion disc at
the magnetospheric radiusrm, which lies outside the co-rotational
radiusr co. As accreted matter piles up in the inner disc region,
the enhanced gas pressure drives ther m inwards. Once the in-
ner disc radius crosses ther co, a rapid accretion episode be-
gins. During this episode, a large amount of mass falls onto
the NS surface suddenly, releasing gravitational potential en-
ergy and producing a burst (D’Angelo and Spruit, 2010). This
theoretical framework is supported by observations in many as-
pects. For example, Bagnoli and in’t Zand (2015) suggested
that type-II bursts are likely linked to accretion disc instabili-
ties. Guerriero et al. (1999) reported that type-II bursts only ap-
pear at luminosities below 0.1L Edd, corresponding to low mass
accretion rates during the trapped-disc phase. van den Eijnden
et al. (2017) measured the inner disc radius of the RB to be
∼40 gravitational radii, consistent with the truncated disc sce-

burst (i.e., the more mass accreted), the longer time is required
for the inner disc to accumulate sufficient mass to overcome
the magnetospheric barrier and trigger the next burst (D’Angelo
and Spruit, 2010). For mode-1 bursts in Epochs E and F, we find
that a BPL model is required to describe theE-∆trelation, and
bursts with higher fluence (long bursts) have a lower powerlaw
index compared to bursts with lower fluence (short bursts). In
other words, to trigger a subsequent burst at a given fluence, the
system requires a longer accumulation period after long bursts
as compared to short bursts. This may suggest that long bursts
would result in a greater depletion of the trapped-disc so that a
longer time is required to rebuild the pile-up region. In addition,
considering long bursts have district profiles from short bursts,
their occurrence might suggest changes of the inner disc, such
as variations in the thickness or temperature. If this is the case,
a change in theE–∆trelation would be expected.
