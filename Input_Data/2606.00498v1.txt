Abstract.Constraining the cosmic neutrino background (CνB) represents a major experi-
mental challenge in cosmology. Recent studies have suggested that relic neutrinos boosted
by ultra-high-energy cosmic rays (UHECRs) may generate observable diffuse neutrino fluxes.
Previous estimates have not effectively propagated the primary cosmic rays, often neglect-
ing crucial energy losses and the unavoidable, competing interactions with diffuse photon
backgrounds. Here we revisit these expectations using a realistic Monte Carlo propagation
framework. This approach allows us to consistently incorporate cosmic ray energy losses,
nuclear photodisintegration, and production of secondary neutrinos. We show that inter-
actions with diffuse photon backgrounds strongly suppress the boosted relic neutrino flux
predicted in simplified propagation scenarios. Furthermore, we demonstrate that to produce
any observable suppression on the UHECR energy spectrum at Earth, or for the boosted
CνB component to become comparable to the cosmogenic neutrino flux, the CνB density
must be enhanced by a factor, the so-called overdensity, of extreme magnitude (η≳10 8).

1 Introduction
Neutrinos are the second most abundant particles in the Universe. Due to their small in-
teraction cross sections, they decoupled during the early stages of the Universe, prior to the
decoupling of the cosmic microwave background (CMB), forming what is known as the cosmic
neutrino background (CνB) [1]. Despite a theoretically predicted average number density of
n0 = 336 cm−3 [2], their extremely low energy in the laboratory frame makes direct detection
quite challenging [3, 4]. In extended cosmological scenarios, dark matter [5] or even dark en-
ergy [6, 7] can produce neutrinos at later epochs, increasing the number of diffuse neutrinos
and leading to what is known as an overdensity. The most stringent experimental constraint
on the local overdensity, set by the KATRIN experiment, isη≲10 11 [8].
Ultra-high-energy cosmic rays (UHECRs), charged particles with energies above 1018 eV,
can interact with both photon and neutrino backgrounds. This provides an opportunity for
neutrino observatories to probe the CνB through the detection of high-energy relic neutrinos
up-scattered by cosmic rays. Recently, several studies have returned to explore this possi-
bility. Regarding the diffuse component of up-scattered neutrinos with massm ν, Ref. [9]
found that for an overdensity ofη= 150, the flux of neutrinos up-scattered solely by protons
could be detected by future experiments. Furthermore, Ref. [10] established a constraint of
η≲10 7 form ν = 0.1 eV by incorporating a mixed mass composition. Other studies, such
as Refs. [11, 12], have focused on scattering within prominent astrophysical sources, such
as the blazar TXS 0506+056 [13] and galaxy clusters. Generally, the methodology used to
calculate the expected flux of boosted relic neutrinos relies on line-of-sight integrals. While
this formalism is robust, it makes it difficult to fully account for cosmic-ray energy losses
resulting from CνB scattering, as well as interactions with the photon backgrounds, which
have long been known to play an important role in UHECR propagation [14, 15]. Simpli-
fied approaches overestimate boosted relic neutrino fluxes because they do not fully account
for realistic propagation losses and competing photon-background interactions. Furthermore,
properly estimating the UHECR flux in the extragalactic medium remains a major challenge.

Consequently, studies focusing on the diffuse component usually rely on an extrapolation of
the energy spectrum measured at Earth by the Pierre Auger Observatory or the Telescope
Array.
In this work, we present a propagation-based Monte-Carlo framework for calculating
the expected flux of boosted CνB neutrinos at Earth using Monte-Carlo methods. This is
achieved by implementing neutral-current neutrino scattering for nucleons and nuclei into
CRPropa3.2.1, a well-established framework for astroparticle propagation [16]. This imple-
mentation allows us to effectively propagate cosmic rays and neutrinos while taking into ac-
count interactions with photon backgrounds, henceforth referred to asγBG. It enables us to
rigorously test different mass compositions, including nuclear photodisintegration and decay
cascades. The production of secondary neutrinos through CνB scattering, interactions with
theγBG, and nuclear decays is naturally simulated, allowing a direct comparison between
the expected neutrino fluxes at Earth from these three different channels. To our knowledge,
this work presents the first Monte-Carlo implementation of relic-neutrino scattering during
UHECR propagation within CRPropa.
Despite several proposed models, the mechanisms behind ultra-high-energy cosmic ray
acceleration and injection remain uncertain. Rather than extrapolating the measured energy
spectrum, our computations are based directly on UHECR source models. First, we consider
two representative idealized scenarios, assuming pure-proton and pure-iron compositions,
characterized by a power-law energy spectrum with an exponential cut-off. We then discuss
the impact of the maximum rigidity and the source spectral index on the flux at Earth.
Consequently, we identify the source conditions under which the boosted CνB contribution
becomes phenomenologically relevant. Finally, using the astrophysical model and combined
fit parameters from the Pierre Auger Observatory [17], we present the expected flux for
different overdensity factors. We also show that effects on the ultra-high-energy cosmic ray
flux, as well as on the Greisen-Zatsepin-Kuzmin (GZK) neutrino flux, are expected only for
overdensities well above standard cosmological expectations,η≳10 8.
Our goal is therefore to reassess previous expectations for boosted relic neutrino fluxes
under a realistic UHECR propagation framework that consistently accounts for competing
photon-background interactions and continuous energy losses.
This paper is organized as follows: in Section 2, we briefly describe the canonical
nucleus-photon interactions and decays during cosmic-ray propagation. We also discuss the
two channels for CνB scattering considered in this work and how we implemented them in
CRPropa3.2.1. Section 3 details our simulation setup and discusses the astrophysical models
adopted. Our results are presented in Section 4, and we summarize our conclusions in Section
5.
2 Interactions in the extragalactic medium
After leaving their acceleration sites, the most energetic cosmic rays can interact with diffuse
target particles in the extragalactic medium. The three fundamental processes that play
a major role in UHECR propagation are photopair production, photopion production, and
photodisintegration.
The photopair reaction (A+γ→A+e + +e −) is a particular case of Bethe–Heitler pair
production in the Coulomb field of a charged cosmic ray [18]. Since we are not interested in
the development of electromagnetic cascades, this interaction acts as a low-inelasticity energy
– 2 –

loss mechanism (K∼10 −3) [19]. Consequently, it is usually treated as a continuous energy
loss in most propagation codes [16, 20].
Photodisintegration is the process by which cosmic-ray nuclei interact with theγBG and
fragment into lighter nuclei and free nucleons. Because this process can produce unstable
nuclei, decay chains must also be taken into account during propagation. These interactions
can severely alter the mass composition injected by the sources before it is observed at Earth.
Distinct physical channels are dominant depending on the energy scale. In the cosmic-ray rest
frame, the giant dipole resonance (GDR) dominates at photon energies ofε ′
γ ≲30–50 MeV,
while quasi-deuteron (QD) emission takes over at energies of 50≲ε ′
γ ≲150 MeV [21, 22].
The precise values of the photodisintegration cross sections remain a significant source of
uncertainty in UHECR propagation models [17, 23, 24].
2.1 Neutrinos from interactions withγBG and decays
Soon after the discovery of the CMB, it was proposed that the production of pions from
interactions between protons and these diffuse photons would yield a suppression in the
UHECR flux at Earth, the so-called GZK cutoff [14, 15]. In the multimessenger era, this
remains a prominent interaction channel. Pions carry∼20% of the parent nucleon’s energy
and subsequently decay. Neutral pions decay into gamma rays (π 0 →2γ), each carrying
∼10% of the initial energy, while charged pions decay into neutrinos and positrons (π + →
e+ +ν e + ¯νµ +ν µ), with each lepton taking∼5% of the initial nucleon energy [25].
Additionally, free neutrons produced as secondaries in photodisintegration or photopion
production will undergoβ-decay (n→p+e −+¯νe). The resulting electron antineutrinos carry
∼0.01–0.04% of the parent neutron energy [19, 26]. The dominance of the GDR channel
for cosmic-ray nuclei makes it difficult for heavy mass compositions injected at the source to
produce more neutrinos than a pure-proton injection [27].

3.1 Propagation setup
The sources were simulated as a 1D shell-like structure in the absence of extragalactic mag-
netic fields. The minimum distance considered wasD min = 1 Mpc. Since expected neutrino
flux from identical and isotropic nearby sources is negligible compared to that from more
distant sources, varying the minimum distance does not change our results. Conversely,
adopting an overly large maximum distance can lead to a significant overestimation of the
expected neutrino fluxes [32]. Since there is no definitive consensus, we adoptedz max ∼6,
following recent studies on boosted neutrinos [9, 33]. We simulated an equal distribution of
the representative nuclear species 1H, 4He, 14N, 28Si, and 56Fe. The energy losses included in
the propagation were photopair production, photopion production, and photodisintegration,
considering the CMB and the Gilmore’s et. al. [34] model for the EBL target photon fields.
Adiabatic energy losses and nuclear decays were also taken into account.
For each simulation, we considered the sum of the two channels explained in Section 2
for cosmic-ray CνB scattering, given an overdensity factorηand a neutrino massm ν. For
simplicity, we fixed the neutrino mass tom ν = 0.1 eV while varyingηfrom 10 2 to 1010. The
behavior of expected boosted CνB component in neutrino flux was already explored [10].
The present work neglects extragalactic magnetic fields and source intermittency, which
could further modify the expected flux normalization.

The panel arrangement in Fig. 2 clearly illustrates distinct physical regimes. In the top
row (η= 10 2–104), interactions with theγBG completely dominate the total flux. For the
pure-iron scenario, nuclear decays also provide a significant contribution to the low-energy tail
of the spectrum. The middle row (η= 10 5–107) exhibits a clear transition regime, where the
CνB contribution becomes increasingly competitive with the standard photon-background
channels. At this point, it is crucial to address the impact ofR cut. ForR cut = 1019 V, the
CνB up-scattering component becomes comparable to the high-energy cosmogenic neutrino
flux aroundη∼10 7. For lower rigidities, this transition overdensity tends to decrease. This
occurs because the production of cosmogenic neutrinos via theγBG is heavily constrained by
the maximum energy attained at the source, owing to the high energy threshold of the GZK
processes. Consequently, the further the maximum rigidity falls below the GZK threshold, the
lower the overdensity factor required for the boosted CνB component to become comparable
to the cosmogenic neutrino background. The last row (η= 10 8–1010) illustrates the regime
in which the boosted CνB component becomes phenomenologically competitive with the
photon-background and decay channels. Notably, at extremely large overdensity factors
(η≳10 9), the mean free path for CνB scattering becomes so short that it actively modifies
the expected neutrino flux from theγBG.
The effects of the source spectral index,γ, and the cosmological evolution of the source
emissivity,H(z), primarily act as overall scaling factors for the expected neutrino flux. Gen-
erally, harder injection spectra (i.e., smaller values ofγ) increase the fraction of high-energy
particles at the source, thereby enhancing the resulting neutrino flux. Conversely, softer
spectra yield the opposite effect. The cosmological evolution of the sources follows a sim-
ilar trend, albeit with an important caveat. Because CνB scattering for low overdensity
factors only becomes probable at high redshifts, source classes characterized by strong local
emissivity and weak high-redshift evolution will yield a suppressed boosted CνB component.
Consequently, in such scenarios, the effects of theγBG will even more strongly dominate

In agreement with past studies [10, 30], we find that extreme CνB overdensity values

our Monte-Carlo approach inherently tracks all energy losses during propagation, it allows us
to explicitly compute the impact of CνB scattering on the final UHECR spectrum, as shown

Since the high-energy tail of the UHECR spectrum is predominantly governed by local
sources (z <1), the spectral modifications begin to manifest at energies just below the ankle.
These suppressions become drastic when the rate of UHECR interactions with relic neutrinos
becomes comparable to that of interactions with theγBG (η≳10 9). It is worth noting,
however, that such extreme overdensity factors are already excluded by the diffuse neutrino
flux limits discussed. Quantitative thresholds on the overdensity factor remain subject to
uncertainties associated with source evolution, EBL modeling, and nuclear interaction cross
sections.
5 Conclusion
In this work, we reassessed previous expectations for boosted relic neutrino fluxes generated
during ultra-high-energy cosmic ray propagation. Simplified approaches overestimate boosted
relic neutrino fluxes because they do not fully account for realistic propagation losses and com-
peting photon-background interactions. We improved upon previous analytical estimates by

Our results demonstrate that the interactions with theγBG heavily suppress the ex-
pected flux of boosted CνB neutrinos. Using the realistic mixed-composition astrophysical
model derived from the Pierre Auger Observatory combined fit, we found that only substan-
tial overdensities yield boosted CνB fluxes potentially accessible to next-generation observa-
tories. Specifically, overdensity factors ofη≳10 7–108 are required for the up-scattered CνB
component to overcome the cosmogenic neutrino background and reach the projected 10-
year sensitivities of next-generation observatories such as GRAND200k and IceCube-Gen2.
Importantly, we showed that the boosted CνB component and the standardγBG channels
exhibit markedly different spectral shapes, providing a potentially useful spectral signature
for future phenomenological discrimination studies.
Furthermore, our simulation explicitly revealed that only extreme overdensity factors
significantly modify the primary UHECR spectrum. Forη≳10 9, the UHECR scattering rate
with relic neutrinos becomes comparable to canonicalγBG interactions, inducing a prominent
suppression in the cosmic ray spectrum at energies just below the ankle. However, our coupled
analysis demonstrates that such extreme values are already strongly disfavored within the

Our results indicate that the suppression of boosted relic neutrino fluxes emerges nat-
urally from the hierarchy between photon-background and relic-neutrino interaction proba-
bilities during realistic UHECR propagation. The suppression is not tied to a specific astro-
physical scenario, but emerges naturally from the hierarchy between photon-background and
relic-neutrino interaction probabilities during UHECR propagation.
Ultimately, while next-generation neutrino observatories may constrain extreme over-
densities, accurately accounting for the dominant propagation effects is essential to avoid
significantly overestimating the expected boosted relic neutrino signal. The dominance of
photon-background interactions over relic-neutrino scattering emerges naturally from the
relative interaction scales governing realistic UHECR propagation.
