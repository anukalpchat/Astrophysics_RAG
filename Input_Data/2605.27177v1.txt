Abstract
The study of complex nitrogen (N)-bearing molecules is essential for probing the physical and chemical evo-
lution of star-forming regions. In this paper, we present the identification of rotational emission lines from
several complex N-bearing species such as methyl cyanide (CH 3CN), ethyl cyanide (C 2H5CN), vinyl cyanide
(C2H3CN), cyanamide (NH 2CN), and formamide (NH 2CHO) toward the high-mass protostar S255IR NIRS3
using ALMA band 4 observations. In addition, the vibrationally excited transitions of cyanoacetylene (HC 3N,
ν7 = 2) were detected. The column densities and excitation temperatures of these molecules were derived
through LTE spectral modelling, yielding excitation temperatures in the range of 175–220 K. The high exci-
tation temperatures (175–220 K) indicate that the identified N-bearing molecules arise from the warm inner
regions (T≥100 K) of the source. The fractional abundances were further estimated relative to H 2, CH3OH,
and CH3CN. A Pearson correlation heat map of the abundances reveals a strong positive correlation (r>0.7)
among three molecules in the cyanide family, such as CH 3CN, C2H3CN, and C 2H5CN, suggesting that these
N-bearing molecules may be chemically linked. Comparison with three-phase warm-up chemical models shows
that the observed abundances of CH 3CN, C2H5CN, C2H3CN, NH2CN, NH2CHO, and HC3N (ν7 = 2) relative
to H2 are consistent with model predictions within factors of 1.04, 0.67, 1.28, 0.76, 0.72, and 0.96, respectively.
Finally, we discuss the potential formation pathways of the identified N-bearing molecules in the context of
gas-grain chemistry within S255IR NIRS3.

1. Introduction
In the interstellar medium (ISM) or circumstellar shells,
more than 340 molecular species were detected at
(sub)millimeter wavelengths 5. Among the 340 molecular
species, approximately 96 molecules have at least one nitro-
gen (N) atom, 12 molecules contain isocyanide (–NC), and
33 molecules have a functional group of cyanide (–CN). CN
was the first detected N-bearing molecule observed in 1940
towards different stars (McKellar 1940). CN plays an impor-
tant role in the formation of cyanopolynes (HC2n+1N), hydro-
carbons (C nH2n+2), and several prebiotic molecules (Herbst
& van Dishoeck 2009; Taniguchi et al. 2019; Jørgensen et
al. 2020; Taniguchi et al. 2022). The study of N-bearing
molecules with CN bonds is crucial because CN bonds form
between the – NH 2 and –COOH groups, creating different
types of amino acids (Goldman et al. 2010). Previous stud-
ies show that N-bearing molecules are essential for the pro-

duction of different types of complex biomolecules (Balu-

and N-ion-bearing molecules have been detected across di-
verse Galactic environments, including high- and low-mass
star-forming regions, comets, protoplanetary disks, and Sat-
urn’s moon Titan (Johnson et al. 1977; Irvine & Schloerb
1984; Kawaguchi et al. 1992; Ziurys et al. 1999; Cordiner
et al. 2015; Öberg et al. 2015; Manna et al. 2024; Ziurys &
Turner 1986; Kawaguchi et al. 1994; Thaddeus et al. 2008;
Cernicharo et al. 2008, 2013; Marcelino et al. 2018; Agúndez
et al. 2015, 2010).
In the ISM, complex organic molecules form efficiently on
dust grain surfaces, which act as catalytic sites for chemi-
cal reactions (Garrod & Herbst 2006). Nitriles heavier than
HCN are classified as interstellar complex organic molecules
(iCOMs), defined as species containing at least six atoms
(Herbst & van Dishoeck 2009; Ceccarelli et al. 2017). Their
formation and detectability are closely linked to the rise in
temperature and density during star formation, particularly
in high-mass environments. The formation of high-mass
stars (M≥8M ⊙) within dense clusters provides a key frame-
work for chemical enrichment, offering insight into the as-

trochemical processes that may have preceded the formation
of our Solar System (Carpenter 2000; Lada & Lada 2003;
Rivilla et al. 2013; Adams 2010). Based on observational
results, the earliest phase can be characterized by a high-
mass starless core (HMSC), which is dense (n≥10 5 cm−3),
cold (T∼15−20 K), and massive molecular condensation,
but there is no evidence of star formation activity due to the
gravitational instability (Tan et al. 2013). The subsequent
evolutionary phase, often associated with high-mass proto-
stellar objects (HMPOs), is thought to mark the onset of pro-
tostar formation within hot molecular cores (HMCs), charac-
terized by temperaturesT≥100 K and gas densitiesn≥10 7
cm−3 (Kurtz et al. 2000; Fontani et al. 2007; Williams &
Viti 2014). High-mass protostellar cores have been identi-
fied observationally (see Olguin et al. 2023, and references
therein) and serve as important test cases for high-mass star
formation (HMSF) theories. However, the existence and life-
time of this evolutionary phase are not yet firmly established
and remain subjects of active investigation. AtT≥100 K,
molecules formed within icy grain mantles are released into
the gas phase via thermal desorption or sputtering by shocks,
UV photons, and cosmic rays (Garrod & Herbst 2006; Gar-
rod 2013). Once in the gas phase, these species undergo
further reactions, producing more complex molecules such
as CH 3OH, CH 3OCHO, CH 3OCH3, and C 2H5OH (Herbst
& van Dishoeck 2009). Several chemical models suggest
that the lifetime of HMCs is on the order of∼10 5 yr for
a medium warm-up phase and up to∼10 6 yr for a slow
warm-up phase (Garrod & Herbst 2006; Garrod 2013). In
the warm-up phase, complex N-bearing iCOMs, including
methyl cyanide (CH 3CN), vinyl cyanide (C 2H3CN), ethyl
cyanide (C 2H5CN), and cyanoacetylene (HC 3N), become
prominent spectral features (Garrod 2013). Using three-
phase chemical modelling, Garrod et al. (2017) demonstrated
that HC 3N acts as a key precursor of both C 2H3CN and
C2H5CN on grain surfaces in hot cores. These three species
are consistently detected in hot cores, hot corinos, and even in
the atmosphere of Saturn’s moon Titan (see Table A1). Their
detection in both Galactic star-forming regions and Titan’s
atmosphere suggests that these molecules may trace chemi-
cal pathways linking interstellar material to planetary atmo-
spheres, highlighting their relevance across different stages
of star and planet formation (Palmer et al. 2017; Iino et al.
2020). Similarly, in the ISM, amide-bearing molecules such
as cyanamide (NH2CN) and formamide (NH2CHO) are rec-
ognized as important prebiotic species and have been identi-
fied in several Galactic sources (see Table A1). Using gas-
grain chemical networks, Manna & Pal (2024a) proposed
that NH2CN may act as a potential precursor of the simplest
amino acid, glycine (NH 2CH2COOH), through both grain-
surface and gas-phase pathways, highlighting its role in link-
ing interstellar chemistry with molecular inventories relevant
to the origins of life.

identified rotational emission lines of numerous oxygen (O)-
and sulphur (S)-bearing molecules toward NIRS3, but placed
little emphasis on complex N-bearing species. Therefore,
NIRS3 is an excellent target for detailed studies of both
the physical properties and chemical evolution of N-bearing
molecules. NIRS3 is situated in the S255IR star-formation
region at a distance of 1.78 kpc (Burns et al. 2016). The
mass and bolometric luminosity of NIRS3 are∼20 M ⊙ and
∼2.4×104 L⊙, respectively (Wang et al. 2011; Zinchenko et
al. 2015). Previous observations have shown that the molec-

emissions of water (H 2O) and methanol (CH 3OH) were de-
tected towards NIRS3 (Hirota et al. 2021; Baek et al. 2023).
The molecular outflows of carbon monoxide (CO), silicon
monoxide (SiO), and carbon monosulfide (CS) were also
found in NIRS3 (Zinchenko et al. 2015).

2. Observations and data reductions
We used the archival data of NIRS3, observed using a high-
resolution Atacama Large Millimeter/Submillimeter Array
(ALMA) (PI: Cesaroni, Riccardo, ID: 2016.A.00008.T) with
the 12 m array. This observation was carried out on De-
cember 14, 2016 (MJD 57736) as part of an ALMA Di-
rector’s Discretionary Time (DDT) program aimed at mon-
itoring the free–free continuum emission associated with the
accretion burst in NIRS3. The primary objective of these
observations was to investigate the connection between ac-
cretion and jet ejection processes. The on-source integra-
tion time was 5.04 minutes, which is relatively short, and
the observations were not originally optimized for a de-
tailed astrochemical study. However, the available spectral
coverage enables the detection of several molecular tran-
sitions, which we exploit here to investigate the chemical
properties of the source. The phase centre of NIRS3 was
(α, δ)J2000 = 06:12:54.020, +17:59:23.100. For this obser-
vation, J0510+1800 was used as both a bandpass and flux
calibrator. In addition, J0613+1708 was used as the phase
calibrator. During the observation, 45 antennas were used
with a minimum baseline of 25 m and a maximum baseline of
400 m. Observations were performed in the frequency ranges
of 127.61–129.49 GHz, 129.56–131.44 GHz, 139.67–141.54
GHz, and 141.56–143.43 GHz, with spectral and velocity
resolutions of 976.56 kHz and 2.06 km s−1, respectively.
We used the Common Astronomy Software Application
(CASA 5.4.1) with the ALMA data analysis pipeline for cal-
ibration and imaging of the data (McMullin et al. 2007).
We applied the Perley-Butler 2017 flux calibration model
for each baseline for flux calibration using the SETJY task
(Perley & Butler 2017). We also used the pipeline tasks
HIFA_bandpassflag and HIFA_flagdata for flagging bad an-
tenna data and channels, which were performed after the flux
and bandpass calibration. After preliminary data reduction,
we separated the target data (NIRS3) using the MSTRANS-
FORM task with all the available rest frequencies. We also
used the UVCONTSUB task to subtract continuum emission
from the UV plane of the calibrated data. We created the
continuum emission map of NIRS3 using the TCLEAN task
with a HOGBOM deconvolver for line-free channels. We
also generated spectral data cubes using the TCLEAN task
with the cube spectral definition mode (SPECMODE) with
a Briggs weighting of 0.5. Several rounds of self-calibration
were applied using the GAINCAL and APPLYCAL tasks to
reduce the RMS. We applied the task IMPBCOR to correct
the primary beam pattern in the continuum images and spec-
tral data cubes.
3. Result
3.1. Dust continuum emission towards NIRS3
We generated the dust continuum images of NIRS3 for
line-free channels at frequencies centering on 128.82 GHz (λ
= 2.32 mm), 130.56 GHz (λ= 2.29 mm), 141.07 GHz (λ=
2.12 mm), and 142.49 GHz (λ= 2.10 mm), respectively. The
dust continuum image of NIRS3 at a wavelength of 2.10 mm
is shown in Figure 1. To estimate the physical parameters
of the continuum emission images, we fitted a 2-D Gaussian
using the IMFIT task over the main core. The physical prop-
erties of the NIRS3 core are shown in Table 1. We found that
the deconvolved source sizes are larger than the correspond-
ing synthesized beam sizes by a factor of∼1.05–1.10 along
both the major and minor axis across all four frequencies,
indicating that the emission is marginally resolved over the
observable frequency ranges of band 4.

Model E is statistically preferred over the other four mod-
els. It is favored by∆AIC = 9.3 relative to the next best
model (Double Plummer), while the remaining models show
∆AIC>10, indicating little or no empirical support. The
residuals show minimal systematic deviations across the ra-
dial range, with a reducedχ 2 = 13.64, confirming that Model
E provides the most robust representation of the observed
profile among the five models considered. The reducedχ 2
values follow a similar trend, decreasing from 20.23 for
Model A to 13.64 for Model E, confirming the progressive
improvement in fit quality with increasing model complex-
ity. However, it is worth noting that even the best-fitting
model has reducedχ 2 >1, suggesting either that the uncer-
tainties may be slightly underestimated or that there is ad-
ditional small-scale structure not captured by any of these
analytical models.

3.2. Line emission towards NIRS3
We extracted the molecular spectra from the spectral data
cubes using a circular region with a diameter of 2.32 ′′ (aver-
age emitting size) centered on the NIRS3 core. Note that
the synthesized beam sizes of the spectral data cubes of
NIRS3 are 2.30 ′′×1.28′′, 2.29 ′′×1.28′′, 2.07 ′′×1.19′′, and
2.13′′×1.18′′ at frequency ranges of 127.61–129.49 GHz,
129.56–131.44 GHz, 139.67–141.54 GHz, and 141.56–
143.43 GHz, respectively. The extracted molecular spectra of
NIRS3 are shown in Figure 5. The systemic velocity (V LSR)
of the molecular spectra of NIRS3 is 5.0 km s −1 (Liu et al.
2020). After spectral extraction, we used CASSIS software
for molecular identification and spectral analysis using the
local thermodynamic equilibrium (LTE) model with an opti-
cally thin assumption with the JPL, CDMS, and LSD molec-
ular databases (Vastel et al. 2015; Pickett et al. 1998; Müller
et al. 2005; Motiyenko & Margulès 2025). The assumption of
LTE is valid in the inner region of NIRS3, where the gas den-
sity is approximately 3×10 7 cm−3 (Szymczak et al. 2018),
comparable to the critical density of dense gas tracers such
as C34S(J= 7-6) (∼10 7 cm−3) (see Zinchenko et al. (2020)).
Moreover, previous studies have shown that the molecular
lines detected toward NIRS3 are optically thin (Liu et al.
2020; Baek et al. 2023), indicating that the molecular en-
ergy levels are effectively thermalized under LTE conditions.
Following the spectral analysis, we detected six individual
N-bearing molecules, such as CH 3CN, C 2H3CN, C 2H5CN,
NH2CN (bothν= 0 and 1), NH 2CHO, and HC 3N (ν7 = 2),
respectively. Additionally, we detected several O-bearing
molecules, including CH3OH, toward NIRS3, and a detailed
physical and chemical analysis of these species will be pre-
sented in a follow-up study.

3.2.1.Methyl cyanide (CH 3CN)
Methyl cyanide (CH 3CN) is a well-known, strongly pro-
late, symmetric top molecule. The rotational energy levels
(orJtransitions) of CH 3CN are divided into successiveK
components. The emission lines of CH3CN are shifted in fre-
quency with respect to each other due to differentKvalues;
this is known as the ‘Kladder’ spectrum. Due to itsKladder,
the CH3CN molecule can determine the proper temperature
of interstellar gas and is hence known as a gas thermome-
ter for interstellar gas. From the spectra of NIRS3, we have
detected the rotational emission lines of CH 3CN with tran-
sitionJ= 7–6 withK= 0–6. A list of molecular lines and
their spectroscopic parameters for CH 3CN was taken from
CDMS based on Müller et al. (2009). Previously, Wang et
al. (2011) and Liu et al. (2020) detected the emission lines
of CH 3CN from NIRS3, and this is a re-detection of this
molecule in this source using the ALMA band 4. Based on
LTE spectral fitting, the column density, excitation temper-
ature, and FWHM of CH 3CN are (1.5±0.7)×10 15 cm−2,
180±26 K, and 5.5±0.4 km s −1. The fractional abundance
of CH3CN with respect to H2 is (1.4±0.7)×10 −9. Similarly,
the fractional abundance of CH 3CN with respect to CH 3OH
is (1.6±0.8)×10 −2. After the detection of emission lines
of CH 3CN (ν= 0), we also searched the emission lines of
CH3CN (ν8 = 1). After spectral analysis, we notice that all
detected emission lines of CH3CN (ν8 = 1) are blended with
different O-bearing molecules. Assuming an excitation tem-
perature of 180 K, the upper-limit column density of CH3CN
(ν8 = 1) is≤(2.4±0.2)×10 14 cm−2.
3.2.2.Ethyl cyanide (C 2H5CN)
Ethyl cyanide (C 2H5CN) is one of the known asymmet-
ric top molecules whose dipole moments areµ a = 3.85 de-
bye andµ a = 1.23 debye (Heise et al. 1974). After spec-
tral analysis, we detected a total of 13 transition lines of
C2H5CN towards NIRS3. To our knowledge, this is the first
detection of this molecule in NIRS3. The molecular line
lists and laboratory spectroscopic parameters for C 2H5CN
are taken from CDMS based mainly on Brauer et al. (2009).
We find that the column density, excitation temperature, and
FWHM of C 2H5CN are (3.8±1.2)×10 14 cm−2, 178±35
K, and 5.5±0.8 km s −1, respectively. The fractional abun-
dance of C 2H5CN with respect to H 2 is (3.5±1.3)×10 −10.
Similarly, the fractional abundances of C 2H5CN with re-
spect to CH 3OH and CH 3CN are (4.0±1.3)×10 −3 and
(2.5±1.4)×10 −1.

3.4. Validation of estimated abundance relative to H2
The column density of H 2 is estimated from the dust con-
tinuum emissions, and the beam sizes of the continuum and
line images are the same. We noticed that the emitting re-
gions of some molecules are the same as the line-emitting
regions of the dust continuum. For example, the emitting
region of the dust continuum at frequency 141.07 GHz is
2.28′′ (see Table 1), and the emitting regions of C 2H5CN,
C2H3CN, and HC3N with transitionJ= 16(7,10)–15(7,9),J
= 15(6,10)—14(6,9), andJ= 14–13,l= 2f are 2.28 ′′ (see Ta-
ble 7). Therefore, we can say that H2 and some transitions of
different molecules are emitted in the same regions. So, the
estimation of the abundance relative to H 2 is valid for this
study.
4. Discussion
4.1. Molecular correlation with other sources
In this section, we compare the abundances of the de-
tected N-bearing molecules relative to CH 3OH and CH 3CN
between NIRS3 and other sources. We also examine the

20
molecular correlations of the abundances relative to CH3OH
and excitation temperature using Pearson correlation coeffi-
cient heat maps. For the comparison of chemical abundances,
we used only those hot core and hot corino sources where
the emission lines of CH 3CN, C 2H3CN, C 2H5CN, HC 3N,
NH2CN, and NH2CHO were detected. In this study, we did
not use the abundance of N-bearing molecules relative to
H2 because of the challenges in accurately determining the
column density of H 2 from the dust continuum or the CO
column density. Instead, we present the abundance of the
detected N-bearing species relative to CH 3OH and CH 3CN.
We compare the abundances of detected N-bearing species in
NIRS3 with several hot core and hot corino sources, such as
Sgr B2 (N) (Belloche et al. 2013), Sgr B2 (M) (Belloche et
al. 2013, 2016), Orion KL (Friedel & Snyder 2008; López et
al. 2014; Motiyenko et al. 2012; Bell et al. 2014; Peng et al.
2017; Pagani et al. 2017), G31.41+0.31 (Mininni et al. 2023;
Wyrowski et al. 1999; Manna & Pal 2024b), G10.47+0.03
(Rolffs et al. 2011; Hernández-Hernández et al. 2014), IRAS
16293–2422 B (Coutens et al. 2018; Calcutt et al. 2018),
IRAS 4A2 (López-Sepulcre et al. 2017; Belloche et al. 2020),
and IRAS 2A1 (Belloche et al. 2020).
A bar diagram of the comparison of the detected N-bearing
species towards NIRS3 and other sources is shown in Fig-
ure 10. It can be seen that the abundances of N-bearing
species vary in different hot cores and hot corinos, which
may indicate that their chemical formation environments are
different. The Galactic centre hot core Sgr B2 (N) gener-
ally shows higher abundances than NIRS3 for most of the
detected species by approximately one order of magnitude
or more. The molecular abundances in NIRS3 show notable
variations when compared with other sources.
Following our comparison shown in the upper panel of
Figure 10, we found that the abundance of CH 3CN relative
to CH3OH varies from 2×10 −3 in IRAS 16293–2422 B to
1.1×10 −1 in Sgr B2 (N) and G10.47+0.03, corresponding to
a spread of nearly two orders of magnitude. The abundance
of CH3CN relative to CH 3OH in NIRS3 shows an interme-
diate value of 1.6×10 −2, comparable to Sgr B2 (M), Orion

4.2. Prebiotic chemistry of detected N-bearing molecules
Here, we focus on understanding the prebiotic chemistry
of the detected N-bearing molecules. To understand the pos-
sible formation pathways of the detected N-bearing species,
we compared the observed abundances with the existing as-
trochemical modelling abundances of these species. For the
chemical modelling results, we followed the three-phase (gas
+ grain + icy mantle) warm-up chemical models of Garrod
(2013) (hereafter, Model A) and Suzuki et al. (2018) (here-
after, Model B). Garrod (2013) used the MAGICKAL astro-
chemical code for three-phase warm-up chemical modelling
in the context of hot cores.
In the chemical model, Garrod (2013) considered an
isothermal collapse stage followed by a static warm-up
phase. In the isothermal collapse stage, the gas density in-
creases from n H = 3×10 3 cm−3 to 1×10 7 cm−3, and the
dust temperature falls to 8 K from 16 K. The second stage is
known as the warm-up phase, where the gas density remains
constant at 1×10 7 cm−3 and the temperature increases from
8 to 400 K. Garrod (2013) estimated the modelled gas-phase
abundances of the complex molecules over the time scales
of 5×10 4 yr (fast warm-up), 2×10 5 yr (medium warm-up),
and 1×10 6 yr (slow warm-up).
Similarly, Suzuki et al. (2018) computed a three-phase
warm-up chemical model in the context of hot cores and
high-mass protostars using the gas-grain chemical kinet-
ics code NAUTILUS. In the first stage (isothermal collapse
phase), Suzuki et al. (2018) increased the gas density from
3×10 3 cm−3 to 2×10 7 cm−3, and the dust temperature fell to
8 K from 16 K. In the second stage (warm-up phase), the gas
density remained fixed at 2×107 cm−3. The dust temperature
increased from 8 to 400 K. Suzuki et al. (2018) estimated the
modelled abundances of different complex molecules in the
gas-phase, grain surface, and icy mantle over fast (7.12×104
yr) and slow (1.43×10 6 yr) warm-up conditions. Suzuki et
al. (2018) showed that the fast warm-up condition is most
suitable for hot cores.
Models A and B are suitable for NIRS3 because the gas
density and temperature of NIRS3 are∼3×10 7 cm−3 and
150 K, respectively, and the source contains a chemically
rich hot molecular core (Szymczak et al. 2018). The com-
parison between the observed and modelled (both Model A
and B) abundances of the detected N-bearing molecules is
shown in Table 8. In the warm-up stages, Garrod (2013)
estimated the peak modelled abundances in the gas-phase,
whereas Suzuki et al. (2018) estimated the abundances of
molecules in the gas-phase, grain surfaces, and in icy man-
tles. Since the observed abundances are in the gas-phase,

4.2.1.Formation mechanism of CH 3CN
Numerous formation pathways for CH 3CN exist in both
the grain surface and gas-phase, yet recent quantum chemical
studies have indicated that CH 3CN predominantly forms on
the grain surface of HMCs (Giani et al. 2023). The potential
formation pathways of CH 3CN on the surface of grains are
outlined below:

Reaction 1 was first proposed by Prasad & Huntress
(1980), which shows that CH3CN is created directly between
the reaction of CH 3 and CN on the grain surface, but the
rate constants of this reaction are assumed and have not
been studied experimentally. The formation of CH 3CN
using reaction 1 on the grain surface is difficult because that
reaction might have an activation energy barrier owing to
the interaction of radicals with H2O-ice molecules (Enrique-
Romero et al. 2019, 2021, 2022). More importantly, the CN
radical reacts with the H 2O molecules of ice, which shows
that recombination reactions involving CN radicals on ice
are not possible (Rimola et al. 2018). Therefore, there is a
low probability of the formation of CH 3CN using reaction 1
on the grain surface of the hot cores.
Reaction 2 shows that CH3CN is formed on the grain sur-
face via hydrogenation of the CH2CN (Garrod 2013; Garrod
et al. 2017; Suzuki et al. 2018; Garrod et al. 2022). Similarly,
CH2CN is formed on the grain surface via the atom addition
sequence of carbon (C) and hydrogen (H) with the CN rad-
ical (Garrod 2013; Garrod et al. 2017; Suzuki et al. 2018).
Previous studies show that the CN radical forms in the star-
formation regions after the destruction of HCN in the gas-
phase (Garrod et al. 2017). The three-phase warm-up chemi-
cal models and quantum chemical studies have shown that re-
action 2 is the most efficient for the formation of CH3CN on
the grain surface of hot cores, particularly Sgr B2 (N) (Gar-
rod 2013; Garrod et al. 2017; Suzuki et al. 2018; Bonfand et
al. 2019; Garrod et al. 2022; Giani et al. 2023).
To understand the possible formation pathways of CH3CN
towards NIRS3, we compared our estimated abundances of
CH3CN with the modelled abundances. We estimate that
the abundance of CH3CN with respect to H2 towards NIRS3
is (1.4±0.7)×10 −9, which is similar to the slow warm-up
modelled abundance of Garrod (2013) (Model A). The gas-
phase modelled abundance of Suzuki et al. (2018) (Model B)
is slightly higher than the observed abundance of CH3CN to-
wards NIRS3. The estimated abundance of CH 3CN with re-
spect to CH3OH towards NIRS3 is (1.6±0.8)×10 −2, which
is approximately two and three orders of magnitude higher
than Models A and B, respectively. In the slow warm-up
conditions, Garrod (2013) showed that reaction 2 is the dom-
inant pathway for the production of CH3CN on the grain sur-
face. Since the observed abundance of CH 3CN with respect
to H 2 towards NIRS3 is similar to the slow warm-up mod-
elled value, this indicates that CH3CN may be formed via the
hydrogenation of CH 2CN (reaction 2) on the grain surface
of NIRS3. McEwan et al. (1989) and Garrod et al. (2022)
showed that CH 3CN is destroyed via the radiative associa-
tion reaction of CH3+ and CH3CN when the source tempera-
ture is above 100 K. We estimate the excitation temperature
of CH 3CN to be 180 K, indicating that the radiative asso-
ciation reaction of CH 3+ and CH3CN towards NIRS3 could
potentially destroy CH 3CN, leading to more active cyanide
chemistry.

that HC 3N is formed on the grain surface of HMCs. The

most efficient pathway for the formation of HC 3N on the
grain surface using quantum chemical studies. Subsequently,
Taniguchi et al. (2019) and Willis et al. (2020) used reaction
4 for the three-phase warm-up chemical modelling of HC3N.

that reaction 7 is responsible for the formation of NH 2CN
towards G31.41+0.31. Reaction 8 shows that the continued
hydrogenation of OCN forms NH2CHO on the grain surface,
and Bonfand et al. (2019) showed that this reaction is the
most suitable pathway for the formation of NH 2CHO on the
grain surface of Sgr B2 (N). Several three-phase warm-up
chemical models have shown that reaction 8 is the most
suitable route for the formation of NH 2CHO towards hot
cores (Garrod 2013; Suzuki et al. 2018; Bonfand et al. 2019;
Garrod et al. 2022).
We compare our estimated abundances of NH 2CN and
NH2CHO with the modelled abundances. We observe that
Model B (Suzuki et al. 2018) did not estimate the modelled
abundance of NH2CN, but Model A (Garrod 2013) estimated
the modelled abundance of NH 2CN. We estimated that the
abundances of NH2CN and NH2CHO with respect to H 2 to-
wards NIRS3 are (2.8±1.0)×10 −10 and (2.1±0.9)×10 −9.
We found that the observed abundance of NH 2CN is ap-
proximately two orders of magnitude lower than the mod-
elled abundance of Model A, but the abundance of NH2CHO
is nearly similar to the slow warm-up modelled value of
Model A. We observed that the derived abundance of NH2CN
relative to CH 3OH towards NIRS3 is nearly similar to the
medium warm-up modelled value of Model A, but the abun-
dance of NH2CHO relative to CH3OH is similar to Model B.
Similarly, we notice that the observed abundances of NH2CN
and NH2CHO relative to CH3CN are lower than that of both
Models A and B. Garrod (2013) showed that reaction 8 is
the main formation route for the formation of NH 2CHO on
the grain surface in slow warm-up conditions. After compar-
ison, we found that the observed and modelled abundances
of NH 2CHO relative to H 2 are very close, which indicates
NH2CHO may have been formed via the hydrogenation of
NH2CO (reaction 8) on the grain surface of NIRS3. We
also noticed that the observed NH 2CN/NH2CHO ratio to-
wards NIRS3 is not similar to the modelled values. Since
the observed and modelled abundances of NH 2CN relative
to H2 are not similar, we do not draw any conclusions about
the formation mechanism of NH 2CN towards NIRS3 using
Model A.
We also examined the three-phase warm-up chemical
model from Coutens et al. (2018) to understand the potential
formation pathway of NH 2CN towards NIRS3. Coutens et
al. (2018) estimated that the modelled gas-phase abundance
of NH 2CN is 3.7×10 −10. During the chemical modelling,
Coutens et al. (2018) used the grain surface reaction between
NH2 and CN (reaction 7) for the formation of NH 2CN. We
noticed that the observed abundance of NH2CN with respect
to H2 towards NIRS3 is nearly similar to the modelled abun-
dance of Coutens et al. (2018). This suggests that NH 2CN
may form through the reaction between NH 2 and CN (reac-
tion 7) on the grain surface of NIRS3.
4.3. Proposed chemical network between detected
N-bearing molecules
To understand the nitrile chemistry of NIRS3, we created a
chemical network between the detected N-bearing molecules
using gas-phase and grain surface chemical reactions, which
are shown in Figure 12. During the making of chemical net-
works, we mainly used the reactions discussed in Sections
4.2.1, 4.2.2, 4.2.3, and 4.2.4. We also used the additional
reactions, which are taken from the UMIST and KIDA astro-
chemistry reactions databases (McElroy et al. 2013; Wake-
lam et al. 2015). In the chemical network, we observed
that CH 3CN is formed via the continued hydrogenation of
C2N. Similarly, the continued hydrogenation of HC3N forms
C2H3CN and C 2H5CN. We also observed that CH 3CN is
destroyed via the radiative association reaction of CH 3+ and
CH3CN and forms C 2H5CNH+ in the gas-phase. Again, the
dissociative recombination of C 2H5CNH+ forms C 2H5CN.
Thus, the N-bearing molecules CH 3CN, HC 3N, C 2H3CN,
and C 2H5CN may be chemically linked via both gas-phase
and grain surface chemical routes. Similarly, CH 3CN may
be chemically linked with NH 2CHO and NH 2CN in both
the gas-phase and grain surface routes. Our molecular cor-
relation heat maps show that CH 3CN, C 2H5CN, C 2H3CN,
NH2CN, and NH 2CHO exhibit a tight correlation with each
other, and our proposed chemical network also exhibits the
chemical link of those molecules. Previously, Garrod et al.
(2017) showed that HC3N, C2H5CN, and C2H3CN are chem-
ically linked towards hot cores. Detailed quantum chemi-
cal and three-phase warm-up chemical modelling are needed
to verify the proposed chemical network and understand
the nitrile chemistry in the warm inner regions of the star-
formation regions.

5. Summary and conclusions
We analyzed ALMA band 4 data of a massive protostar,
S255IR NIRS3, and the following conclusions are made.
•From the dust continuum emission, we estimate that
the mass, dust temperature, and dust spectral index are
20.5±3.0 M ⊙, 150±16 K, and 1.7±0.6, respectively.
Similarly, the column density of molecular hydrogen and
dust optical depth towards NIRS3 are (1.1±0.2)×10 24
cm−2 and 2.1×10 −2.
•We found the emission lines of CH 3CN, C 2H5CN,
C2H3CN, NH 2CN, NH 2CHO, and HC 3N (ν 7 = 2) towards
NIRS3. We derive the column density and excitation
temperatures of detected molecules using the LTE-modelled
spectra. We observed the excitation temperatures of detected
N-bearing vary between 175 K and 220 K, which means
all detected N-bearing molecules emit from the warm inner
regions (T>100 K) of NIRS3. We also estimate the frac-
tional abundances of all detected N-bearing species relative
to H2, CH 3OH, and CH 3CN. Since NH 2CN and NH 2CHO
have NH 2 as a common precursor, the NH 2CN/NH2CHO
column density ratio towards NIRS3 is 0.13±0.06.
•We compared our estimated abundances of detected
N-bearing molecules with existing three-phase warm-up
chemical models of Garrod (2013), Suzuki et al. (2018),
Willis et al. (2020), and Coutens et al. (2018). We find that
the modelled abundances of CH3CN and NH2CHO reported
by Garrod (2013) are broadly consistent with our observed
values, differing by factors of only 1.04 and 0.72, respec-
tively. Likewise, the modelled abundances of C 2H3CN,
C2H5CN, and HC 3N predicted by Willis et al. (2020) agree
well with our observations within factors of 1.28, 0.67, and
0.96. Similarly, the modelled abundance of NH 2CN from
Coutens et al. (2018) matches the observed value within a
factor of 0.76. We also discuss the possible formation path-
ways of the detected N-bearing species based on gas/grain
chemistry towards NIRS3. Since the observed abundance
of CH 3CN towards NIRS3 is in good agreement with the
modelled value reported by Garrod (2013), we conclude that
CH3CN is likely formed via the hydrogenation of CH 2CN
(reaction 2) on the grain surface of NIRS3. Based on the
comparison between the observed abundances of HC 3N,
C2H5CN, and C 2H3CN and the model predictions of Willis
et al. (2020), we infer that HC 3N may be formed through
the reaction between C 2H2 and CN (reaction 4) on the
grain surface of NIRS3. Likewise, C 2H3CN may have been
formed via subsequent hydrogenation of HC 3N (reaction 5),
and C 2H5CN may then be formed through the continued
hydrogenation of C2H3CN (reaction 6) on the grain surfaces
of NIRS3. Additionally, the observed abundances of the
amide-like molecules NH 2CN and NH 2CHO are in close
agreement with the modelled values reported by Coutens
et al. (2018) and Garrod (2013). This agreement suggests
that NH 2CN and NH 2CHO are likely formed on the grain
surfaces of NIRS3 via the reaction between NH 2 and CN
(reaction 7) and the hydrogenation of NH 2CO (reaction 8),
respectively. However, a more detailed analysis of dominant
chemical pathways in tailored chemical models is required
to confirm these proposed interpretations.
•We also proposed a chemical network to understand the
chemical link between the detected N-bearing species. This
chemical link is important to understand the nitrile chemistry
towards NIRS3. A detailed quantum chemical model is re-
quired to verify this network.

In addition to various N-bearing species, we also detected rotational emission lines of CH 3OH toward NIRS3. Based on LTE
spectral modeling, the column density and excitation temperature of CH 3OH are (9.52±0.62)×10 16 cm−2 and 250±24 K,
respectively. The LTE spectral fit to the CH 3OH emission lines is presented in Figure A1. Details of previously detected N-
bearing molecules in the ISM and planetary environments are listed in Table A1. The RADMC model parameters adopted for
NIRS3 in the simulations are summarized in Table A2.
Figure A1.Detected rotational emission lines of CH 3OH towards NIRS3. The green lines indicate the observed molecular spectra of NIRS3,
and the blue lines represent the LTE model spectra of CH3OH. The optical depths (τ) of the detected CH 3OH transitions range from 5×10−2 to
8×10−2.
