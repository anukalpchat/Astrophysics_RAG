Stars in the initial and carbon-oxygen core mass ranges1, 2, 3, 4 of∼140−260and50−130M ⊙,
respectively, with low metallicity5, 6 are predicted to experience copious electron-positron pair
production in their cores 7, 8, leading to a runaway thermonuclear explosion that obliterates
the entire star in a luminous and long-duration pair-instability supernova explosion 9, 10, 11, 12 .
Some previous supernovae13, 14 have been interpreted in this context but lack the full range of
predicted properties. Here, we report detailed observations and modeling of the hydrogen-
rich supernova 2023vbw, which exploded in a low-metallicity (∼0.1Z ⊙) environment in a
dwarf star-forming galaxy at a redshift of0.088. Its light curve exhibits a luminous (1.6×
1043 erg s−1) and long-duration (190days) main peak, resulting in a total radiated energy of
3×10 50 erg, more than an order of magnitude greater than canonical core-collapse super-
novae. Semi-analytical light-curve modeling 15 yields a blue supergiant-like progenitor with
an ejecta mass of170−350M ⊙, radioactive nickel mass of1.2−1.6M ⊙, and explosion energy
of(6−13)×10 52 erg, well matched by pair-instability models. The early and late-phase light
curve and spectra also show evidence for interaction of the supernova ejecta with an aspher-
ical circumstellar medium. Discoveries of numerous such events with the upcoming Rubin
Observatory andRoman Space Telescopewill shed light on the deaths of the most massive
stars in the Universe.
On 2023 October 12.5 (UTC dates are used throughout), the Zwicky Transient Facility (ZTF)
discovered16 the transient AT 2023vbw with anr-band magnitude of19.9in the outskirts of its
dwarf host galaxy (Fig. 1). Using the last nondetection and first prediscovery detection from ZTF
forced photometry (Methods), we estimate an epoch of first light of 2023 October 10.0±1.4 and
3

use it as a reference epoch for all phases. Following a tentative spectroscopic classification 17 as a
hydrogen-rich Type II supernova (SN II), we confirmed the classification with a higher signal-to-
noise ratio spectrum at 134 days (Fig. 1) and measured a precise redshift of 0.0879 from narrow
host-galaxy emission lines (Methods), corresponding to a luminosity distance18 of 396 Mpc. Given
the lack of NaID absorption at the host redshift, we correct all data only for the Milky Way
extinction19 (AV = 0.126mag) assuming the reddening law 20 withR V = 3.1.
Our classification spectrum of SN 2023vbw is characterized by broad Balmer P Cygni pro-
files without narrow emission components, typical of SNe II 21. However, the best match from
spectral cross-correlation22 is with an SN phase∼80days earlier, indicating the unusually slow
evolution of SN 2023vbw. A comparison to theoretical spectral models 23 reveals a similar phase
offset, and a best match to a low-metallicity (0.1 Z ⊙ withZ ⊙ = 0.02) model based on weak FeII
absorption. This is consistent with a gas-phase metallicity of∼0.3 Z ⊙ inferred from host emission
lines at the SN location (Methods). The integrated host spectral energy distribution (SED) also re-
veals overall low gas-phase (∼0.2 Z ⊙) and stellar (∼0.08 Z ⊙) metallicities, active star formation
(∼0.2 M ⊙ yr−1), and low stellar mass (∼10 9.3 M⊙) (Extended Data Fig. 1; Methods).
Consistent with the slow spectral evolution, the optical light curves (Fig. 2) exhibit an ex-
tended cooling phase (∼40days) with blue colour (evolving fromg−r≈ −0.2to0.3mag),
followed by a long steady rise to a bright peak ofM r =−19.2mag at∼190days with nearly
constant colour (g−r≈0.6mag). The light curves then rapidly decline by1mag in∼40days
with red colour (up tog−r≈0.9mag) and subsequently settle on an extended tail with a mean
4

decline rate of∼0.005mag day −1, slower than the 56Co decay rate of∼0.01mag day −1, with
mild fluctuations and slightly bluer colour (g−r≈0.5mag) than the main peak.
Blackbody fits to the multi-epoch optical SEDs (Methods) reveal rapid temperature and
radius evolution in the initial cooling phase, from∼14,000to7,000K and∼0.6×10 15 to
2×10 15 cm, respectively, followed by a constant temperature (∼5,000K) and more gradual in-
crease in radius (up to∼6×10 15 cm) during the rise to peak (Extended Data Fig. 2). During the
decline and tail phases, the photospheric radius begins to recede (down to∼2×10 15 cm, matching
the end of the initial cooling phase), while the temperature slightly rises (up to∼6,000K).
A comparison of the bolometric light curve with prototypical SNe II 24 (e.g., SNe 1999em
and 2013fs) from red supergiant (RSG) progenitors highlights the unusual nature of SN 2023vbw
(Fig. 2), which more closely resembles the light-curve morphology of SN II 1987A25 from a com-
pact blue supergiant (BSG) progenitor, but with significantly larger luminosity and timescale. This
results in a total radiated energy of∼3×10 50 erg, more than an order of magnitude larger than
normal SNe II, which radiate∼10 49 erg(∼1%of their explosion energy). SN 2023vbw even
exceeds previously known very energetic SNe II (e.g., the SN 1987A-like OGLE14-073 13, 14 and
the peculiar iPTF14hls 26, 27), likely requiring a higher explosion energy than achievable with the
neutrino-driven mechanism28 (≲2×10 51 erg) for iron core-collapse SNe (CCSNe). The late-time
fluctuations of SN 2023vbw (≳330days) have comparable luminosity and timescale to those of
iPTF14hls.
The various light-curve phases (i.e., rise, decline, and tail) are accompanied by distinctive
5

spectral evolution (Fig. 3). During the light-curve rise, the typical SN II features remain steady,
with a roughly constant velocity (∼8,000and4,500km s −1 for Hαand FeIIλ5,169, respectively;
Extended Data Fig. 3). This indicates that the photosphere remains at the same mass coordinate,
which is physically expanding with a constant temperature (Extended Data Fig. 2), requiring a
large continuous heating source, unlike typical SNe II, but similar to SN 1987A and iPTF14hls.
Forbidden lines emerge during the light-curve decline, gradually shifting into the optically thin
phase with a receding photosphere and decreasing velocity (Extended Data Figs. 2 & 3). During
the light-curve tail, the Hαline evolves to exhibit a multicomponent profile with prominent red
emission at a velocity offset of∼2,800km s −1 (similarly seen in other hydrogen lines; Extended
Data Fig. 4). This indicates asymmetry in the photosphere, likely revealing previously hidden
shock interaction with disc-like circumstellar material (CSM) viewed off-axis 29, 30 (Methods), as
similarly observed in iPTF14hls31, 27, which provides the rise in temperature inferred from the light
curve (Extended Data Fig. 2) and responsible for the persistent continuum below5,500 ˚A in the
spectra (Extended Data Fig. 4). The lack of the narrow absorption features from unshocked CSM
places an upper limit on its velocity of≲170km s −1 (Methods). The coincidence of the blackbody
radii at the tail phase (≳400days) and the end of the intial cooling phase (∼40days) indicates
that the early phase may also be powered by CSM interaction (until the more spherical SN ejecta
become dominant during the light-curve rise and peak).
Motivated by the SN 1987A-like light-curve morphology (Fig. 2) and velocity evolution (Ex-
tended Data Fig. 3) of SN 2023vbw, we use a semi-analytical light-curve model 15 (Methods) that
calculates the bolometric luminosity by tracking hydrogen recombination in the ejecta and energy
6

deposition by 56Ni. We assume an outermost scale velocity ofv sc = 8,000km s −1 from the Hα
velocity (i.e., proxy for the fastest ejecta velocity in homologous phase), relating the ejecta ki-
netic energy and mass asE ej = 0.3Mejv2
sc, and a BSG-like progenitor radius9, 10, 3 ofR 0 = 100 R⊙
(Methods; see Extended Data Fig. 5 for the light-curve model dependence onR0, preferring a com-
pact progenitor). The main peak is well fit with a narrow region of the parameter space: ejecta and
56Ni masses ofM ej ≈170−350 M ⊙ andM Ni ≈1.2−1.6 M ⊙, respectively (Fig. 4). These masses,
and the resultant kinetic energy ofE ej ∼(6−13)×10 52 erg, exceed the values for ordinary iron
CCSNe28, 32 by more than an order of magnitude (e.g.,M ej ≈16 M ⊙ andM Ni ≈0.075 M ⊙ with
∼1.3×10 51 erg for SN 1987A 33, 15), and significantly exceed even the extreme SN II OGLE14-
07313, 15 (Mej ≈60 M ⊙ andM Ni ≈0.47 M ⊙ with∼1.2×10 52 erg).
The initial extended cooling phase, as well as the tail-phase excess relative to56Co decay, can
both be fit by a single power-law component with timet−3/8 (Fig. 4), whose evolution is consistent
with interaction of the SN ejecta with dense CSM having a wind-like density profile with mass-loss
rate of∼0.05 M ⊙ yr−1(vCSM/170 km s−1)(vsc/8,000 km s−1)−3, wherev CSM is the CSM velocity,
scaled to its upper limit (Methods). The CSM interaction is also evident by the emergence of strong
double-peaked spectral features during the tail phase (Fig. 3 & Extended Data Fig. 4), which points
to a disc-like CSM configuration viewed off-axis.
The observed and modeled properties of SN 2023vbw are well matched to the regime of
pair-instability supernova (PISN) predictions1, 2, 3, 9, 10 (Fig. 4; see also Extended Data Fig. 6 for the
compatible light-curve and velocity evolution with the nearest neighbor PISN numerical models
7

from BSG progenitors). The low, but nonzero, metallicity (∼0.1 Z ⊙) inferred from the SN itself
and its environment (Fig. 1 & Extended Data Fig. 1) is also predicted for PISNe 5, 6. Although the
progenitor formation channel to retain the sufficient hydrogen-rich envelope within a small BSG-
like radius is not well understood, one possible channel is a binary merger 34, 35, which may also
explain the presence of the dense disc-like CSM 36 (Methods). Alternative iron CCSN scenarios
with an additional power source, such as a central magnetar 37 or black hole accretion 38, yield
comparableM ej to fit the light-curve main peak due to photon diffusion (Methods); however,
magnetar formation is not naturally expected in the high progenitor mass range 2, 3, and sustained
accretion (≳100days) is likely implausible given the required large fallback mass and energy
deposition38 (Methods).
Thanks to its relatively low redshift, SN 2023vbw remains sufficiently bright for continued
multiwavelength observations that will reveal its progenitor mass-loss history and explosive nucle-
osynthesis. With declining optical depth, synchrotron emission from CSM interaction is expected
to become detectable in the X-rays and radio (Methods), while strong nebular emission lines from
iron-group elements are expected to emerge in the optical and near-infrared10, 39. Scaling from our
discovery of SN 2023vbw in current surveys, we expect that upcoming surveys with a significant
increase in search volume and redshift reach, namely the Vera C. Rubin Observatory andNancy
Grace Roman Space Telescope, will find tens to hundreds of such events, shedding light on the
environment, formation, evolution, and death of the most massive stars in the Universe 1, 2, 3, 4, as
well as on their wide-ranging implications from the mass limits for black hole formation 40 to the
unique fingerprints on early chemical evolution41.

Observatory (LCO43)BV gri-band imaging with the Sinistro cameras on the network of 1 m tele-

at the Haleakal ¯a Observatory (Hawai‘i, USA), from 2024 March 2 to 2025 June 3 (133 to 554
days after first light). LCO photometry was performed with point-spread-function (PSF) fitting
using the PyRAF 45-basedlcogtsnpipe 24 pipepline.BV- andgriz-band data were calibrated
to Vega46 and AB47 magnitudes, respectively, with the AA VSO Photometric All Sky Survey48 and

Additionally, we collected public photometry from ZTF 50, 51 and the Asteroid Terrestrial-

were corrected for the baseline. ZTFgri-band photometry was retrieved from the ZTF forced-
photometry service56, 57 and processed with quality cuts 57 and5σclipping. For each combination
of the band, ZTF field, and CCD quadrant, flux measurements were corrected for the baseline, as
similarly done for the ATLAS photometry. Flux uncertainties were corrected for the reducedχ 2
distribution of PSF fits57.

All the photometry in each band is weighted by uncertainty and stacked every 0.5 days. The
detection significance (σ) of the ATLAS and ZTF forced photometry is determined from the ratio of
measured flux (Fν) to its error (Fν,err). For the measurements above and below5σ, we report their
magnitudes (−2.5 log10Fν + ZP) and5σupper limits (−2.5 log 10[5×F ν,err] + ZP), respectively,
where “ZP” is the zero-point in the AB magnitude system. This yields a last nondetection at21.6
mag in the ZTFrband on MJD 60,225.5 and first detection at20.0mag in the ZTFgband on
MJD 60,228.4 (Fig. 2). By taking the midpoint of the last nondetection and first detection, we
estimate the epoch of first light to be MJD 60,227.0±1.4 (or 2023 October 10.0±1.4), with the
uncertainty corresponding to the difference between the first light and detection. Even if we use the
most conservative epoch of first light of the last nondetection, the difference is only1.3rest-frame
days, not affecting the main results of this paper. We find no significant detection (≥5σ) before
first light when stacking all the forced photometry every month.
We fit a blackbody SED to every epoch of photometry containing at least two filters obtained
within 2 days of each other to estimate the blackbody temperature and radius usingemcee 58.
The fitted blackbody SED is then integrated to obtain bolometric luminosity shown in Extended

sian Process (GP) regression 60 on the bolometric light curve, as shown in Extended Data Fig. 2,
with a combination of Mat ´ern (ν= 2.5) and Constant kernels for model parameter constraints
(“Light-curve modeling” section). The peak luminosity (Lp) and recombination timescale (tf) are

Light-curve modeling.With the lack of comprehensive numerical light-curve models spanning
PISN parameters (see the discussion at the end of this section), we compare the bolometric light
curve of SN 2023vbw to a parameter grid obtained by a semi-analytical model constructed for
SNe II15 to infer the properties of its progenitor and explosion. The model assumes homologously
expanding SN ejecta with uniform density (also supported by multi-dimensional simulations of
hydrogen-rich PISN models100), and takes into account the time-dependent hydrogen recombina-
tion in the ejecta as well as differences in opacity within the ejecta due to recombination. The
model can also incorporate heating sources in the ejecta, such as radioactive decay of56Ni (and its
decay product 56Co) synthesized in the explosion. The morphology of the main peak of SN 1987A-
like SNe (Fig. 2), with a slow rise and steep decline, is governed by an interplay of both recombi-
nation and heating. For accurately constraining the ejecta mass, the current model is favoured over
31

other widely used semi-analytical modeling tools101, 102, which assume uniform opacity throughout
the ejecta for simplicity.
The main parameters of the model are the ejecta mass (Mej), kinetic energy (Eej), progenitor
radius (R0), and 56Ni mass (M Ni) in the ejecta.M ej andE ej are related by the outermost scale
velocity of the ejecta (v sc; the fastest ejecta velocity in homologous phase), asE ej = 0.3M ejv2
sc,
and we adoptv sc = 8,000km s −1 based on the HαP Cygni line and the earliest blackbody radius
evolution that tracks the fastest part of the SN ejecta (Extended Data Figs. 2 & 3). For the distri-
bution of 56Ni in the ejecta, we choose the model’s “EXP+SOE” prescription that well reproduces
the light-curve morphology of SN 1987A 15. This distribution sets the 56Ni to be within the cen-
tral∼10%in mass, which is also in reasonable agreement with nucleosynthesis predictions of
numerical PISN models103, 10, 104, 12, 100.
We constrain the parameters that reproduce two characteristic quantities in the light-curve
main peak: the peak luminosity (L p) and the end of the optically thick phase (t f). Fig. 4 shows
the constraint onM ej andM Ni for a radiusR 0 = 100 R ⊙, typical for BSG progenitors. For
such compact progenitors, radioactive decay of 56Ni is mainly responsible for powering the main
peak, and thusL p mostly constrainsM Ni. On the other hand,t f is governed by bothM ej that sets
photon diffusion andM Ni that sets the heating of the ejecta, creating a diagonal contour. These two
constraints yield values ofM ej andM Ni that agree with theoretical expectations of PISNe1, 2, 3, 9, 10.
AsR 0 increases, the SN ejecta are subject to less adiabatic loss that reduces their internal
energy asE int ∝(R 0/vsct). Thus, a higherR 0 leads to an increasing contribution from the internal

energy generated by the explosion105, 106. As shown in Extended Data Fig. 5 with two variations of
R0 (50and500 R ⊙), while reducingR 0 makes little change from our fiducial assumption (100 R⊙),

for lower-mass explosions107. For much larger radii (≳1,000 R ⊙) expected for RSGs, theoretical
models9, 10 predict a nearly plateau-like light-curve morphology, analogous to SNe IIP, but contrary
to SN 2023vbw’s slow rise. Hence, we favour a BSG over an RSG progenitor.

Alternative scenarios.In this paper, we have considered the PISN scenario of the entire star
exploding and synthesizing large amounts of 56Ni, where the powering source of the main peak
is the radioactive decay of 56Ni and 56Co. In the literature, however, alternative mechanisms to
luminous SNe have been proposed, where an iron CCSN is heated by energy injection from a
central compact object, either a rapidly spinning neutron star (NS)37 or an accreting compact object
(NS or black hole)38.
Regardless of the nature of the heating mechanism, the rise of the light curve is governed by
photon diffusion, which directly relates toM ej. Hence, we expect the light curve to still require a
largeM ej (≳100 M ⊙), and thus a similarly massive progenitor. As the time dependence of fallback
accretion is uncertain, we consider the case of energy injection by NS dipole radiation where the
heating rate in the ejecta is given asLsd =L 0[1+(t/t sd)]−2, witht sd being the spindown timescale
andL 0 being the initial spindown luminosity. Here, we neglect heating due to 56Ni decay, as
an iron CCSN from a canonical neutrino-powered explosion 28 would produce only≲0.1 M ⊙
38

of 56Ni (Fig. 4), much less than the∼1 M ⊙ required to dominantly power the light curve of
SN 2023vbw by 56Ni decay. Our modeling similarly gives the constraint on the ejecta mass of
Mej ≈100−300 M ⊙, with a best fit of∼200 M ⊙. The best-fit values of the spindown parameters
areL 0 ≈4×10 43 erg s−1 andt sd ≈150days, corresponding to an initial spin period of∼8ms
and surface magnetic field of∼10 14 G for an NS mass ofM NS = 1.4 M⊙ and radius of12km.
A rapidly spinning NS powering the SN can be ruled out with high confidence based on the
inferredM ej. An NS remnant from a massive progenitor of massM ∗ =M ej +M NS ≳100 M ⊙
is difficult to reconcile with stellar evolution theory2, 3, 114, that instead predicts either a black hole
remnant or a PISN with no remnant (Fig. 4). Although binary evolution with mass transfer during
main sequence (Case A) may allow NS formation from such a massive progenitor due to hydrogen-
rich envelope stripping and helium core reduction119, the resultant explosion would be a hydrogen-
poor supernova unlike SN 2023vbw. The inferred mass further indicatesEej ≈10 53 erg, two orders
of magnitude larger than both the explosion energy of canonical iron CCSNe and the rotational
energy of an NS with spin period of 8 ms. Finally, a hydrogen-rich progenitor is expected to have
efficient angular momentum transport from the core to the envelope that rapidly spins down the
core, preventing the formation of a fast-rotating NS upon core-collapse120.
