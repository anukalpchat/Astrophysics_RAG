ABSTRACT
We report the results of a deep (≈65 ks)Chandraobservation of the globular cluster NGC 6540, obtained to investigate the nature
of the peculiar X-ray source 3XMM J180608.9–274553. This source was previously observed withXMM–Newtonto exhibit a short
(≈300 s) and intense X-ray flare whose luminosity and duration are inconsistent with both typical type I X-ray bursts from low
mass X-ray binaries and stellar flares. Our new data show the presence of three faint X-ray sources near the position of the flare
seen byXMM–Newton, only one of which was detected in a previous, much shorterChandraobservation. Based on the properties of
these sources, localized at sub-arcsecond precision, and of their optical counterparts, we discuss their possible nature and association
with 3XMM J180608.9–274553. We also discuss some scenarios to explain the X-ray flare, such as microlensing-induced amplifi-
cation, black hole flaring activity analogous to that observed from Sgr A ∗. Our results place new constraints on the nature of this
unusual transient and highlight the power of high-resolution X-ray observations for disentangling rare phenomena in the dense stellar
environments of globular clusters.
Key words.globular clusters: individual: NGC 6540, stars: flare, X-rays: binaries, X-rays: bursts
1. Introduction
Globular clusters (GCs), given their high stellar densities, are ex-
cellent places to search for rare and peculiar systems of interact-
ing compact objects. These systems often exhibit significant X-
ray emission, the brightest (L X ≳10 36 erg s−1) being low-mass
X-ray binaries (LMXBs), the faintest a larger and more varied
population of objects (e.g. LMXBs in quiescence, millisecond
pulsars, cataclysmic variables, as well as non-degenerate stars,
Pooley 2009).
NGC 6540 was initially reported as an open cluster of angu-
lar size∼0.5-1 ′, and later rediscovered as a candidate globular
cluster, possibly with a post-collapse core by Djorgovski (1987).
Bica et al. (1994) confirmed its globular cluster nature and de-
rived a distance of 3.5±0.4 kpc1.
NGC 6540 hosts several X-ray sources, and among these,
3XMM J180608.9–274553 (J1806, in the following), which was
discovered withXMM–Newtonclose to the cluster center. Dur-
ing the EXTraS project 2, it was found that J1806 displayed a
unique variability behavior (Mereghetti et al. 2018). In fact, on
2005 September 21, this source emitted a brief flare, lasting only
∼300 s, during which its flux increased by about two orders
of magnitude, reaching a peak luminosity of≈10 34d2
4kpc erg
s−1. This luminosity is too faint for a typical type I burst from

2. Observations and data analysis
The firstChandraobservation of NGC 6540, performed in 2008,
had a duration of only 5 ks and revealed the presence of a
faint source at the position of J1806 (Mereghetti et al. 2018).
To better study this variable source and to get a deeper X-
ray survey of NGC 6540, we obtained three newChandra
observations in 2023-2024, amounting to about 65 ks of to-
tal exposure time (see Tab. 1 for details). We employed, for
all datasets, theChandraInteractive Analysis of Observations
(CIAO, v4.17.0 Fruscione et al. 2006), with version v4.12.2 of
the calibration database. We reprocessed the observations with
the taskchandra_reproand, as all observations were per-
formed inVFAINTmode, we set thecheckvfphaflag toyes.
Unless otherwise specified, for the source extractions, we
employed circular regions with a radius corresponding to 90%
of the point spread function (PSF). As all the sources presented
here are within 1 ′ of the instrument aimpoint, this amounts to
≈2 ′′. The background was extracted from a circular 20 ′′-radius
source-free region. For both timing and spectral analysis, we
only considered events in the 0.5–7 keV band. Source and back-
ground spectra, spectral redistribution matrices and ancillary re-
sponse files were generated with the routinespecextract.
Given that X-ray sources often exhibit significant variability,
we first analysed the three new observations individually. When

3.1. Refined astrometry
We corrected the astrometry of the merged dataset by cross-
matching theChandrapoint sources against a higher-precision
catalog. We used the 2 micron all sky survey catalog (2MASS,
Skrutskie et al. 2006, the same used as reference for the Hubble
Space Telescope (HST) astrometry in Mereghetti et al. (2018)).
To this end, we employed fiveChandrasources. After this cor-
rection, we identified the centroid of theChandrasources by
fitting a 2D Gaussian profile (mimicking the PSF), with free am-
plitude and location, and scale factor fixed at the PSF size at the
approximate location of the source. The derived positions are
given in Table 2. The errors on these positions are dominated by
the uncertainty on the astrometry (1σerror of 0.075 ′′ on R.A.
and 0.099 ′′ on Dec.), which is larger than the statistical errors
derived from the Gaussian fits.
3.2. Spectral properties
The limited photon statistics collected for the three sources in
the J1806 region does not allow meaningful spectral modeling.
Hence, we characterize their spectral properties using hardness
ratios. We define the hardness ratio as HR=(H−S)/(H+S),
whereSandHare the net counts measured in the soft (0.5–
2 keV) and hard (2–7 keV) energy bands, respectively. Given
the low-count regime, uncertainties were computed using the
Bayesian Estimation of Hardness Ratios code (Park et al. 2006,
BEHR), which properly accounts for Poisson statistics and back-
ground subtraction.
The three sources display different spectral properties.
Source C exhibits a hard spectrum with HR=0.5 +0.5
−0.5. This value
indicates that nearly all detected counts are concentrated in the
hard band. Sources A and B show comparatively softer emission,
sharing the same hardness ratios of HR=−0.2 +0.6
−0.8.

3.3. Timing properties
The small number of counts detected from sources A, B, and C
does not allow a detailed study of their possible variability. No
statistically significant long-term variations were seen in sources
A and C by comparing their fluxes measured in the different
observations. We searched for variability on short timescales in
the longest observation (2024) by comparing the distributions of
time of arrivals of the three sources with that expected for a con-
stant flux using a Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) test. The resulting
probabilities that the flux is constant were 0.11, 0.19, and 0.24
for sources A, B, and C, respectively.

3.4. Position of J1806
To assess the exact position of the flaring source J1806 we re-
analysed theXMM–Newtondata using a maximum likelihood
(ML) method as described in Rigoselli & Mereghetti (2018), in
order to properly take into account the presence of sources N and
S. We based our analysis on the data of the EPIC-MOS cameras
in the 0.5–5 keV energy range, as the EPIC-pn data presented a
gap between CCDs just in correspondence of source N.
We first performed a ML fit using the whole observation to
simultaneously derive the positions of sources J1806, N, and S
in instrumental coordinates. These were then registered using the
Chandrapositions of sources N and S given in Table 2. We fi-
nally applied the ML fit to the image corresponding only to the
time interval of the flare to obtain the position of J1806: R.A.=
271.5379, Dec.=−27.7653 (1σerror of 0.75" on R.A. and 0.77"
on Dec.). Applying the same analysis procedure to different en-
ergy ranges and to the EPIC-pn data, we obtained consistent re-
sults.
4. Discussion
Figure 3 shows the positions of sources A, B, and C plotted on
the optical HST image of the central part of the globular clus-
ter NGC 6540. We also indicate in the figure the error region
of J1806 derived above (sect. 3.4), as well as the position of
source A as previously reported in Mereghetti et al. (2018). The
improved location accuracy of our newChandradata reduces
the number of possible optical counterparts of source A, which,
considering its position and brightness, is the most likely ori-
gin of the 2005 flare seen byXMM–Newton. Its flux measured
withChandrais lower than the quiescent level seen withXMM–
Newton, implying long-term variability by a factor∼7±2. How-
ever, the quiescent flux of J1806 was probably contaminated by
the contributions of sources B and C, which could not be re-
solved with the angular resolution ofXMM–Newton. Taking this
possibility into account, no strong claims for long-term variabil-
ity can be made.

Using a ML method to properly account for the small num-
ber of counts in each bin, we fitted the flare light curve with the
magnification model expected in the approximation of a point-
like source and lens. The best fit parameters derived with the
microlensing model areτ e =R E/v=906 s andu 0=0.012, where
RE is the Einstein radius,vis the lens transverse velocity with
respect to the Earth-source direction, andu 0 is the impact pa-
rameter (see, e.g., Paczynski 1986; Gould 1992). However, the
best fit (red line in Fig. 4) does not reproduce well the observed
profile, which is instead much better described by a Gaussian
curve (blue line).
Beyond the goodness of fit issue is the challenge of creating a
physical model within which binary self-lensing can produce an
event with the observed properties. The above best-fit parameters
imply orbital periods longer than 200 d for a neutron star lens (or
more than 100 d if the lens is a black hole of 10M⊙). Would such
a wide binary survive in the dense central region of a globular
cluster? Only a subset of those consistent with the light curve fit
are “hard” binaries, with orbital velocities larger than the ambi-
ent stellar velocities. Hard binaries are less likely to be torn apart
by interactions with other stellar systems in the dense environ-
ment near the cluster center, but developing consistent physical
models for such systems is even more challenging. Furthermore,
the high required system inclination, extremely low chance prob-
ability of observing at the right orbital phase, and possible hints
of spectral variability between flare and quiescence, lead us to
discard the binary self-lensing hypothesis.
The symmetry of the rise and fall times of the X-ray flare
observed byXMM-Newtonis unusual, as most X-ray flares ob-
served in a galaxy have a fast-rise/exponential decay (FRED)
shape, indicative of some sort of fast energy injection describ-
ing the rise in luminosity and slower thermodynamic cooling
describing the decline in luminosity. Very few flare mechanisms
can lead to a symmetric profile other than the lensing model de-
scribed above. However, symmetric X-ray flares are sometimes
seen from the supermassive black hole Sgr A ⋆ (Porquet et al.
(2008); Neilsen et al. (2013); Haggard et al. (2019)). At first
glance, it appears possible that the X-ray flare seen in NGC 6540
could be a scaled-down version of the symmetric flares seen in
Sgr A ⋆ but from an intermediate-mass black hole (IMBH) in
NGC 6540. The energy output from representative flares of Sgr
A⋆ (0.7−3.3×10 39 erg; Haggard et al. (2019)) is about 700-
3000 times greater than the total energy output of the J1806 flare,
which would imply an IMBH mass of a few tens of thousands
of solar masses, a reasonable value for an expected IMBH in a
globular cluster. In addition, the fitted power law X-ray index
of the spectra of the quiescent and flare emission of Sgr A ⋆ of
Γ =3.0±0.2 and 2.0±0.2, respectively (Haggard et al. (2019)),
are remarkably similar to the quiescent and flare emission power
law values of J1806 ofΓ =2.5±0.5 and 1.7±0.2, respectively
(Mereghetti et al. (2018)).
However, there are two major inconsistencies with an IMBH
interpretation for the J1806 flare. The most striking feature is that
J1806 is 7 ′′ (0.14 pc) from the dynamical center of NGC 6540.
Although the cluster is rather heavily obscured and somewhat ill-
defined in shape, it seems unlikely that the error in the position
center could be as large as 7′′, and dynamical friction would very
quickly cause a 10,000 M ⊙ black hole to sink to the dynamical
center of the cluster. Also, if the emission mechanism causing
the∼1 hour flares seen in Sgr A ⋆ (interpreted as magnetic re-
combination near the innermost stable circular orbit (ISCO) of
the black hole) is scaled down by mass would imply a flare dura-
tion of a 10,000 M ⊙ black hole of only∼15 seconds, far shorter
than the observed∼5 minute duration of J1806’s flare. Although
a flare emanating from material at a larger radius (∼100 gravita-
tional radii) in the disk of a 10,000 M⊙ black hole could have an
orbital period of five minutes, such a flare would cool radiatively
(instead of adiabatic expansion and rapid synchrotron radiation
as is the case for Sgr A ⋆) and therefore exhibit a FRED pro-
file. Thus, an explanation involving a Sgr A ⋆-like flare from an
IMBH in NGC 6540 has significant issues.
The fact that source A has two neighbors within 4 ′′ is
surprising given that there are only nine sources within 1 ′ of
the center of the cluster. Two of the nine sources are likely
foreground/background sources for the 1 ′ region considered
(Lehmer et al. 2012), leaving∼7 sources associated with the
cluster itself. We performed 100,000 simulations of randomly
placing seven sources in a 1 ′ region according to a King profile
with a core radius of 1.8 ′′ (appropriate for NGC 6540; Harris
1996). We found that only∼1% of the simulations contained
such a grouping of three sources located at a distance of at least
three core radii from the cluster center. Although this could sim-
ply be fortuitous, the grouping of two highly variable sources in

5. Conclusions
In this work, we have presented a re-analysis of the X-ray emis-
sion from NGC 6540, exploiting both archivalXMM–Newton
data and≈65 ks of newChandraobservations. The superior
angular resolution ofChandrais critical here: it resolves what
appeared as a singleXMM–Newtonsource into three distinct
sources (A, B, and C), s the most likely counterpart of the 2005
flare, and demonstrates that the quiescentXMM–Newtonflux
was likely the result of blended emission from the newly re-
solved sources. The origin of the remarkably symmetric X-ray
flare, however, remains unidentified. A binary self-lensing event
is disfavoured by the unphysical orbital parameters, lowa priori
probability it requires, as well as by the hints of spectral variabil-
ity of the flare with respect to the quiescent emission. We consid-
ered an IMBH interpretation, however this is inconsistent with
the source’s offset from the dynamical centre of the cluster and
the expected flare timescale. Despite this lack of a clear physi-
cal explanation, our work represents a significant step forward in
characterising the X-ray source population of NGC 6540, disen-
tangling blended emission that was previously unresolvable, and
placing meaningful constraints on the nature of one of the most
unusual X-ray flares observed in a Galactic globular cluster. This
ongoing puzzle calls for dedicated theoretical modelling efforts
to identify viable physical mechanisms capable of reproducing
the observed phenomenology.
