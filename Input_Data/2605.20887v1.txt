ABSTRACT
Using JWST observations in CANDELS fields, we measure the half-light radius (r e,light) and half-mass ra-
dius (re,mass) for 14,333 galaxies with stellar massesM ∗ >10 9M⊙ at redshifts0.2< z <2.5. To investigate
the difference betweenr e,light andr e,mass, we find thatr e,light is larger thanr e,mass for both quiescent galaxies
(QGs) and star-forming galaxies (SFGs). Moreover, the difference between these two radius is clearly corre-
lated with galaxy stellar mass,r e,light, and the rest-frameU−Vcolor. When examining the evolution of the
re,mass/re,light ratio, we observe a significant increase for SFGs atz >1.7. In contrast, no clear increase is
observed for QGs atz >1, though a slight decreasing trend is seen between0.2< z <1.0. By fitting a linear
relationship between galaxy size and stellar mass, we find that the slope forr e,light is∼0.1–0.3 dex larger than
that forr e,mass. In terms of galaxy size evolution at a fixed stellar mass, there,mass of QGs increases by a factor
of∼3–5 fromz∼2.5toz∼0.2. In contrast, ther e,mass of SFGs increases by a factor of approximately 2
over the same redshift range, with this growth trend closely following that of theirre,light. These results indicate
that previous insights into galaxy evolution based onr e,light remain valid when consideringr e,mass, although
the specific slopes show some variations.

1.INTRODUCTION
The study of galaxy sizes and their evolution over cosmic
time has been ongoing for decades and serves as a critical test
for galaxy evolution models (e.g., Mo et al. 1998; Kravtsov
2013; Jiang et al. 2019). One of the key methods for un-
derstanding how stellar mass is assembled within a galaxy
is the relationship between galaxy size and stellar mass (e.g.,
van der Wel et al. 2014; Lange et al. 2015; Mosleh et al. 2020;
Miller et al. 2023). Numerous previous studies have exam-
ined this relation across a wide range of redshifts (0< z <4)
and stellar masses (109.0M⊙ < M ∗ <10 11.5M⊙), revealing
that both quiescent (QGs) and star-forming galaxies (SFGs)
exhibit a positive correlation in their mass-size relation (e.g.,
Shen et al. 2003; Ferguson et al. 2004; Trujillo et al. 2006;
Williams et al. 2010; van der Wel et al. 2014; Mowla et al.
2019; van der Wel et al. 2023; Ji et al. 2024; Jia et al. 2024;
Martorano et al. 2024). Moreover, the slopes of the mass-
size relation for QGs are generally steeper compared to SFGs
(e.g., van der Wel et al. 2014; Mowla et al. 2019). Addi-
∗ Corresponding author: Guanwen Fang
tionally, several studies have also shown that, at a fixed stel-
lar mass, galaxies at lower redshift (0< z <2) exhibit
larger sizes compared to those at higher redshift (z >2),
which is likely driven by a complication of the “inside-out”
growth mechanism and the “progenitor bias” effect. (e.g.,
van Dokkum & Franx 2001; Bezanson et al. 2009; Naab et al.
2009; Carollo et al. 2013; Poggianti et al. 2013; van de Sande
et al. 2013).
However, these studies are primarily based on the light-
weighted morphologies (particularly in the optical band)
of galaxies. Since most galaxies exhibit negative radial
color gradients and, consequently, negative mass-to-light ra-
tio (M/L) gradients, light serves as a biased tracer of stellar
mass (e.g., Szomoru et al. 2013; Tacchella et al. 2016; Suess
et al. 2019; Miller et al. 2023; Buitrago, Fernando & Trujillo,
Ignacio 2024; Chamba, Nushkia et al. 2024). Such negative
color gradients may be attributed to a variety of mechanisms.
For QGs, the color gradient is often attributed to lower metal-
licity in the outer regions, while for SFGs, it is typically due
to the presence of distinct components: an older, redder bulge
at the center and a younger, bluer disk at larger radii (e.g., Wu
et al. 2005; Barbera & de Carvalho 2009; Miller et al. 2023).
Therefore, the commonly used half-light radius (re,light) does

not directly probe the underlying stellar mass distribution of
galaxies (e.g., Mosleh et al. 2017; Suess et al. 2019; Miller
et al. 2023).
A more robust parameter for representing the size of a
galaxy is its half-mass radius (r e,mass), which can be ob-
tained by fitting the galaxy’s stellar mass map using a S´ersic
function. Several methods are available to obtain a galaxy’s
stellar mass map. Some studies derive the stellar mass
map through the relationship between color andM/L(e.g.,
Szomoru et al. 2013; Chan et al. 2016; Suess et al. 2019;
van der Wel et al. 2023). However, given the significant scat-
ter in the relationship between color andM/L, the stellar
mass maps obtained through this method may be unreliable.
Some other research had utilized the pixel-by-pixel spectral
energy distribution (SED) fitting method to obtain the stellar
mass map (e.g., Suess et al. 2019; Mosleh et al. 2020). This
method can reliably obtain the distribution of various physi-
cal properties within galaxies and has been applied in many
studies, including star formation rate profiles, color gradi-
ents, and galaxy mergers (e.g., Lanyon-Foster et al. 2007,
2012; Zibetti et al. 2009; Hemmati et al. 2014; Cibinel et al.
2019). Thus, this method can be employed to investigate the
re,mass of galaxies.
However, some recent studies have indicated that apply-
ing this method at high redshifts (for example, atz >1)
may involve certain risks (e.g., Zibetti et al. 2009; Mart´ınez-
Garc´ıa et al. 2017; Sorba & Sawicki 2018; Papovich et al.
2023; Song et al. 2023; Williams et al. 2024; Wang et al.
2025). To perform the pixel-by-pixel SED fitting, imaging
with high resolution is crucial. Before the successful launch
of the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST), all available
data were derived from the Hubble Space Telescope (HST,
e.g., Jafariyazani et al. 2019; Suess et al. 2019; Mosleh et al.
2020, 2025). But, it is important to note that the reddest band
of the HST is F160W, which can only trace rest-frame wave-
lengths shorter than 1µmatz >1. Recent work by Song
et al. (2023) has shown that when using SED fitting, rest-
frame near-infrared data plays a crucial role in accurately
measuring galaxy stellar masses. When lacking data whose
wavelength is longer than 1µmin the rest-frame, the stellar
mass of galaxies may be overestimated by 0.2 dex. There-
fore, the results about the evolution ofr e,mass derived solely
from HST data require further validation.
Fortunately, the high-resolution near-infrared data pro-
vided by JWST offer a valuable opportunity for conducting
more in-depth investigations aboutr e,mass. The release of
JWST/NIRCam imaging over fields previously observed by
CANDELS (e.g., GOODS, COSMOS, EGS, and UDS; Gi-
avalisco et al. 2004; Scoville et al. 2007; Davis et al. 2007;
Lawrence et al. 2007; Grogin et al. 2011; Koekemoer et al.
2011) enables more precise stellar mass mapping atz >1.
In this study, we combine JWST/NIRCam data with existing
HST observations to construct improved stellar mass maps
for galaxies withM ∗ >10 9 M⊙ across0.2< z <2.5using
pixel-by-pixel SED fitting. We then measure both there,light
andr e,mass for these galaxies.

degree of size growth is somewhat similar forr e,mass and
forr e,light, consistent with the previous JWST-based studies
(e.g., van der Wel et al. 2023; Clausen et al. 2024; Ji et al.
2024; Jia et al. 2024; Martorano et al. 2024).

5. Finally, the conclusions are summarized in Section 6.

withH 0 = 70 km s −1 Mpc−1,Ω m = 0.3, andΩ Λ = 0.7,

2.DATA AND SAMPLE SELECTION
2.1.Dataset
In this work, we utilize imaging data from three major
JWST extragalactic surveys: the Cosmic Evolution Early
Release Science Survey (CEERS; Finkelstein et al. 2023),
the JWST Advanced Deep Extragalactic Survey (JADES,
including JADES-GDS and JADES-GDN; Eisenstein et al.
2023), and the Public Release IMaging for Extragalac-

tic Research (PRIMER, including PRIMER-COSMOS and
PRIMER-UDS; Dunlop et al. 2021). The CEERS program
covers an area of94.6arcmin 2 within the EGS field. We
make use of six NIRCam broad-band filters available in this
field: F115W, F150W, F200W, F277W, F356W, and F444W.
The JADES survey spans approximately177arcmin 2 across
the GOODS-N and GOODS-S regions, while PRIMER cov-
ers roughly393arcmin 2 in the COSMOS and UDS fields.
For both JADES and PRIMER, we include observations in
the same six bands as CEERS, along with F090W imaging.
Although medium-band NIRCam observations are available
in portions of these fields, they are significantly shallower
than the broad-band data. Thus, we restrict our analysis to
the broad-band images. Moreover, we have tested that the
lack of F090W data in the CEERS field does not have a sig-
nificant impact on our results, so we include the CEERS field
in our subsequent analyses. To supplement the JWST data
at shorter wavelengths, we incorporate HST imaging in the
optical bands F435W, F606W, and F814W. This extended
wavelength baseline improves the reliability of photometric
redshift and stellar population estimates.
All JWST and HST imaging used in this work have been
uniformly processed by Valentino et al. (2023), and the re-
duced images are publicly available via the Cosmic Dawn
Center1. In addition to imaging products, Valentino et al.
(2023) also provides source catalogs generated via multi-
band detection, including photometry and photometric red-
shift estimates. In deriving photometric redshifts, Valentino
et al. (2023) have already taken into account the use of
medium bands, since many studies have demonstrated that
incorporating medium bands plays an important role in accu-
rately determining galaxy redshifts. We also tested the effect
of medium-band data on stellar mass estimates from SED fit-
ting at fixed redshift, and found that the stellar masses derived
with and without medium-band data are nearly identical.
Using the photometric catalogs andz phot provided by the
Cosmic Dawn Center, Song et al. (in prep) performed SED
fitting withCIGALE(Bradley et al. 2022) to derive physi-
cal properties of galaxies in these fields. Their fitting proce-
dure assumes a delayed-τstar formation history (SFR(t)∝
texp(−t/τ)), the Bruzual & Charlot (2003) stellar popula-
tion models, nebular emission following Inoue (2011), and
the dust attenuation law from Charlot & Fall (2000). The pa-
rameter settings used in their modeling are consistent with
those adopted in Shen et al. (2024). The resulting catalogs
provide estimates of stellar mass and other physical proper-
ties, which we adopt in this work.
2.2.Sample Selection
To ensure the robustness of our analysis, we adopt a
set of selection criteria following the recommendations of
Valentino et al. (2023) and Song et al. (in prep). Specif-
ically, we select galaxies that satisfy the following condi-
tions: (1) signal-to-noise ratio (S/N) greater than 10 in the
1 https://dawn-cph.github.io/dja/index.html
detection image (S/N det >10) to ensure reliable source se-
lection; (2) reduced chi-squared value (χ 2/Nfit ≤8) with
at least six filters available for photometric redshift estima-
tion (Nfit ≥6) to ensure thatz phot is reliable; (3) S/N greater
than 3 in all six JWST broad bands (F115W, F150W, F200W,
F277W, F356W, F444W), which ensures reliable SED fit-
ting; (4)log(M ∗/M⊙)>9; and (5)0.2< z <2.5.
Since our analysis incorporates observations from mul-
tiple fields with varying depths, it is important to ensure
that our sample is mass-complete across all fields. Using
source injection simulations, Merlin et al. (2024) demon-
strate that the detection completeness reaches nearly 100%
in all fields for sources brighter than 28 mag. Based on
F444Wlim = 28mag, Song et al. (in prep) derived the
90% stellar mass completeness as a function of redshift us-
ing the following relation:M comp(z) = 7.36 + 0.5 ln(z).
The stellar masses of galaxies in our sample lie more than 1
dex above the completeness threshold across the full redshift
range. Additionally, we examine the F444W magnitude dis-
tribution of our sample and find that all galaxies are brighter
than 26 mag. Therefore, we think that the varying observa-
tional depths across different fields do not affect our results.
After applying the selection criteria, we obtain a final sam-
ple of 14,333 galaxies. The distributions of stellar mass and
redshift are shown in the right and top panels of Figure 1,
respectively. The joint distribution of stellar mass versus red-
shift is displayed in the center panel of Figure 1.
2.3.Classifying Star-forming and Quiescent Galaxies
To differentiate the results between SFGs and QGs, we first
classify our sample into SFGs and QGs using the rest-frame
U−VandV−Jcolors. As demonstrated by Franx et al.
(2003) and Whitaker et al. (2012), galaxies occupy different
regions in theU V Jplane, depending on their particular star
formation rate and dust. In this paper, we adopt the criteria
from Muzzin et al. (2013) to distinguish SFGs from QGs,
where QGs are defined as:
(U−V) rest >1.3for allz
(V−J) rest <1.5for allz
(U−V) rest >0.88(V−J) rest + 0.69 [0.0< z <1.0]
(U−V) rest >0.88(V−J) rest + 0.59 [1.0< z <3.0]
The distribution of our sample in the rest-frameU V Jcolor
space is shown in Figure 2. From this figure, it is evident
that the proportion of QGs in our sample is quite low, partic-
ularly atz >1.8. This is also consistent with galaxy evolu-
tion models, which predict that the proportion of QGs grad-
ually increases as redshift decreases (Marchesini et al. 2014;
Mowla et al. 2019). Finally, we obtained a sample of 13080
SFGs and 1253 QGs.

During the fitting, the effective radius is constrained to lie
between 1 and 200 pixels, and the S ´ersic index is limited to
n∼0−8. Then, we estimate the morphology at rest-frame
5000 ˚A by linearly interpolating the S ´ersic parameters from

3.3.Half-mass Radius
In this work, we adopt two different methods to measure
the galaxy’sr e,mass. The first method (2D stellar mass map
method) is to derive the stellar mass map of each galaxy
through pixel-by-pixel SED fitting, and then fit the 2D stellar
mass map with a single S ´ersic model usingGALFIT(Peng
et al. 2010) to obtainr e,mass, along with the corresponding
uncertainties provided by GALFIT. However, some studies
have shown thatGALFITtends to underestimate the uncer-
tainties of morphological parameters (e.g., H ¨aussler et al.
2007; Dimauro et al. 2018). In Section 5, we evaluate the re-
liability of our method using a mock recovery test. Therefore,
we use the results of this test to re-estimate the uncertainties
inr e,mass. In brief, we divide the mock galaxies into sev-
eral stellar-mass bins and, for each bin, compute the scatter
of the difference between the recoveredr e,mass and the in-
put values. Then, using the median stellar mass of each mass
bin together with the corresponding uncertainties, we deter-
mine ther e,mass uncertainty for each galaxy in our sample by
interpolating. The second method (1D stellar mass density
profile method) is to perform SED fitting on the multi-band
fluxes measured within elliptical apertures of different radii,
thereby constructing the 1D stellar mass density profile of the
galaxy. We then fit this stellar mass density profile to derive
re,mass.
3.3.1.Method 1: 2D Stellar Mass Map Method
We construct stellar mass maps of the galaxies through
pixel-by-pixel SED fitting and measure theirr e,mass using
GALFIT(Peng et al. 2010). The specific processing steps
are described below: we first extract a400×400pixel cutout
for each galaxy using the PSF-matched images. Then, we
perform SED fitting for each pixel within the cutout using the
CIGALEprogram (Boquien et al. 2019). When performing
SED fitting, the redshift of each pixel is fixed to the galaxy’s
redshift, which is obtained from the Cosmic Dawn Center
catalog, and the parameter settings are the same as described
in Section 2.1. Some examples of the stellar mass maps
we obtained are shown in the left column of Figure 4. By
masking out other galaxies in the cutouts, we then measure
re,mass of these galaxies usingGALFITwith the same setup
as described in Section 3.2, which has been shown to reli-
ably recoverr e,mass from mock data by Mosleh et al. (2020).
The fitting results of stellar mass maps in Figure 4 are shown
in the second column, with the corresponding residuals dis-
played in the third column. Figure 4 reveals that the S ´ersic
model provides a good fit to the stellar mass map of the
galaxy.
Since we perform SED fitting on each pixel of the galaxy,
this approach may introduce potential issues, such as the ne-
glect of correlated noise and the low S/N ratio in the outer
regions, which could lead to unreliable fitting results. Some
studies have suggested that pixel binning can mitigate these
effects to some extent (e.g., Abdurro’uf et al. 2023; Haryana
et al. 2025). However, it is important to note that binning es-
sentially acts as a smoothing process, which may partially
wash out the intrinsic structural information of galaxies.
Since different pixels are not completely independent, their
fluxes can still partially reflect the variations within differ-
ent regions of a galaxy. Simple pixel binning would smooth
over these variations and reduce the spatial detail captured.
Our goal is therefore to preserve as much of JWST’s high-
resolution information as possible, motivating the choice of
pixel-by-pixel SED fitting without binning. In Section 5, we
further demonstrate the reliability of our method for deriving
re,mass using two complementary approaches: first, through
mock simulation tests, and second, by comparing our results
with those from previous studies.
It is worth noting thatGALFIT(Peng et al. 2010) fitting
fails for approximately 27.66% of the galaxies. To assess
the potential impact of these failures, we compared the dis-
tributions of stellar mass and other relevant properties be-
tween galaxies with successful and failed fits. We find that
the distributions are similar, suggesting that these failures are
unlikely to introduce significant biases in our results. Never-
theless, we caution that some residual effects cannot be com-
pletely ruled out, and readers should keep this limitation in
mind when interpreting our measurements.
3.3.2.Method 2: 1D Stellar Mass Density Profile Method
While the 2D stellar mass map method provides high-
resolution measurements, it may be sensitive to pixel-level
noise and outer low-S/N regions. To complement this, we
also adopt an alternative approach based on 1D stellar mass
density (Σ ∗) profiles. For each galaxy, we can derive its
Σ∗ profile by fitting spectral energy distributions at differ-
ent radii. Then the galaxy’sr e,mass can be determined from
these profiles. This method naturally accounts for correlated
noise, mitigates the effects of low-S/N pixels in the outskirts,
and is less sensitive to non-S´ersic structural components.
Specifically, we first estimate the galaxy morphologies at
rest-frame1µmto define the measurement apertures. Since
we have already derived the galaxy morphologies in all avail-
able bands in section 3.2, we can obtain the galaxy morphol-
ogy at rest-frame1µm by linearly interpolating the S ´ersic
parameters from the observed bands. Then, we extract the
fluxes in elliptical annuli with a step of0.2R e to obtain ra-
dial flux profiles, and perform resolved SED fitting in each
elliptical annulus usingCIGALE, adopting the same con-
figurations described previously. An example of this pro-
cedure is shown in Figure 5: panel a) presents a galaxy in
the F444W band, with black dashed lines denoting elliptical
annuli spaced at intervals of0.2R e. The red, orange, cyan,

4.RESULTS
In the previous section, we derive the stellar mass struc-
tural parameters of galaxies using both the 2D stellar mass
map method and the 1D stellar mass density profile method.
The following Section 5.1 will confirm that the results ob-
tained from the two approaches are in good agreement.
Therefore, in the subsequent analyses, we primarily adopt
the results derived from Method 1. In this section, we explore
the differences between thesere,mass andr e,light in detail and
investigate the relationships between the galaxy size, stellar
mass, and redshift of galaxies.
4.1.Difference betweenr e,mass andr e,light
As mentioned in the Introduction, many studies have
shown significant differences betweenr e,mass andr e,light
(e.g., Suess et al. 2019; Mosleh et al. 2020; Miller et al.
2023). The ratio ofr e,mass/re,light reflects the color gra-
dient within a galaxy. In the absence of a color gradient,
re,mass andr e,light would be equal. Generally, a strong nega-
tive color gradient could result inr e,mass/re,light <1, while
a positive color gradient could lead tor e,mass/re,light >1.
Since a galaxy’s color gradient is closely tied to its growth
processes, studying the relationship betweenr e,mass/re,light
and other physical properties can provide us with valuable
insights into the mechanisms driving galaxy evolution.

4.3.Size Evolution at Fixed Stellar Mass
Given the influence of redshift on the ratio ofr e,mass to
re,light, the evolution ofr e,mass could differ fromr e,light. In
this section, we examine the evolution of galaxy sizes within
the redshift range0.2< z <2.5. To minimize the effect
that galaxy’s size is closely related to stellar mass, we divide
our sample into two stellar mass bins (10 9.0M⊙ < M ∗ ≤
1010.5M⊙ and10 10.5M⊙ < M ∗ ≤10 12.0M⊙) for further
analysis.
The distributions of galaxy size as a function of redshift
are shown in Figure 10. The evolution ofr e,mass is shown in
left panels a)-b), while the results forr e,light are presented in
right panels c)-d). In each panel, the red and blue points rep-
resent the median size within different redshift intervals of
SFGs and QGs, respectively. The evolution of galaxy sizes is
described by the relationr e ∝(1+z) γ. We fit all the median
points in Figure 10. The fitting method and the error estima-
tion method remain consistent with those in the analysis of
the mass-size relation. The best fitting results are also plot-
ted as solid lines in this figure, while the best-fit parameters
γare listed in Table 3. When we directly fit the individual
points instead of the medians, the results remain unchanged.
In Figure 10, it can be observed that the size evolution of
QGs is significantly faster than that of SFGs, regardless of
whether consideringr e,mass andr e,light. This observation
may suggest distinct mass assembly mechanisms for QGs
and SFGs. When examining the size evolution of galaxies
with different stellar masses, the slope for massive galaxies
appears slightly steeper than that for less massive galaxies,
although the difference is not pronounced. This trend has
also been demonstrated in other studies. For example, by se-
lecting galaxies in the CANDELS fields, van der Wel et al.
(2014) found a slope ofγ=−1.01for early-type galax-
ies andγ=−0.52for late-type galaxies at a stellar mass
of10 10.25M⊙. In contrast, for galaxies with a stellar mass
of10 11.25M⊙, the slope becomes steeper, withγ=−1.32
for early-type galaxies and -0.80 for late-type galaxies. This
dependency between galaxy size evolution and stellar mass
could be attributed to massive galaxies undergoing more fre-
quent minor mergers, which drive faster size evolution.

5.DISCUSSION
5.1.Comparison with 1D results
In this work, our main conclusions are based onr e,Galfit,
the effective radius derived from 2D stellar mass maps using
GALFIT(Method 1; Section 3.3.1). To verify the reliabil-
ity of this method, we also deriver e,1D from 1DΣ ∗ profiles
(Method 2; Section 3.3.2) and compare the results for galax-
ies in the JADES-GDS field, as shown in panel a) of Figure

5.2.Comparison with Previous Studies
Building on the internal consistency of our methods, we
now situate our results within the context of existing litera-
ture. Several previous studies have provided catalogs of mea-
suredr e,mass values (e.g., Szomoru et al. 2013; Suess et al.
2019; Mosleh et al. 2020; Miller et al. 2023; van der Wel
et al. 2023). To further assess the reliability of our method,
we compare our results with those from earlier works, fo-
cusing on Suess et al. (2019) and van der Wel et al. (2023).
The former is widely used as a benchmark, while the latter is
likewise based on JWST observations, as in our study. In the
case of Suess et al. (2019), multiple approaches were used to
deriver e,mass, and here we adopt the results from Method 1,
which the authors recommend as their preferred strategy. For
van der Wel et al. (2023), we specifically compare with the
results obtained from their 2D measurement method.
As shown in Figure 12, we match the results of this study
with those reported by Suess et al. (2019) and van der Wel
et al. (2023). Compared with Suess et al. (2019), the overall
offset between the two measurements is small (0.04 dex), but
the scatter is more pronounced at about 0.26 dex. This find-
ing is consistent with previous comparative analyses: Mosleh
et al. (2020) reported an average offset of 0.04 dex and
a scatter of∼0.20 dex when comparing their results with
those of Suess et al. (2019), while Miller et al. (2023), us-
ing a Bayesian multi-Gaussian expansion technique to model
galaxy profiles and measurer e,mass, found a mean offset of
0.05 dex and a scatter of 0.20 dex relative to Suess et al.
(2019). Notably, despite differences in both data and method-
ology, both studies similarly indicated that the Suess et al.
(2019) measurements tend to be systematically larger. In
contrast, our comparison with van der Wel et al. (2023) yields
a smaller overall offset (0.02 dex) and scatter (0.19 dex). Rel-
ative to Suess et al. (2019), the better agreement with van der
Wel et al. (2023) suggests that JWST data are likely criti-
cal for obtaining robust estimates ofr e,mass. This may be
because incorporating JWST data allows SED fitting to bet-
ter disentangle the degeneracy between dust attenuation and
stellar population age. Taken together, the consistent offsets
and scatters across different studies demonstrate the robust-
ness of our results.
5.3.Mock recovery test
To further validate the reliability of our method, we per-
form mock recovery tests using simulated galaxies. Follow-
ing Mosleh et al. (2020), we randomly select 500 real galax-
ies from our catalog to construct single-S ´ersic models for
each filter, using identical structural parameters (n,r 50,b/a,
and PA). The range of these parameters is chosen to match
the observed distribution of galaxies in the stellar mass–size
plane (see Figure 9). All models are convolved with the
F444W-band PSF. To remove any inherent color gradients,
we assume the same structural shape in all filters, so the
simulated galaxies have identical shapes at different wave-
lengths. The total flux of each model in each filter is scaled
to match the flux of the corresponding real galaxy, ensuring
that the total stellar mass is conserved. Finally, the mod-
els are inserted into empty regions of the mosaic images that
contain realistic background noise, to generate mock obser-
vations. Figure 13 shows four randomly selected examples
of these multi-band mock galaxies generated using GALFIT.

5.4.Possible physical explanation
In this study, we utilize JWST observations within the
CANDELS fields to systematically examine the differences
betweenr e,mass andr e,light, as well as to investigate how the
choice of radius definition influences the size–mass relation
and its redshift evolution. Overall, our observations support
the inside-out growth scenario, in which the central regions
of galaxies form and evolve earlier than their outskirts, pro-
viding key insights into the structural development of galax-
ies.
In Figure 7, we have found a negative correlation between
re,mass/re,light and galaxy stellar mass. This trend can be

naturally interpreted in the framework of inside-out growth:
as the stellar mass of galaxies increases, their centers become

Moreover, we observe distinct redshift evolution of
re,mass/re,light between SFGs and QGs. Atz >1.5,
re,mass/re,light of SFGs increase with redshift. This may be
because, at high redshift, SFGs may maintain active star for-
mation across all radii, resulting in relatively flat color gradi-
ents (e.g., Genel et al. 2017; Costantin et al. 2023; Ormerod
et al. 2024). This is also consistent with our finding that SFGs
at high redshift exhibit a shallower slope in the size–mass re-
lation. At high redshift, star formation occurs across all radii
of galaxies, which increases their stellar mass but may not
significantly change their size. In contrast, at low redshift,
star formation is primarily concentrated in the outskirts of
galaxies, so the increase in stellar mass leads to a more pro-
nounced growth in galaxy size. While for QGs, their central
regions are already older and richer in metals or dust; over
time, outer regions gradually accumulate metals and dust or
undergo mergers that redistribute stellar populations, flatten-
ing the color gradients and stabilizing or slightly increasing
re,mass/re,light at lower redshifts (e.g., Naab et al. 2009; Oser
et al. 2011; Whitaker et al. 2013; Pacifici et al. 2016).
6.SUMMARY
In this study, by combining HST and JWST data, we con-
struct stellar mass maps for galaxies with redshifts in the
range of0.2< z <2.5using pixel-by-pixel SED fitting.
Then we measure theirr e,mass andr e,light for these galaxies
to investigate their mass-size relation and size evolution. Our
conclusions are summarized below:
(1) Both QGs and SFGs exhibitr e,mass smaller than their
re,light. The differences between these two radii are clearly
negatively correlated with stellar mass,(U−V) rest color,
andr e,light.
(2) Ther e,mass also exhibits a clear positive correlation
with stellar mass. But the slope forr e,mass is flatter than that
ofr e,light.
(3) Similar tor e,light,r e,mass also exhibits clear evolu-
tion with redshift. In our sample, at fixed stellar mass, QGs
increase their effective radius by a factor of∼3–5 from
z∼2.5toz∼0.2, while SFGs grow more moderately,
by a factor of∼2.
(4) For massive galaxies, we findγ=−0.97 +0.11
−0.30 for
SFGs andγ=−1.64 +0.08
−0.14 for QGs. These trends are con-
sistent with those observed forr e,light, indicating that both
mass-weighted and light-weighted sizes follow a similar red-
shift evolution.
However, in this study, we are unable to determine the
physical origin of the difference betweenr e,mass andr e,light
because we cannot distinguish the effects of age, metallicity,
dust, and some other physical factors. Additionally, due to
the wavelength limitations of NIRCam, we are unable to ex-
tend this study to galaxies at higher redshifts. In the future,
we will incorporate MIRI data to investigatere,mass of galax-
ies at even higher redshifts. We also plan to use some integral
field spectroscopic data to investigate the physical origins of
color gradients and explore how these gradients andr e,mass
evolve as galaxies transition from star-forming to quiescent
states.
