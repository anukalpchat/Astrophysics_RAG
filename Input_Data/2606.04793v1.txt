ABSTRACT
We present a systematic study of the multiphase circumgalactic medium (CGM) around galaxies and
quasars, traced by Caiiλλ3934, 3969, Mgiiλλ2796, 2803, and Civλλ1548, 1550, using the Year 1
dataset from the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument. These three doublets trace CGM gas across
a range of temperatures, from cold to warm phases, and we employ a stacking technique to measure
the corresponding absorption signals using background sources. We show that CGM structure is
strongly phase-dependent: ions tracing progressively cooler gas exhibit increasingly steep radial profiles
in equivalent width of absorption (W i). These trends are broadly consistent with predictions from
cosmological simulations, supporting a phase-stratified CGM in which cooler gas is more centrally
concentrated. Specifically, halos of emission-line galaxies exhibit a strong radial transition from cool
to warm gas, whereas halos of quasars show a more uniform distribution, likely regulated by active
galactic nuclei feedback; in contrast, the cold gas traced by Caiiin low-redshift galaxies is tightly
confined to inner regions. We further demonstrate that the radial scalingW i ∝D α is primarily set
by host stellar mass, particularly for the cool-phase medium, suggesting efficient heating processes in
massive halos. By jointly leveraging multiple absorption tracers from observation and simulations, we
map the CGM from cold to warm phases and place new constraints on the baryon cycle that governs
galaxy evolution.
Keywords:quasars: absorption lines, galaxies: halos, circumgalactic medium
1.INTRODUCTION
The circumgalactic medium (CGM) serves as the criti-
cal interface between galaxies and the surrounding large-
scale structure, playing a fundamental role in regulating
galaxy formation and evolution (e.g., Tumlinson et al.
2017). This surrounding medium regulates the supply
of gas for star formation, the redistribution of metals,
and the impact of feedback processes, while also govern-
ing gas accretion, outflow recycling, and the thermal and
dynamical state of baryons in galaxy halos (P´ eroux et al.
2020; Faucher-Gigu` ere & Oh 2023; Yu et al. 2025; Lyu
et al. 2026). Thus, observations of the CGM, together
with their connection to galaxy properties, provide key

constraints on models of galaxy evolution, especially on
the subgrid prescriptions of stellar and black hole feed-
back (Ford et al. 2016; Angl´ es-Alc´ azar et al. 2017; Wang
et al. 2019; Wang & Lilly 2022a,b; Ma et al. 2024; Jia
et al. 2025; Lyu et al. 2025; Peroux & Nelson 2024; Wang
2026).
It is widely recognized that the CGM is a highly mul-
tiphase and dynamically complex medium, spanning a
wide range of temperatures and densities, from cool
(T∼10 4 K) dense clouds to hot (T≳10 6 K) diffuse
halo gas (e.g., Werner et al. 2014; Zahedy et al. 2019;
Olivares et al. 2022; Dutta et al. 2024). Numerical sim-
ulations indicate that cold-mode accretion (Dekel et al.
2009), feedback-driven outflows (Shah et al. 2025), and
gas recycling processes coexist and interact within the
CGM, giving rise to filamentary structures, turbulent
mixing layers, and condensation driven by thermal in-

These different phases in the CGM exhibit substantial
differences in their spatial distribution and evolutionary
behavior (Faucher-Gigu` ere & Oh 2023; Saeedzadeh et al.
2023).
EAGLE zoom simulations show that low-ionization
metal gas predominantly resides in cool, dense clouds in
the inner CGM, while Ovitraces a spatially and phys-
ically distinct, diffuse high-temperature phase (Oppen-
heimer et al. 2018). TheErissimulation also highlights
that hot, metal-enriched gas (T >10 5 K) is preferen-
tially transported into the outer CGM through high-
velocity outflows (Shen et al. 2012). Cool, photoionized
gas (T∼10 4 K), commonly traced by Ciiand Siii, ex-
hibits a rapidly declining covering fraction beyond the
virial radius (R vir). In contrast, high-ionization species
such as Ovi(e.g., Sanchez et al. 2019; Li et al. 2021)
and Civmaintain substantial covering fractions out to
large distances, with Ovi-enriched halos extending to
∼4R vir (Shen et al. 2013).
Although simulations suggest a well-defined double-
exponential structure of the CGM, with an inner region
dominated by dense, low-ionization gas and an outer re-
gion dominated by diffuse, high-ionization gas (Liang
et al. 2016), observationally probing this multiphase na-
ture of the CGM remains extremely challenging due to
its intrinsically low surface brightness (Lokhorst et al.
2019; Zaritsky et al. 2019). As a result, absorption-
line studies that employ phase-sensitive tracers are es-
sential for effectively characterizing its different compo-
nents (Zhu et al. 2014; Ng et al. 2025; Chen et al. 2025b).
For example, Mgiiand Caiilines are commonly used
to trace cool gas (∼10 4 K) (e.g., Bergeron & Stasi´ nska
1986; Zhu & M´ enard 2013a; Lan & Mo 2018; Anand
et al. 2021; Chen et al. 2025c; Ng et al. 2025), while
Civ(∼10 4.5–105 K) and Ovi(∼10 5.5 K) trace rela-
tively warmer gas (e.g., Tumlinson et al. 2011; Bordoloi
et al. 2014; Kacprzak et al. 2015; Garza et al. 2025).
Based on multi-ion observations of individual absorp-
tion systems (e.g., Hi, Mgii, Siii, Civ, and Ovi),
Sameer et al. (2024) employ a cloud-by-cloud multiphase
ionization modeling approach and demonstrate that the
CGM is not a continuous medium but is instead com-
posed of a superposition of numerous, highly fragmented
discrete clouds spanning cool (∼10 4 K), warm (∼10 4.5–
105 K), and hot (∼10 5.5–106 K) phases. This means
that multiphase absorption features can overlap along
the same line of sight (LOS), but typically do not arise
from the same physical gas cloud (Werk et al. 2016). On
global scales, Lan et al. (2025) showed from individual
absorber measurements that Mgiipredominantly traces
gas in the inner CGM, close to galaxies, whereas Civ
extends to larger radii, reaching the outer CGM and
extending into the intergalactic medium (IGM). Kine-
matically, quasar sightline observations near the galaxy
major axis (e.g., DeFelippis et al. 2021; Kacprzak et al.
2025; Ho et al. 2026) show that low-ionization gas (e.g.,
Hi, Mgii, and Siii) exhibits stronger co-rotation with
the host galaxy disk and more ordered motion, whereas
high-ionization gas (e.g., Ovi) shows weaker co-rotation
and more kinematically diffuse behavior.
As multi-wavelength observations are required to
probe different tracers (Tumlinson et al. 2017; Nelson
et al. 2025), achieving a consistent characterization of
the multiphase CGM remains highly challenging. De-
spite substantial progress in previous studies, a unified
view of the CGM across temperature regimes remains
limited by the reliance on either small samples of in-
dividually detected absorbers (Lan & Fukugita 2017;
Anand et al. 2021; Zou et al. 2024b,c) or single-phase
analyses (e.g., Zhu & M´ enard 2013a; Zhu et al. 2014;
Wu et al. 2025; Ng et al. 2025). Thus, with the goal
of establishing a statistically robust connection between
different ionic tracers and fully capturing the ensemble
properties of multiphase gas in galaxy halos, this work
leverages statistical samples of thousands of galaxy–
quasar (or quasar–quasar) pairs and employs a stacking
approach that preserves the ensemble properties of the
CGM while mitigating biases associated with indepen-
dent detections of individual absorption lines.
We utilize the Year 1 (Y1) dataset from the Dark En-
ergy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) to systematically
examine three representative absorption features trac-
ing multiple CGM gas phases from cold to warm: the
CaiiH & Kλλ3934, 3969 (≲10 4 K), Mgiiλλ2796, 2803
(∼10 4 K), and Civλλ1548, 1550 (∼10 4.5–105.5 K) dou-
blets. Furthermore, we compare our results with DESI-
like synthetic spectra from TNG50 provided by the Syn-
thetic Absorption Line Spectral Almanac (SALSA; Nel-
son et al. 2025), offering a physically motivated frame-
work for interpreting the observed multiphase CGM
properties. This work is structured as follows. We intro-
duce the DESI Y1 data, sample selection, and spectral
stacking methodology in Section 2. Section 3 presents
our key findings: the spatial distribution of the multi-
phase gas traced by the three absorption lines and the
comparisons with simulations, as well as the dependence
of their radial profiles on host galaxy redshift and stel-
lar mass. In Section 4, we summarize the implications
for the multiphase CGM and present future prospects.

2.DATA ANALYSIS
2.1.DESI Y1 Dataset
DESI is a Stage-IV ground-based dark energy experi-
ment (Levi et al. 2019) installed on the Mayall 4-meter
telescope at Kitt Peak National Observatory. Equipped
with 5,000 robotic fiber positioners and a wide field of
view, DESI is capable of obtaining spectra for thou-
sands of galaxies and quasars simultaneously in a sin-
gle exposure (Silber et al. 2023; Miller et al. 2024; Pop-
pett et al. 2024). The instrument’s high throughput
and spectral resolution (R∼2000–5100) over a broad
wavelength coverage (3600–9824 ˚A) make it an ideal fa-
cility for investigating the diffuse CGM through absorp-
tion line spectroscopy (e.g., Chen et al. 2025c,b; Lan
et al. 2025). Extragalactic sources in DESI can be cat-
egorized into four classes (Myers et al. 2023), including
QSOs (0.2< z <6.0; Y` eche et al. 2020; Chaussidon
et al. 2023) and three types of galaxies: Bright Galaxy
Survey galaxies (BGSs; 0< z <0.6; Ruiz-Macias et al.
2020; Hahn et al. 2023), Luminous Red Galaxies (LRGs;
0.3< z <1.2; Zhou et al. 2020, 2023), Emission Line
Galaxies (ELGs; 0.6< z <1.6) (Raichoor et al. 2020,
2023).
DESI Y1, as the first major data release of the DESI
collaboration, contains spectral data for more than 18
million targets (DESI Collaboration et al. 2025). This
release represents a significant leap in statistical power
compared to the Early Data Release (EDR), which was
published in June 2023 (DESI Collaboration et al. 2024),
offering an order-of-magnitude increase in the number
of extragalactic spectra. This large dataset enables un-
precedented statistical sensitivity to detect weak metal
absorption lines and to probe multiphase gas across di-
verse halo environments (e.g., Wu et al. 2025; Ng et al.
2025).

2.4.Measurement
We estimate the uncertainty of the composite spec-
trum at each wavelength pixel using a bootstrap re-
sampling approach with 100 realizations, which is suf-
ficient for convergence. To quantify the absorption
strength, we fit each absorption feature with (double)
Gaussian profiles using theemceesampler 5 (Foreman-
Mackey et al. 2013) and calculate the rest-frame equiv-
5 https://emcee.readthedocs.io/en/stable/index.html
alent width (W i) by integrating the best-fit Gaussian
models over a window of±3σfrom the line centroid.
The measuredW i, along with the mean redshift and
stellar mass of the galaxy sample in each bin, are cat-
aloged for the subsequent analysis of scaling relations.
The uncertainty of theW i is estimated as the root mean
square (RMS) of the flux errors within the same wave-
length interval (see Chen et al. 2025c, for details).

In simulations, the surrounding CGM actively in-
teracts with host galaxies, comprising both inflowing
and outflowing gas. Feedback-driven outflows provide
a natural physical explanation for these observed ion-
dependent trends: low-ionization species trace relatively
recent outflows, in which cool, dense gas has been re-
cently launched from the galactic disk and has not yet
propagated far before being slowed or recycled by grav-
ity, forming a fountain-like cycle (Liang et al. 2016).
By contrast, high-ionization species are associated with
older outflows that were expelled at earlier times, sub-
sequently advected to larger radii and modified by long-
term expansion and shock heating, reaching warmer
phases that are more spatially extended. However, the
inflow scenario also provides a plausible explanation for
these ion-dependent trends. The low-ionization tracers
(e.g., Mgii, Siii, Cii) probe cool, compact clumps that
are likely in a recycling or inflowing phase, and are ex-
pected to accrete onto the galaxy on Gyr timescales,
while high-ionization species trace a hot, diffuse halo
medium (Oppenheimer et al. 2018). The inner regions of
halos have higher baryon densities and therefore higher
cooling rates, which can lead to a more concentrated dis-
tribution of cool gas (Sutherland & Dopita 1993; Ford
et al. 2016; Voit et al. 2017). In practice, the CGM likely
reflects a combination of both effects.

Notably, even for the same absorption line, theαcan
vary significantly across different samples. For Mgii, for

QSOs. QSO host halos typically reside in more massive

As a consequence, the centrally concentrated structure
of cool gas is more easily disrupted, while high-ionization

halo environment. This also reflects that host galaxy
properties, such as stellar mass and redshift, can signif-
icantly affect the radial profiles ofW i, and these effects
will be further discussed in Section 3.3. This motivates
our effort to use consistent sightlines for comparisons,
or at least ensure similar foreground samples.

The left panel of Figure 5 shows Mgii-related
equivalent-width ratios (Wi/WMg II) as a function of im-
pact parameter, revealing the underlying phase struc-
ture. For the high-redshift samples (ELGs and QSOs),
the Civ/Mgiiratio increases monotonically with im-
pact parameter, indicating that the outer halo is in-
creasingly dominated by the warm, highly ionized gas
phase. However, the steepness of this trend differs signif-
icantly between the two populations. The ratio around
ELGs exhibits a rapid rise with a power-law index of
1.11±0.03, whereas the profile around QSOs is much
flatter (0.37±0.04).
A similar increasing trend of Civ/MgiiwithDis
also seen in simulations, albeit with variations in am-
plitude and slope. This difference may partly arise from
the use of median equivalent widths (e.g., Oppenheimer
et al. 2018; Li et al. 2021) versus mean-based equivalent
widths (Nelson et al. 2025) (further discussion provided
in Appendix A). In addition, the non-linear relationship
betweenW i and column densityN i may also contribute
to differences between (e.g., Oppenheimer et al. 2018)
and other studies. Lan et al. (2025) interpret the in-
creasingW CIV/WMgII with impact parameter within a
multiphase CGM framework, where different ionization
phases are not strictly co-spatial but exhibit distinct ra-
dial distributions and covering fractions. As the Mgii-
bearing phase declines more rapidly with radius than the
Civ-bearing phase, the resulting line-of-sight mixtures
naturally produce an increasing Civ/Mgiiratio toward
larger galactocentric distances.
The difference in theαalso reflects variations in the
strength of the radial thermodynamic (or ionization)
gradient within their host halos, with ELGs showing a
more pronounced transition from cooler inner regions to
a warmer, more highly ionized outer CGM compared
to QSO host halos. In this picture, QSO halos main-
tain a relatively high ionization state throughout their
volume, with the cool and warm gas phases more uni-
formly mixed, whereas ELG halos exhibit a clear ion-
ization stratification, transitioning from a cool-gas-rich
core to a warm-gas-dominated envelope. In contrast,
the low-redshift BGS sample shows an opposite trend for
the cold-to-cool gas ratio: the Caii/Mgiiratio decreases
with a negative slope (∼ −0.6 to−1.2), confirming that
the cold gas phase traced by Caiiis strongly confined to
the inner regions, relative to the more spatially extended
Mgii-traced cool gas.
By sequentially comparing different tracers within
matched samples (Caiivs. Mgii, Mgiivs. Civ), we
have mapped the spatial distribution patterns of dis-
tinct gas phases in the CGM. As illustrated in the right
panel of Figure 5, we present a schematic summary of
theα–temperature relation for the three ions, their cor-
responding equivalent-width radial profiles, as well as
a cartoon representation of their two-dimensional spa-
tial distributions. Here, the CGM shows a clear mul-
tiphase structure: the coldest gas (traced by Caii) is
confined to the innermost regions, the cool gas (traced
by Mgii) extends farther out, and the warm gas (traced
by Civ) forms the most extended component. Although
the absolute radial extent and absorption strength of
each phase depend on host galaxy properties, such as
stellar and halo mass, the relative central concentration
remains unchanged across different galaxies: cold gas is
the most concentrated, followed by cool gas, and then
warm gas (Liang et al. 2016; Oppenheimer et al. 2018).

4.SUMMARY
This study presents a systematic analysis of the mul-
tiphase CGM around galaxies and quasars utilizing
the DESI Y1 dataset. We focus on three absorption
lines, including Caiiλλ3934, 3969, Mgiiλλ2796, 2803,
and Civλλ1548, 1550, across a wide redshift range
(0.1< z <4) and impact parameters up to∼150 kpc.
These three classical absorption lines trace gas spanning
a temperature range ofT∼10 3.5–105.5 K, probing the
cold, cool, and warm phases, thus serving as key di-
agnostics of the multiphase CGM. By combining DESI
observations with predictions from cosmological simu-
lations (e.g., TNG50, FIRE, and EAGLE), we explore
how their spatial distributions differ and how they relate
to the properties of the host galaxies. Our main findings
are summarized as follows:
Spatial Distribution.Within comparable foreground
samples, the radial profile ofW Mg II is more centrally
concentrated than that of Civ(Figure 2 & 4), while
the Caiiprofile is even more centrally concentrated
than Mgii(Figure 3). This suggests that in the mul-
tiphase CGM, progressively colder components are in-
creasingly confined to the dense central regions, whereas
the warmer phases exhibit a more extended and stable
distribution. This phase-dependent stratification is also
reproduced in TNG50 (Nelson et al. 2025) and FIRE (Li
et al. 2021) simulations, which suggests that it arises
from the combined effects of feedback-driven outflows,
gas recycling, and halo cooling, with low-ionization gas
confined to inner regions and high-ionization gas more
extended.
Ratio Profiles.For ELGs, theW C IV/WMg II ratio rises
steeply from the inner to outer halo, indicating a rapid
transition from a cool-gas-dominated core to a warm-
gas-dominated envelope (Figure 5). In contrast, QSO
halos show a much flatter ratio profile, suggesting that
AGN feedback maintains a relatively uniform distribu-
tion of warm and cool gas phases throughout the halo.
In the BGS sample, the rapidly decliningW Ca II/WMg II
ratio with distance reflects the strict confinement of the
cold Caiiphase to the dense central regions, regulated
by both gas density and temperature.
Dependence on Galaxy Properties.W i ∝D α, with
the indexαvarying across foreground samples and likely
driven primarily by the stellar mass of the host galaxies
(Figure 6). This trend, particularly pronounced for the
colder tracers, likely reflects AGN-driven heating that
progressively rises with galaxy mass, disrupting the cold
structures in the inner halo and thereby potentially ex-
plaining their quenching behavior. While simulations
broadly reproduce the observed radial scaling relations,
they exhibit differences in normalization and mass de-
pendence, suggesting that the efficiency of feedback and
the redistribution of cool gas remain key uncertainties
in current models.
These absorption-line measurements provide a critical
benchmark for cosmological simulations, offering new
constraints on feedback processes and the multiphase
structure of the CGM. As mentioned above, a more cen-
trally concentrated distribution of cooler gas may result
from the preferential transport of hot, metal-enriched
gas into the outer CGM via high-velocity outflows (Shen
et al. 2012), while the colder or more dust-prone com-
ponents remain confined to the inner regions (Richter
et al. 2011). With the advent of high-resolution imaging
from space telescopes, such as Euclid (Euclid Collabora-

13
tion et al. 2022, 2024) and the Roman Telescope (Wang
et al. 2022), this scenario could be tested through the
anisotropy in the spatial distribution of multiphase gas.
Meanwhile, other large cosmological surveys, such as
DESI-II and Spec-5 (Schlegel et al. 2022), will en-
able systematic statistical measurements of the CGM at
higher redshifts, thereby advancing our understanding
of galaxy evolution and the baryon cycling.

B.HALO SIZE EFFECT
The comparison in this paper using a fixed physical
distance therefore inevitably mixes halo-mass and virial-
radius effects. Massive galaxies have larger halos, and
comparisons at fixed kpc may therefore make the profiles
appear flatter or reduce the apparent phase distinction.
Here, we discuss this effect in more detail. For the CGM
of an individual galaxy, ifW∝D α, then it can also be
expressed asW∝(D/R vir)α up to a normalization fac-
tor. Therefore, if the redshift and stellar mass distribu-
tions of the sample are well controlled, the scatter inRvir
within each bin is small. In this case, binning inDor
inR vir yields consistent results:W∝D α (equivalently
W∝(D/⟨R vir⟩)α) is consistent withW∝(D/R vir)α,
and the three forms yield approximately the sameα.
However, in practice such control is rarely perfect, and
the resulting scatter inR vir within a givenDbin may
introduce additional stacking-induced effects.
In addition, measurements ofαat a fixed physical
scale effectively probe different relative locations within
halos of different sizes. However, the Mgii(e.g., Zhu
et al. 2014; Lan & Mo 2018; Wu et al. 2025; Chen et al.
2025c) and Caii(e.g., Zhu & M´ enard 2013a; Ng et al.
2025) profiles show broadly consistent values ofαwithin
the one-halo regime, suggesting that comparisons across
