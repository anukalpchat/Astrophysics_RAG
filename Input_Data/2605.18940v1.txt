ABSTRACT
Dark matter halos destined to fall into a more massive host differ from typical field halos of the
same mass even before infall. In cosmological simulations, we find that the progenitor mass functions
of these “future subhalos” are systematically shifted toward higher masses, with the shift growing as
infall approaches. The bias takes a compact form within extended Press-Schechter theory: the collapse
barrier is multiplied by a functionβ(D/D infall, a), whereDis the linear growth factor at scale factor
aandD infall is the growth factor at infall. We findβ(x, a) = (1−x) 1.20+0.14a for theM 200c mass
definition and (1−x) 1.20+0.05a forM 200m; the explicit scale-factor dependence captures the late-time
influence of dark energy. One consequence is that halos shortly before infall are 10–15% more centrally
concentrated than typical field halos of the same mass.
1.INTRODUCTION
Dark matter subhalos – halos that orbit within larger
host halos – differ from field halos of the same mass (J.
Diemand et al. 2007; V. Springel et al. 2008; ´A. Molin´ e
et al. 2017). After a subhalo falls into a host, tidal
forces reshape its structure while stripping its mass, and
its growth by accretion is suppressed or halted. These
post-infall effects have been extensively studied in both
simulations and analytics, and they are a key ingredi-
ent in semianalytic models of cosmic substructure (A. J.
Benson 2012; F. Jiang et al. 2021; N. Hiroshima et al.
2018; J. St¨ ucker et al. 2023).
In this paper, we describe howfuture subhalos– halos
that will fall into a more massive host – differ from typ-
ical field halos even before infall. This pre-infall bias is
part of every subhalo’s history, distinct from the post-
infall effects described above. It is a corollary of the
assembly biasphenomenon: at fixed halo mass, older
halos (those that grew to a set fraction of their present
mass earlier) tend to be more densely clustered (L. Gao
et al. 2005; R. H. Wechsler et al. 2006; L. Gao & S. D. M.
White 2007; N. Dalal et al. 2008). Assembly bias also
runs the other way: halos in dense environments tend
to be older than average halos of the same mass (R. K.
Sheth & G. Tormen 2004; G. Harker et al. 2006; C. Maul-
betsch et al. 2007). The bias extends to halo density
profiles as well, because older halos are more centrally
concentrated (J. F. Navarro et al. 1997; R. H. Wechsler
et al. 2002; A. D. Ludlow et al. 2013). The effects of as-

sembly bias are typically attributed to tidal forces from
massive neighbors and from larger-scale structure, which
suppress the growth of halos in dense regions (H. Y.
Wang et al. 2007; O. Hahn et al. 2009; H. Wang et al.
2011; A. P. Hearin et al. 2015; A. N. Salcedo et al. 2018;
A. Paranjape et al. 2018; P. Mansfield & A. V. Kravtsov
2020). Future subhalos are the extreme case, with the
massive neighbor close enough to soon become the host.
Indeed, P. S. Behroozi et al. (2014) found that infalling
halos cease accreting and begin losing mass well out-
side the eventual host’s virial radius, and J. Shi et al.
(2018) reported qualitative differences between the as-
sembly histories of infalling and field halos.
Here we systematically characterize the pre-infall bias
by measuring the progenitor mass functions (PMFs) of
future subhalos in cosmological simulations. To ensure
the bias is truly pre-infall, we restrict the measurement
to halos that have never previously been subhalos, since
a substantial fraction of halos near a massive host have
already had a subhalo episode (L. Bakels et al. 2021).
We then develop a simple analytic model that captures
the bias across cosmologies and infall epochs, and we
use it to show how the bias translates into higher con-
centrations for subhalos near infall.
We find that the pre-infall bias can be expressed in
the following compact form. Consider halos at scale fac-
tora 0 and linear growth factorD 0, a subset of which
will become subhalos when the growth factor isD infall.

2.CHARACTERIZING THE SUBHALO BIAS IN
SCALE-FREE SIMULATIONS
We analyze publicly available halo catalogues from the
Erebos simulation suite (B. Diemer 2020). We primar-
ily make use of the scale-free simulations of B. Diemer
& A. V. Kravtsov (2015). These simulations assume
matter domination and are initialized with power-law
power spectra,P(k)∝k n. There are four simulations,
corresponding ton=−2.5,−2,−1.5, and−1. Each
simulation employs 1024 3 particles. Halos from these
simulations are identified with therockstarhalo finder
(P. S. Behroozi et al. 2013), and the calculation of their
masses is described by B. Diemer (2020). We use the
M200 halo mass definition, which is the mass within the
radiusR 200 that encloses an average density 200 times
the cosmological background density.
Scale-free simulations are convenient because all times
are equivalent, which allows all of the simulation snap-
shots to be stacked together to maximize halo sample
sizes. In particular, at each scale factora, we define a
characteristic mass scaleM ∗(a) such thatσ(M ∗) = 1.

2.1.Main-progenitor mass accretion histories
For each of the four scale-free simulations, figure 2
shows the median main-progenitor growth histories for
a range ofM(a 0)/M∗(a0) (descendant masses). For each
curve, we stack 7 adjacent mass bins (so we include ha-
los within 0.175 dex of the nominal mass), but to avoid
bias arising from the underlying descendant halo distri-
bution, we weight descendant halos such that all mass
bins contribute equally. At each nominal descendant
mass and at eacha/a 0 <1, we evaluate the median
value ofM(a)/M(a 0), the progenitor mass in units of
the descendant mass. Note that inM ∗(a0) units, each
a0 is associated with a different mass resolution limitL.
We account for the inhomogeneous resolution limits by
using the product limit estimator of E. L. Kaplan & P.
Meier (1958) to evaluate the median. For visual clar-
ity, in figure 2 we multiply the medianM(a)/M(a 0) by
the bin-centerM(a 0), so the vertical axis represents the
growth history inM ∗ units. The statistical error is es-
sentially negligible for the field halos, but it is of concern
for the future subhalos due to their much lower sample
sizes. Thus, for the future subhalos, we additionally use
the method of R. Brookmeyer & J. Crowley (1982) to
estimate a 68 percent confidence interval on the median.
The nature of the subhalo bias is evident in figure 2.
The future subhalos, witha infall ≃1.11a 0, tend to be
“older” than field halos of the same mass, in the sense
that they grew more slowly in the past. At any fixed
progenitor timea/a 0 <1, the main progenitor of a fu-
ture subhalo ata 0 tends to be more massive than that of
a general field halo of the same mass at the same time.
This progenitor mass difference tends to be higher for
the cosmologies with more negative spectral indicesn,
and within a cosmology, it is higher for more massive de-
scendant halos ata 0. Generally, when framed in terms
of the progenitor masses at a fixed time, the subhalo
bias is stronger for halos that assembled more rapidly.

4.IMPLICATION FOR SUBHALO
CONCENTRATIONS
We have shown that halos that will become subha-
los in the future have biased assembly histories. But
a halo’s assembly history sets the radial profile of its
internal density; halos that grew more slowly are more
centrally concentrated in mass (e.g. J. F. Navarro et al.
1997; R. H. Wechsler et al. 2002; N. Dalal et al. 2010;
A. D. Ludlow et al. 2013; M. S. Delos et al. 2019). Con-
sequently, future subhalos are expected to be more cen-
trally concentrated than field halos of the same mass at
the same time. Indeed, M. S. Delos et al. (2025, ap-
pendix A) noticed in the simulations of M. S. Delos &
S. D. M. White (2023) that future subhalos are 10–20%
more concentrated than average field halos of the same
mass shortly before they become subhalos.

5.CONCLUSION
Halos destined to fall into a more massive host have
biased growth histories before infall. Their main-
progenitor masses and full progenitor mass functions
both lie above those of typical field halos of the same
mass at the same time.
We have characterized this pre-infall bias compactly
through a functionβ(D/D infall, a), whereDis the lin-
ear growth factor at scale factoraandD infall is the

14
growth factor at infall. Within EPS theory, the bias
corresponds to a modified collapse thresholdδ c →βδ c
for future subhalos. Calibrated against scale-free simu-
lations and a concordance cosmology,βtakes the simple
formβ(x, a) = (1−x) p+qa, withp= 1.20 from the scale-
free fit. Theqaterm accounts for the late-time influence
of dark energy; we findq= 0.14 for theM 200c mass def-
inition andq= 0.05 for theM 200m mass definition.
The bias propagates into subhalo properties. Because
halos that assembled earlier are more centrally concen-
trated, future subhalos are more concentrated than typ-
ical field halos of the same mass at the same time. Ap-
plying our model to the concentration-mass relation of
A. D. Ludlow et al. (2016) yields a 10–15% concentra-
tion excess shortly before infall, consistent with the di-
rect measurements of M. S. Delos et al. (2025). More
generally, the assembly history bias should be incorpo-
rated into subhalo population models, which currently
apply no pre-infall correction.
The pre-infall subhalo bias is the extreme case of the
more general phenomenon of assembly bias. In the EPS
picture,x=D/D infall has a direct interpretation: it is
the normalized linear-theory overdensityδ env/δc of the
surrounding region at progenitor timeD, sinceδ env =
δcD/Dinfall for a region collapsing atDinfall. Recast with
x=δ env/δc, the effective (1−x) p+qaδc threshold might
apply in general to halos in dense environments, not only
to future subhalos. We leave further exploration of this
point to future work.

A.NO SUBHALO BIAS FROM EXCURSION SET THEORY WITH TOP-HAT WINDOW
Here we show that excursion set theory cannot predict the subhalo bias, even when correlations between steps are
correctly accounted for. We use explicit calculations similar to those of M. S. Delos (2024). To set up the problem,
suppose we are interested in a halo of massM s merging onto a larger halo of massM h at linear growth factor
Dinfall. In the language of excursion set theory, this corresponds to a trajectoryδ(M) that is constrained such that
δ(Ms) =δ(M h) =δ c/Dinfall,δ(M)< δ c/Dinfall forM s < M < M h, andδ(M)< δ c/Dinfall forM > M h. Note that
these constraints imply dδ(M)/dM| M=M h
= 0.

a halo of massM sub to residing in a halo of massM host ≫M sub at redshiftz infall. For comparison, the orange curves show
trajectories without thez infall conditioning. The thick and thin dashed curves show the median and 16th/84th percentiles of
δ(M), respectively, while the solid curves and shading show the median and 16th/84th percentiles of the downward-running
maximum ofδ(M), which is the more relevant quantity for halo assembly histories in excursion set theory.
consider several combinations of the subhalo massM sub =M s, host massM host =M h, and infall redshiftz infall. We
randomly sample trajectoriesδ(M) using the methods of M. S. Delos (2024) and discard any trajectories that fail to
satisfyδ(M)< δ c/Dinfall for allM s < M < M h and allM > M h. We adoptδ c = 1.5 following M. S. Delos (2024), but
this has no impact on our results. We continue until we have sampled 3×10 4 trajectories for each scenario.
Figure 11 shows theδ(M) trajectories sampled in this manner (blue). We show both the median and 16th and 84th
percentiles ofδ(M) (dashed curves) along with the same quantiles of max M ′>M δ(M ′) (solid curves and shading). The
latter are more relevant for halo growth in the context of excursion set theory. Specifically, each trajectory corresponds
to a dark matter particle, and at the linear growth factorD, the massMof the halo hosting the particle satisfies

At fixed massM, trajectories with higher values of max M ′>M δ(M ′) correspond to particles whose halos achieved the

For comparison, we also sample 3×10 4 trajectories with the relaxed constraintsδ(M s) =δ c/Dinfall andδ(M)<
δc/Dinfall for allM > M s. These are the general field-halo analogues – they are constrained to produce a halo of
massM s at the same timeD infall but are not further constrained to infall into a halo of massM h at that time. These
trajectories are also shown in figure 11 (orange).
In figure 11, the unconstrained trajectories (orange) tend to lie above the constrained trajectories (blue) at any
fixed massM < M s. Within the excursion set theory interpretation, this would imply that general field halos tend to
have slower growth histories, with more mass accreted earlier, relative to future subhalos. This is exactly the opposite
trend from the subhalo bias seen in simulations. Hence, excursion set theory with correlated steps (assuming a top-hat
windowW) cannot explain the subhalo bias.
