ABSTRACT
Asteroseismology is the study of resonant oscillations of stars to infer their internal structure and
dynamics. It is also a powerful tool for precisely determining stellar parameters such as mass, radius,
surface gravity, and age. The ongoing TESS mission, with its nearly complete sky coverage, presents
a unique opportunity to uniformly probe stellar populations across the Milky Way. TESS is estimated
to have observed more than 300,000 oscillating red giants, most of which have one to two months
of observations. Given the scale of this dataset, we need a fast, efficient, and robust way to analyse
the data. In this work, our objective is to develop a machine learning (ML) based method to infer
asteroseismic parameters from short-duration observations. Specifically, we focus on two global seismic
parameters, the large frequency separation (∆ν) and the frequency at maximum power (ν max), from
one-month-long TESS observations of red giants. Meanwhile, for K2 data, our focus extends to infer-
ring the period spacings of dipolar gravity modes (∆Π 1), in addition to ∆νandν max. Our findings
demonstrate that our machine learning algorithm can accurately infer ∆νandν max for approximately
50% of samples created by taking one-month Kepler and K2 observations. For TESS one sector data
however, we recover reliable ∆νfor only about 23% of the stars. Additionally, we get reliable ∆Π 1
inferences for about 200 young red-giants from K2. For these ∆Π 1 inferences, we see a good match
with the well known ∆ν−∆Π 1 degenerate sequence observed in Kepler red-giants.
1.INTRODUCTION
Space-based missions such as CoRoT (Baglin et al.
2006), Kepler (Borucki et al. 2010), K2 (Howell et al.
2014) and TESS (Ricker et al. 2015) have provided
vast datasets of high-precision photometric observa-
tions. These datasets are expected to increase signifi-
cantly in size with upcoming missions such as PLATO
(Rauer et al. 2024). Precise determinations of fun-
damental stellar parameters such as mass, radius and
age are made possible using asteroseismology. Red-
giant asteroseismology is a powerful tool with which to
probe populations throughout the Milky Way, enabling
ensemble-scale galactic archaeology (Anders et al. 2017;
Silva Aguirre et al. 2018; Sharma et al. 2019; Miglio et al.
2021).
Red giants exhibit mixed modes, which behave as pres-

These mixed modes probe the stellar core and can place
strong constraints on stellar evolution (Bedding et al.
2011; Mosser et al. 2011). These modes also allow us
to infer period spacings of gravity modes, which are di-
rectly related to core mass (Montalb´ an et al. 2013).
Kepler has observed∼21,000 oscillating red giants
(Hon et al. 2019; Dhanpal et al. 2022) in a 100 square-
degree field in the northern hemisphere having four years
of continuous observations. K2 observed∼19,500 oscil-
lating red giants (Zinn et al. 2022) in 18 Kepler sized
fields along the ecliptic, each with three months of ob-
servation. TESS, with its vast coverage, is estimated to
have observed more than 300,000 oscillating red giants
(Mackereth et al. 2021) across the whole sky, with most
of them observed for a duration of one to two months.
The full sky coverage and an order of magnitude more
oscillating red-giants provided by TESS makes it possi-
ble to study populations almost uniformly throughout
the Milky Way.

and red giants observed by TESS using 1-2 sectors of
short-cadence data. Hatt et al. (2023) analyzed both 2-

identify 4,177 solar-like oscillators, reporting estimates

For long-cadence (∼30-minute) data, Silva Aguirre et al.
(2020) used 1–2 sectors of TESS observations for bright
red giants and detected oscillations in all stars in their
sample. Combining seismic inferences with Gaia DR2
parallaxes (Gaia Collaboration et al. 2018), they esti-
mated stellar masses, radii, and ages with typical uncer-
tainties of∼3%, 5%, and 20%, respectively. While these
precision levels are comparable to those reported for full-
length Kepler observations, it’s worth noting that the
dominant source of uncertainty differs: TESS results are
largely limited by statistical measurement errors due to
shorter time series, whereas for Kepler, systematic un-
certainties (e.g., in stellar models and scaling relations)
dominate.
Hon et al. (2022) analyzed bright HD catalog red
giants with 1–2 years of TESS observations near the
continuous viewing zones, achieving mass and radius
precisions of∼8% and∼3%. They also compared aster-
oseismic radii from scaling relations with interferometric
measurements for 16 stars, finding a median fractional
difference of only∼3%. Furthermore, Mackereth et al.
(2021) used up to 13 sectors of data near the TESS
continuous viewing zone in the southern hemisphere
and recovered a median uncertainty of∼8% in mass
and∼26% in age for a subset of the sample when using
Gaia-derived luminosities as additional constraints, in
combination with asteroseismic inferences. They also
show that these ages are precise enough to identify
trends in galactic populations. Hon et al. (2021) used
one sector TESS data and usedν max inferences in com-
bination with parallaxes, radial velocities and proper
motions from Gaia EDR3 (Gaia Collaboration et al.
2021) to obtain a near all-sky Gaia-asteroseismology
mass map, showing potential for all-sky galactic archae-
ology with TESS. Stello et al. (2022) analysed Kepler
stars observed by TESS in one to two sectors and recov-
eredν max and ∆νwith uncertainties of∼5% and∼3%
respectively. The short duration makes it challenging
to infer detailed seismic parameters such as ∆νand
they were able to reliably recover ∆νin only about 20%
(14% for one sector and 26% for two sectors) of the
population.
Given the vast amount of data from the ongoing TESS
mission — with even larger volumes expected from the
upcoming PLATO mission — there is a pressing need
for fast, efficient, and robust methods to infer seismic
parameters from photometric observations. Dhanpal
et al. (2022, 2023) have developed one such machine-
learning–based algorithm, which they applied to Kepler
red giants to infer a range of asteroseismic parameters,
includingν max, ∆ν, ∆Π1, andq, using the full four-year
Kepler data sets.
In this work, our goal is to explore the feasibility of
inferring these parameters from significantly shorter
time series using similar machine-learning techniques.
Specifically, we focus on (i) inferring ∆νandν max
from single-sector (∼27 days) TESS observations, and
(ii) inferring ∆ν,ν max, and ∆Π 1 from approximately
three-month K2 observations, where the longer baseline
provides sufficient resolution to recover mixed-mode
spacings. Previous work by Davies & Miglio (2016) has
already demonstrated that ∆Π 1 can be inferred from
up to 70 days of data (comparable to K2 campaigns
or multiple TESS sectors), but not from 27-day single-
sector light curves, based on analyses of varying time
series lengths for a young Kepler red giant.
2.DATA
2.1.TESS data
We use light-curves detrended using the co-trending
basis vector (CBV) correction method implemented in
the TASOC (TESS Asteroseismic Science Operations
Center) pipeline developed by the coordinated activ-
ity TESS Data for Asteroseismology (T’DA) within
the TESS Asteroseismic Science Consortium (TASC)
(Handberg et al. 2021; Lund et al. 2021). We cross-
matched the red giants identified by Hon et al. (2021)
and used light-curves recorded at a cadence of 30 min-
utes for all the available stars. Instead of raw QLP
data, which was used by Hon et al. (2021), we use
the detrended data from TASOC pipeline because it is
optimized for asteroseismology and preserves the stel-
lar oscillation signal better that QLP, which is opti-
mized for planet searches and removes stellar variabil-
ity over long timescales while performing corrections.
These detrended lightcurves from TASOC pipeline are
useful for inferring ∆νfrom one-month TESS observa-
tions. We perform sigma clipping to reject flux values
with more than 5-σdiscrepancy. Following a similar
procedure to Stello et al. (2015), we fill all gaps smaller
than 1.5 hours in length using linear interpolation to
minimize the impact of windowing Garc´ ıa et al. (2014).
Finally, we reject all the stars with greater than 30%
empty timestamps at this stage. We use the lightkurve

3
package (Lightkurve Collaboration et al. 2018) to down-
load light curves hosted at Mikulski Archive for Space
Telescopes (MAST) and compute Lomb-Scargle peri-
odograms (Lomb 1976; Scargle 1982; Press & Rybicki
1989). To make a uniform size sample, we use the same
frequency range to calculate the power spectral density
(PSD) for all the stars, which is to be fed into the neu-
ral network for analysis. In total, we have 30,720 TESS
red-giants for seismic analysis.
2.2.K2 data
We use detrended lightcurves form theK2SFFpipeline
(Vanderburg & Johnson 2014) which performs correc-
tions for trends related to spacecraft pointing and other
systematics. We take the long cadence (∼30 minutes)
data for red-giant stars listed in K2 GAP DR3 (Zinn
et al. 2022) and high-pass filter each lightcurve with a
boxcar filter of width 4 days. Next, we perform sigma
clipping to reject flux values with more than 5-σdiscrep-
ancy. Finally, we fill gaps shorter than 1.5 hours using
linear interpolation similar to TESS data (2.1). Again,
we use the lightkurve package to download light-curves
from MAST and compute Lomb-Scargle periodograms
for all these stars with the same frequency range. In to-
tal, we have 18,560 K2 red-giants with ∆νin the range
1−19µHz, out of which 2,176 lie within ∆νrange of
9−19µHz.
2.3.Training sets
2.3.1.For TESS Model
We combine red giant datasets from the Kepler and
K2 missions observations to train a model designed for
application to one-month TESS observations; we here-
after refer to this as theTESS-model. For K2, we use
data from K2 GAP DR3 (Zinn et al. 2022), which pro-
vided asteroseismic measurements of ∆νandν max for
19,417 red giants. For Kepler, we use observations of
21,144 red giants, identified by (Dhanpal et al. 2022),
who have also provided measurements of ∆νandν max
for these stars. To create training samples comparable
in resolution to TESS 1-sector data, we divided each
4-year (in case of Kepler) and 3-month (in case of K2)
light-curve into multiple, non-overlapping, 27-day seg-
ments. Due to the stochastic excitation of oscillations,
each of these shorter-duration lightcurves has a unique
noise profile. This stochastic behaviour may also lead to
some of these shorter light-curves not showing solar-like
oscillations.
Again, we follow the same procedure as described in 2.1,
filling gaps smaller than 1.5 hrs in duration using linear
interpolation and discarding samples having more than
30% empty data points at the end. Following this, we
have 822,274 samples in total, 768,267 from Kepler and
53,980 from K2. We use 70% of these data for training,
15% for validation and the remaining 15% as a test set.
The decision to use observations rather than synthetic
data for training is motivated by the well-established
links between granulation timescales, oscillation ampli-
tudes, and the location of oscillation power within the
PSD (Kallinger & Matthews 2010; Huber et al. 2011;
Kjeldsen & Bedding 2011; Mathur et al. 2011; Chaplin
et al. 2011; Yu et al. 2018). This was also emphasized
by Hon et al. (2018), who leveraged binned 2D images
of the PSD to detect oscillations and estimateν max. In-
corporating these empirical relationships can potentially
help the neural network better identify oscillation signa-
tures, especially in short-duration datasets like TESS,
where poor frequency resolution and low signal-to-noise
ratios pose additional challenges. We also experimented
with training a model on synthetic data, but it signifi-
cantly underperformed compared to the model trained
on observations when evaluated on the Kepler-as-TESS
test set. This highlights key differences between syn-
thetic and real data, particularly at low resolution and
SNR. However, training on observations carries the risk
of the model internalizing ∆ν–ν max,τ conv–νmax, and
amplitude-scaling relations that may not hold perfectly
in the TESS sample. However, as shown by Stello et al.
(2022), the overall offsets between Kepler and TESS
measurements ofν max and ∆νare small when aver-
aged over the population. Nevertheless, there are mild
evolutionary-state–dependent trends, with TESSν max
values being typically 2–3% lower for red clump stars
nearν max ∼40, µHz, and indications of a small positive
bias at highν max for red-giant–branch stars. However,
the training set remains broadly representative for our
purposes, apart from instrument-specific effects such as
Earthshine and other systematics.
2.3.2.For K2 Model
Unlike theTESS Model, theK2 Models—which are in-
tended for use with three-month K2 observations (see
Section 3.4)—are trained entirely on synthetic datasets.
This approach is necessary due to the scarcity of ob-
served ∆Π1 measurements for red giants, which limits
the feasibility of training directly on Kepler or TESS
data. The synthetic spectra are generated using the sim-
ulator developed by Othman Benomar (Benomar 2023),
based on the asymptotic theory of stellar oscillations
(Aerts et al. 2010; Garc´ ıa & Ballot 2019). The theoreti-
cal framework and detailed methodology for generating
these synthetic datasets are described in Appendix A.
We construct two separate datasets to train two distinct
models (see Section 3.4 for details), each comprising ap-

these, 82.5% are used for training, 15% for validation,
and 2.5% (approximately 320,000 samples) for testing.
Both datasets span the same range of parameters, ex-

3.METHODS
We train deep neural networks to infer seismic pa-
rameters directly from PSD profiles—using one-month
TESS observations for theTESS Model, and three-
month K2 observations forK2 Model-1andModel-2,
collectively referred to as theK2 Models(see Section
3.4 for details). Inputs to all the models are 1-D arrays
of normalized power from the PSDs, without any back-
ground correction or feature extraction, and outputs are
probability distributions for each parameter (see section
3.1). All the relevant features are learned by the neu-
ral networks on their own from the full PSD. Although
theTESS ModelandK2 Modelsdiffer in terms of kernel
sizes and number of output parameters, the overall ar-
chitecture is the same for all the models (see section 3.2).
3.1.Converting regression problem to classification
Identifying parameters corresponding to the PSD of a
given star constitutes a regression problem. However, in
our approach, we divided the continuous range of each
parameter into a fixed number of bins and trained the
model to classify the correct bin number based on the
given PSD, effectively recasting the regression task as
a classification problem. The model then outputs an
array of probabilities, representing the likelihood that
each parameter falls into a specific bin.
There are several scientific and practical reasons for this
choice. First, classification can improve predictive accu-
racy, particularly when the relationship between inputs
and outputs is highly non-linear (Stewart et al. 2022) or
when the output parameters span many orders of magni-
tude (as is often the case in asteroseismology). Second,
classification inherently respects physical constraints —
for example, it avoids predicting unphysical negative val-
ues for quantities like ∆νor ∆Π1, which sometimes arise
in unconstrained regression models. Lastly, but most
importantly, formulating the inference as a classification
problem allows us to recover a probabilistic distribution
over the parameter space, giving us access not just to
a point prediction but to a meaningful measure of the
uncertainty and possible multimodality in the inferred
values.
As established by Richard & Lippmann (1991), neural
network classifiers trained with one-hot encoded target
vectors and a cross-entropy loss function yield outputs
that estimate Bayesian posterior probabilities. In this
context, the output probability distribution over the
bins can be interpreted as a quantized approximation
to the posterior distribution of the parameter, condi-
tioned on the observed data and the learned model. To
validate this capability of the ML models we have shown
a comparison of output probabilities from these models
with the posteriors obtained using MCMC for a Kepler
red giant at K2 resolution (∼88 days observations) in
section 5.1.
While ML output represents a Bayesian posterior, it is
not the same posterior that can be obtained using meth-
ods such as Markov-Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC). The
uncertainties derived from the present distribution are
systematically limited due to the finite sizes of the bins,
number of training samples used and network complex-
ity. Consequently, MCMC fits could generally provide
more accurate values and more precise uncertainties for
these stars.
Nonetheless, this method effectively provides an em-
pirical distribution over the parameter range, from
which we can extract not only the most probable value
(the mode) but also meaningful uncertainty estimates
(which are reasonably well calibrated– as reflected by the
empirical 1σfractions close to the ideal value of 0.683 in
subsequent sections). This offers a significant advan-
tage over standard regression based neural networks,
which typically do not provide any probability distri-
bution directly. Although techniques like k-fold cross-
validation can be employed to estimate uncertainties,
this approach requires training multiple instances of the
model, substantially increasing training time. Further-
more, this approach often fails to fully capture complex
multi-modal behavior, which presents a significant limi-
tation in our specific application because the asteroseis-
mic parameters, especially ∆Π1, can exhibit multimodal
distributions. Alternatively, mixture density networks
(Bishop 1994; Hon et al. 2020) can be used to obtain
probability distributions, but these necessitate a pre-
defined number of modes and are susceptible to mode

3.2.Model architecture
In our initial tests, we explored a range of neu-
ral network architectures, including a vanilla CNN
(Convolutional Neural Network), CNN-LSTM (com-
bining CNNs with Long Short-Term Memory layers),
ResNet (Residual Networks), and Vision Transform-
ers (ViT). These architectures were chosen to represent
both traditional convolution-based models and more re-
cent attention-based models, allowing us to assess their
relative strengths for the task of inferring seismic pa-
rameters from photometric power spectra.
Among these, ResNet, which augments CNNs with skip
(residual) connections between layers, consistently out-
performed the other architectures in both validation loss
and stability. We attribute this to ResNet’s ability to
mitigate vanishing gradient problems and efficiently cap-
ture both local and global patterns in the PSD, which
proved particularly useful given the multi-scale features

3.3.Fine-tuning model hyperparameters
To further optimize model performance, we tuned key
hyperparameters such as kernel size, learning rate, num-
ber of ResNet blocks, and dropout rate.
Based on the results from a grid search, for theTESS
Model, we used a smaller kernel size of 21 (compared
to 51 for theK2 Models) which account for the lower
frequency resolution in the one-month TESS datasets.
This choice allows the model to focus on narrower spec-
tral features without oversmoothing. We also found that
a higher dropout rate of 0.4 was necessary for theTESS
Model(compared to 0.1 for theK2 Models) to miti-
gate overfitting, due to significant limitations on train-
ing data available for TESS. Additionally, the number
of ResNet blocks was tuned to balance model complex-
ity against overfitting risk, leading to the selection of a
slightly shallower configuration.
The learning rate and batch size were held constant
across models (see Section 3.4) after confirming that
these settings provided stable convergence in all cases.
Throughout this tuning process, the final hyperparam-
eter choices were determined by the configuration that
yielded the lowest validation loss while maintaining con-
sistent performance across multiple training runs.
These design and tuning decisions were guided by em-
pirical performance on the validation set and repeated
testing, ensuring that the final models are robust, well-
calibrated, and capable of generalizing effectively to un-
seen data.
3.4.Training
We feed the normalized power from the PSD as in-
put to the model, along with the corresponding one-hot-
encoded vectors (bin numbers) for the target output pa-
rameters (∆νandν max for theTESS Model; ∆ν,ν max,
∆Π1, andqfor theK2 Models). The models are trained
as supervised classifiers with the objective of minimiz-
ing the categorical cross-entropy loss across all output
parameters.
For optimization, we use the ADAM optimizer, which
we selected for its robustness and efficiency in handling
sparse gradients and adaptive learning rates. To im-
prove convergence, we apply a cosine decay with restarts
schedule on the learning rate, which periodically resets
the learning rate to avoid local minima and encourage
exploration during training.
Training is conducted on 2 GPUs, with an effective ini-
tial learning rate of 1.5×10 −4/2 (scaled by the num-
ber of GPUs) and a batch size of 64 per GPU, bal-
ancing memory constraints with stable gradient esti-
mates. To ensure the model achieves optimal conver-
gence while mitigating overfitting, we implement early
stopping based on the validation loss. Training is halted
if the validation loss fails to improve for five consecu-
tive epochs (patience = 5), allowing the model sufficient
opportunity to learn meaningful patterns without over-
training on noise or spurious correlations. Throughout
training, the validation loss is continuously monitored,
and the model state corresponding to the lowest ob-
served validation loss is saved as a checkpoint. This
checkpointed model—representing the best generaliza-
tion performance on unseen data—is then used for in-
ference on both the test dataset and subsequent appli-
cation samples.
3.4.1.TESS Model
We trained theTESS Modelusing a one-month
Kepler/K2-as-TESS dataset to predictν max and ∆ν,
spanning the ranges 8–283µHz and 1–19µHz, respec-
tively. The bin sizes are set to 2µHz forν max and 0.1
µHz for ∆ν. Details on the rationale behind these bin
sizes are provided in Appendix B. During training, both
parameters are given equal weight in the loss function to
ensure the model learns to infer them with comparable
accuracy and equal attention.
3.4.2.K2 Models
For K2 data, we trained two separate models, each
tailored to different parameter ranges, but with identical
architectures. Both models outputν max, ∆ν, ∆Π 1, and
q, differing only in the parameter ranges they cover:
(i)K2 Model-1operates over a ∆νrange of 1−19µHz
and aν max range of 8−283µHz.
(ii)K2 Model-2operates over a ∆νrange of 9−19µHz
and aν max range of 88−283µHz.
K2 Model-1is primarily optimized to inferν max and
∆ν, each assigned a weight of 0.4. We also include ∆Π 1
andqwith smaller weights of 0.1 each. Our exper-
iments show that including these two parameters im-
proves model performance by reducing outliers and bet-
ter matching theν max and ∆νvalues from K2 GAP
DR3. These parameters likely helps the model better
distinguish mixed modes and avoid mislabeling.
K2 Model-2is composed of two sub-models: one fo-
cused onν max and ∆ν, with weights (0.4, 0.4, 0.1, 0.1)
assigned respectively toν max, ∆ν, ∆Π 1, andq; and an-
other focused on ∆Π1 andq, with weights (0.1, 0.1, 0.4,

8
0.4). For predictions, we use theν max and ∆νoutputs
from the first sub-model, and the ∆Π 1 andqoutputs
from the second.
The bin sizes for all theK2 Modelsmodels are set to
1µHz forν max, 0.05µHz for ∆ν, 1.25 seconds for ∆Π 1,
and 0.025 forq. The methodology for determining these
bin sizes is described in Appendix B.
The need for different models arises from the difficulty
of inferring ∆Π1 from just three months of observations.
While Dhanpal et al. (2023) demonstrated that all four
parameters can be inferred from a single model using 4-
year Kepler data, shorter observations pose significant
challenges. In particular, measuring ∆Π 1 for evolved
red giants and red-clump stars is complicated by the
decreasing spacing between successive radial orders and
increased granulation noise at low frequencies. Addi-
tionally, as stars evolve, mixed-mode inertia increases,
reducing mode detectability (Grosjean et al. 2014), and
mixed mode density decreases with the onset of helium
burning (Gehan et al. 2018), further complicating pe-
riod spacing measurements.
Initially, we attempted to infer ∆Π 1 across the full ∆ν
range of 1−19µHz, but useful results were obtained
only for stars with ∆ν >9µHz. This led to training
a separate model specifically for the 9−19µHz range,
which proved more effective in inferring ∆Π1 within this
domain. Therefore, all ∆Π 1 inferences presented here
are derived exclusively fromK2 Model-2, which targets

4.RESULTS
In this section, we first compare the performance of
all models on their respective test sets to evaluate and
validate prediction accuracy. Subsequently, we apply
the models to the target observational datasets—TESS
data for theTESS Modeland K2 data for theK2
Models—and present the results of reliable inferences
in the form of tables, intended for prospective users.
For these tests, we report the mode of the output prob-
ability distributions as the predicted value for each pa-
rameter. We choose the mode because the predicted
distributions can be broad and could also exhibit mul-
timodal behaviour, particularly for ∆Π 1. In such cases,
the mode — as the most probable class — offers a more
representative estimate than the mean or median, both
of which can lie in low-probability regions or fall be-
tween distinct peaks. Moreover, due to the typically
peaked nature of the softmax outputs in most cases, the
mode and median often coincide.
To quantify uncertainty, we compute the difference be-
tween the values at the 16th and 84th percentiles rela-
tive to the 50th percentile (median) of the output dis-
tribution. The total uncertainty is then defined as the
absolute sum of these two differences. This approach
provides both a sharp point prediction (via the mode)
and a meaningful measure of the spread and asymme-
try in the predicted distribution (via the percentiles).
Furthermore, to assess the calibration and reliability of
the reported uncertainties, we use normalized residual,
which is defined as:

Since our ML models generate predictions for all pro-
cessed stars without prior vetting, we apply empiri-
cally defined uncertainty thresholds to filter and retain
only the most reliable inferences. These thresholds vary
across models and are determined based on test set per-
formance, by evaluating how closely the model predic-
tions align with reference values. We analyze how this
agreement degrades with increasing uncertainty and se-
lect cutoff values—typically between 5% and 20%—that
preserve predictive accuracy while minimizing the num-
ber of outliers.
For example, in the Kepler/K2-as-TESS test set, we find
that using aν max uncertainty threshold of 20% retains
approximately 85% of the sample, for which 99.1% of
predictions have relative errors below 20%. Reducing
the threshold to 15% lowers the yield to 78%, while im-
proving accuracy only marginally to 99.4%. Given this
trade-off, we adopt the 20% threshold in this case.
Thus, the selected cuts strike a balance between maxi-
mizing the usable sample size and maintaining high con-
fidence in the reported parameters.

4.1.1.Kepler/K2-as-TESS test set
We first validate the performance of theTESS Model
using a test set composed of one-month segments from
Kepler/K2 red-giant observations. This dataset was ex-
cluded from both training and validation, making it an
unbiased benchmark. The set constitutes approximately
15% of the full Kepler/K2-as-TESS dataset, totalling
112,640 samples. However, we note that, the values re-
ported by Dhanpal et al. (2022) for Kepler red giants are
derived using a similar machine learning technique and
it is desirable to compare the present inferences with
classical methods. Hence, we make use of the Yu et al.
(2018) catalogue for Kepler giants, where the authors
used the SYD pipeline to determineν max and ∆νand
visually verified the results for approximately 16,000 red
giant stars. For K2 red giants, we use values from K2
GAP DR3 (Zinn et al. 2022), which combines results
from multiple pipelines including A2Z (Mathur et al.
2010), BAM (Zinn et al. 2019), CAN (Kallinger et al.
2010), COR (Mosser & Appourchaux 2009) and SYD
(Huber et al. 2009). Of the 112,640 segment-based sam-
ples, we find only 84,480 in these two catalogues, which
makes our final test set for this comparison.
To identify reliable inferences, we select stars with pre-
dictedν max uncertainties below 20%, resulting in a fil-
tered subset of 79,421 stars (about 94% of the total).
Figure 3a shows a comparison between model predic-
tions and reference values from classical catalogues men-
tioned above, derived from time series that are 3 times
loner for K2 and 48 times for Kepler (which make major-
ity of the sample). Among the filtered stars, 78.4% agree
to within uncertainties and 99.6% have relative errors
inν max below 20% (see fig 3b), demonstrating that the
TESS Modelcan accurately inferν max from one-month
Kepler/K2 observations. Furthermore, the empirical 1σ
fraction is 0.683, indicating that theν max uncertainties
are perfectly calibrated for this reliable set.
To validate the ∆νperformance, we apply an additional
uncertainty threshold: only stars with ∆νuncertainties
below 10% are retained. This further narrows the subset
to 67,424 stars, which is roughly 79.8% of the original
test set. In this subset, 84.5% of stars agree with the
reference values within uncertainties, and 98.3% exhibit
relative errors in ∆νbelow 10% (see fig 4b). The empir-
ical 1σfraction for this set is 0.772, indicating that the
reported ∆νuncertainties are slightly overestimated on
average, and therefore conservative. This confirms the
model’s ability to recover ∆νreliably from one-month
observations within this more restricted subset and give
realistic estimates of uncertainty. Figure 4a also dis-
plays this comparison, with predictions color-coded by
confidence, defined as the maximum value of the output
probability distribution for a given parameter and star.
The increased scatter in fractional residuals for stars
with ∆ν≲5µHz is expected: small misclassifica-
tions (e.g., by one or two bins) in this low-frequency
regime lead to larger relative errors compared to the
high-frequency regime. This effect is compounded by
the reduced frequency spacing between radial modes at
low ∆ν, where peaks lie closer together and convective
background noise becomes more dominant. These chal-
lenges are exacerbated by the limited frequency resolu-
tion of one-month-long light curves. The higher aver-
age confidence in this low-∆νregime is also explained
by the stricter filtering imposed by the same 5% un-
certainty threshold in the low-frequency regime, which
retains only high-certainty cases in this range. Taken
together, these two effects may give the visual impres-
sion that stars with higher prediction confidence have
larger fractional residuals. However, statistical analysis
reveals the opposite: the Pearson correlation between
confidence and absolute fractional residual is -0.263 (p
<0.0001), and the Spearman correlation is -0.365 (p<
0.0001), indicating a statistically significant inverse re-
lationship. However, the correlation is weak to moder-
ate, suggesting that the relationship between confidence
and absolute fractional residual might not be strictly
linear. Nevertheless, it suggests that, on average, model
predictions with higher confidence have lower absolute
fractional residual.
Additionally, in appendix C we show that ∆νvalues
returned by the model are not simply derived based on
the ∆ν−ν max relation for solar-like oscillators (Stello
et al. 2009), which is inherent in the training sample
made of Kepler and K2 red giants. In fact, the model is
inferring ∆νwith much higher precision than is expected
when relying on the ∆ν−ν max scaling. We therefore ar-
gue that this is achieved using the underlying pattern of
frequency spacing in the PSD. In order to explicitly test
and verify that the model can indeed infer ∆νwithout
νmax inputs, we train a ∆ν-only model. The predictions
of this ∆ν-only model match with the values returned
byTESS Model(which has both ∆νandν max), see ap-
pendix C for comparison.

4.2.2.Kepler-as-K2
To validate theK2 Model-2predictions of ∆Π 1 on
observational data, we use three-month segments of Ke-
pler light curves for young red giants and compare the
inferred values with those from stars in common with
the catalogue of Kuszlewicz et al. (2023).
As also noted previously, the short observational base-
line of three months inherently limits the reliability of
∆Π1 estimates for many stars, since only a small number
of dipole mixed modes are typically detectable—even for
targets with relatively high signal-to-noise ratios.
To isolate reliable predictions, we apply quality cuts
based on fractional uncertainties: specifically, we retain
stars with ∆Π 1 uncertainties below 10% andquncer-
tainties below 0.12. We further exclude predictions in
the lowest and highest ∆Π 1 bins, as these often reflect
boundary effects—cases where the model assigns values
at the edges of the training range, thus being limited by
the imposed bounds rather than the data. Additionally,
we discard stars withq <0.05, as predictions in this
regime are particularly prone to failure.
This lower threshold onqis motivated by the find-
ings of Dhanpal et al. (2023), who showed that both
machine learning and MCMC-based inferences become
increasingly unreliable whenqis very small. Further-
more, Ong & Gehan (2023) demonstrated that when
the spacing between gravity modes exceeds the width of
the avoided crossing (which shrinks asqdecreases), the
mixed modes no longer adequately sample the stretch-
ing function. This results in poor constraints on the
coupling strength, and might also reduce the reliability
of ∆Π1 estimates for such stars.
The uncertainty thresholds were determined empiri-
cally by testing model performance on a this test set of
three-month Kepler observations. These cuts were op-
timized to balance sample size and reliability, ensuring
that the final set of retained stars exhibits high predic-
tion accuracy and a low fraction of unidentified outliers.
This filtering is essential to extract meaningful ∆Π 1 in-
ferences from three-month datasets with limited reso-
lution. Full details on determining the ∆Π 1 selection
threshold are presented in appendix D.
Out of the 59,904 samples (where multiple PSDs orig-
inate from different three-month segments of the same
star), 13,297 have matches in Kuszlewicz et al. (2023).
These 13,297 samples correspond to 775 unique red-
giant stars. Selecting at most one PSD per star and
applying the quality cuts yields a final sample of 191
stars. Figure 7a shows comparisons of ∆Π 1 for these
191 stars, with values inferred by theK2 Model-2from
three-month segments of Kepler observations on the y-

based on analyses of full four-year long Kepler obser-
vations on the x-axis. We observe a good agreement
in the inferred ∆Π 1 values, except for 8 stars, which
are showing significant discrepancies. Figure 7b shows
the histogram of relative errors for non-anomalous stars
in this sample,∼98% of which have relative errors less
than 2%. Figure 8 illustrates the ∆ν–∆Π 1 distribution
for these stars, where most follow the well-established
degenerate sequence (Vrard et al. 2016; Deheuvels et al.
2022). Furthermore, the empirical 1σfraction is 0.78 for
this subset, which goes to show that the reported un-
certainties are, on average, conservative and therefore
reliable. The mean and median normalized residuals

that there is no overall bias, and reinforcing the reliabil-

Nine stars are flagged as anomalous— eight of which are
the stars that show discrepancies with Kuszlewicz et al.

Notably, these secondary peaks lie along the ∆ν–∆Π 1

those reported by Kuszlewicz et al. (2023), which may
contribute to the ambiguity observed in their ∆Π 1 esti-
mates. A more detailed investigation of this discrepancy
is beyond the scope of the present study and will be ad-
dressed in future work.

5.DISCUSSION
5.1.MCMC and ML Comparison
To validate the capability of our ML models to approx-
imate Bayesian posterior distributions, we compare the
network outputs with posteriors obtained using Markov
Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) analysis for the Kepler
red giant KIC 11912315, truncated to K2-like resolution
(∼88 days of observation). We employ the Metropolis-
Hastings algorithm (Metropolis et al. 1953; Hastings
1970) to sample the posterior, assuming a likelihood
based onχ 2 with 2 degrees of freedom, given the ob-
served power spectral density (PSD) and a synthetic
modelM(ν,Θ). This methodology follows the approach
summarized in Dhanpal et al. (2023), based on the for-
malism described in Benomar et al. (2009). We refer the
reader to these works for further details on the MCMC
implementation. Fig. 10 displays the best-fit MCMC
model (red) overlaid on the smoothed data (black); the
PSD obtained from the full 4-year Kepler observation
is shown in gray for reference. Fig. 11 presents the
comparison between the MCMC posteriors and theK2
Modeloutputs forν max, ∆ν, ∆Π 1 andq. To facilitate
visual comparison, the MCMC posteriors have been nor-
malized to match the peak probability of the ML out-
puts. We observe that the ML outputs and MCMC pos-
teriors overlap and agree well within uncertainties for all
parameters. Notably, the ML output forν max exhibits a
bimodal structure with peaks roughly separated by ∆ν.
This suggests the model detects an ambiguity in the
νmax inference, identifying two different sets of modes
for potential Gaussian envelope for the oscillation signal
separated by one radial order. To better illustrate the
model’s capability to approximate the underlyingν max
posteriors, Appendix G presents direct MCMC compar-
isons for six additional stars.
Beyond theν max multi-modality, the ML distribu-
tion for all parameters except ∆νare broader than the
MCMC posterior. This difference in posterior width is
not unexpected. The MCMC algorithm uses significant
computational resources (thousands of steps) to map
the likelihood surface for a given target, allowing for
a highly optimized exploration of the parameter space.
In contrast, the ML model provides an instantaneous,
generalized inference. The exact difference between the
posteriors depends on many factors such as bin sizes
for models, training set size, network complexities, and
number of steps in MCMC and its convergence criteria.
Therefore, the systematically broader ML distributions
are a natural consequence of these methodological differ-
ences, and the ML uncertainties may be interpreted as
being more conservative. A detailed comparison disen-

For bin convergence exercises (appendix B), we utilized
one-hot encoding across all models. While this approach
is straightforward for models with larger bin sizes, uti-
lizing one-hot encoding to describe the PSD parameters
at the finest bin sizes presents a theoretical challenge.
This issue arises because the intrinsic noise in the PSD
limits the precision achievable by an ideal theoretical
sampler, effectively broadening the posterior. Conse-
quently, the “ideal” theoretical bin size would need to
vary dynamically from target to target depending on
the local signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). However, fixing a
conservatively large bin size globally to accommodate
the lowest-SNR targets would be counterproductive, as
it would artificially degrade the predictive resolution for
the high-SNR targets that are of primary scientific in-
terest.
To assess the extent of this theoretical limitation across
our datasets, we note the approximate intrinsic preci-
sion limits dictated by the observational baselines. For
3-month K2 data, these theoretical limits are roughly
0.68µHz forν max, 0.04µHz for ∆ν, 0.1 seconds for
∆Π1, and 0.01 forq. For shorter 1-month TESS ob-
servations, the intrinsic noise floor is naturally higher,
with precision limits of roughly 1.1µHz forν max and
0.07µHz for ∆ν. When utilizing bin sizes smaller than
these thresholds for noise-dominated spectra, represent-
ing the target with a single one-hot bin strains the strict
mathematical preconditions required for pure Bayesian
interpretation.
To address this, we rely on tested machine learning prac-
tice and prioritize high-resolution precision. For models
trained on synthetic data (e.g., theK2 Models), the true
underlying parameters are known exactly. In such cases,
while treating the target as a strict one-hotδ-function is
a formal theoretical approximation, empirical evidence
across astrophysical machine learning demonstrates that
prioritizing high-resolution mapping is a proven, effec-
tive strategy (e.g., Shuntov et al. 2020; Pasquet et al.
2019; Rothe et al. 2015). Rather than artificially widen-
ing the bins, the network implicitly learns the posterior
width dynamically from the variance of the training en-
semble. During training, the model encounters many
similar noisy input spectra corresponding to slightly dif-
ferent exact ground-truth labels. This forces the global
minimum to naturally distribute the softmax probabil-
ity mass across multiple adjacent bins.
The empirical behavior of the network under this ap-
proximation is demonstrated in our convergence exer-
cises (Appendix B; Tables 3 and 4), where model per-
formance metrics (e.g., normalized residuals, median un-
certainties and 1-sigma fractions) transition smoothly
without abrupt changes or qualitative degradation, even
as bin sizes are reduced below the aforementioned in-
trinsic precision limits dictated by the noise. While
this empirical robustness does not substitute for formal
Bayesian correctness at extreme bin sizes sizes, it un-
derscores the network’s predictive stability when pushed
beyond its theoretical constraints.
Crucially, we emphasize that this theoretical limitation
primarily affects the extreme lower bounds of our con-
vergence exercises. As shown in Appendix B, the Me-
dian Squared Error (MdnSE) for all parameters — in
both theK2andTESSmodels — converges at bin sizes
that are safelylargerthan these intrinsic precision lim-
its (see Figures 12 and 13, and Tables 3 and 4). As
the network achieves optimal performance before reach-
ing the average noise floor, the final asteroseismic pa-
rameters reported in this work are derived from models
utilizing bin sizes that are larger than the intrinsic preci-
sion limits of the PSDs. Consequently, the outputs from
our final models are not limited due to this theoretical
training constraint and remain robustly interpretable as
Bayesian posterior probabilities.
Additionally, for models trained directly on observa-
tional data (e.g., theTESS Model), the “ground truth”
labels were derived from cataloged central values and
implemented as one-hot encoded targets, effectively col-
lapsing the known uncertainties of the reference catalogs
into single discrete bins. Treating cataloged central val-
ues as exact training targets is a widely used practice
in astrophysical machine learning (e.g., Leung & Bovy
2019; Fabbro et al. 2018). Specifically within astero-
seismology, deep neural networks have been successfully
used in regression tasks for continuous parameters by
utilizing central catalog values as exact ground-truth la-
bels (e.g., Hon et al. 2018, 2021). Thus, while framing
this specific task as a discretized classification problem
introduces an artificial source of symmetric label noise,
deep neural networks are fundamentally mathematically
robust to such noise when trained across large ensembles
(Rolnick et al. 2017).
Nevertheless, we acknowledge that this discretization
inherently acts as an artificial source of label noise.
Consequently, the network may struggle to infer pos-
terior widths that are narrower than the intrinsic uncer-
tainties present in the original training catalogs. Our
training data contains heterogeneous precision; for in-
stance, the subset from Dhanpal et al. (2022) is inher-
ently resolution-limited to 0.1µHz in ∆νand 2µHz in
νmax, whereas the K2 GAP DR3 catalog is not. How-
ever, as established during our model validation, the net-
work optimizes its weights using the higher-precision K2
data to generalize beyond the limits of the coarser sub-

19
sets. When benchmarked against high-precision test sets
(Table 3), the model achieves median absolute errors of
0.04µHz for ∆νand 1.5µHz forν max. These predictive
errors are significantly smaller than the resolution lim-
its of the training subset used by Dhanpal et al. (2022),
confirming that the model successfully internalizes the
highest available precision and is not bottlenecked by
heterogeneous label quality.
Finally, in practical terms, this theoretical constraint
is negligible for our specific application. Because the
TESS Modelis applied to short, one-month observations
but was trained on significantly more precise values de-
rived from longer-baseline data, the intrinsic observa-
tional noise of the 1-month TESS spectra naturally dom-
inates over the variance floor inherited from the training
labels.
6.SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS
We have developed deep learning–based methods to
infer key asteroseismic parameters from short-duration
photometric observations. These models take normal-
ized power spectral densities (PSDs) as input and output
probability distributions for each parameter, learning
relevant features directly from the data—without requir-
ing background fitting or mode identification. Specifi-
cally, we target the inference ofν max and ∆νfrom one-
sector TESS-like observations, and ∆Π 1, in addition to
these two, from three-month K2 light curves.
TheTESS Modelis trained on one-month PSD seg-
ments derived from Kepler and K2 red giant light curves.
For theK2 Models, we generate synthetic datasets to
support training across a wide range of evolutionary
stages. We validate each model on its respective test
sets using observations and apply empirically derived
quality-based selection criteria to isolate reliable predic-
tions. In addition, we assess the robustness of theK2
Modelson a separate synthetic test set using the same
filtering strategy.
Across the various comparison sets, the empirical 1σ
fraction typically ranges from 0.63 to 0.80. This sug-
gests that the reported uncertainties are, in most cases,
conservatively estimated or reasonably well calibrated.
Our main results are summarized below:
•Validation on Kepler/K2 One-Month Segments:

B.CHOOSING BIN SIZE
To determine the optimal bin sizes for our network architectures, we conducted convergence exercises by training
multiple model instances with progressively finer bins. We compared performance across several diagnostics: Median
Squared Error (MdnSE), empirical 1σfraction, mean/median and standard deviation of normalized residuals, median
predicted uncertainty, and the reliable fraction (the number of confident inferences). Together, these metrics capture
the effects of binning resolution on model accuracy, precision, calibration, and outlier behavior. We utilized MdnSE
rather than Mean Squared Error because it is significantly more robust to extreme outliers, thereby more accurately
reflecting the model’s baseline performance across diverse bin configurations.
While strictly varying one parameter at a time across our entire multi-dimensional grid constitutes an ideal controlled
experiment. Our convergence exercises primarily utilize a multi-variable sweep to efficiently map the parameter space
without training approximately 60 separate models, supplemented by targeted single-parameter control models for
most sensitive parameters (discussed below) to demonstrate empirical robustness of this approach.
Finally, as detailed in Section 5.2, we note that utilizing strict one-hot encoding at finest bin sizes stretches the
theoretical preconditions for interpreting model outputs when the bin width falls below the intrinsic noise limit of the
data. We refer the reader to Section 5.2 for a comprehensive discussion on this trade-off between empirical precision
and formal mathematical correctness.
B.1.ForTESS Model
For determining the optimal bin sizes for ∆νandν max in theTESS Model, we evaluated the series of models detailed
in Table 3. All models in these models were trained on one-monthKeplerandK2segments and evaluated on a
held-out test set (section 2.3.1). The same uncertainty-based quality cuts described in section 4.1 were used to define
reliable inferences, except that for ∆νwe used only its uncertainty cut to avoid any dependencies onν max. All the
matrices shown in table 3 were evaluated on stars within these reliable subsets among the full set of 84,480 samples.

As bins become finer, theMdnSEdecreases sharply and then plateaus (figure 12), indicating a limit set by the data
and model. Forν max, theMdnSEfalls from 48.82 at 32µHz to 2.37 at 2µHz, with no significant improvement at
finer bins. For ∆ν, it decreases from 0.088 to 0.003 at 0.1µHz and then no significant improvements. This identifies
a natural “knee” near 2µHz and 0.1µHz forν max and ∆ν, respectively. The empirical 1σfraction—representing the
spread in the posterior—drops from∼0.82 at coarse bins to∼0.63 at 0.125µHz forν max (and from∼0.83 to∼0.67
for ∆ν), showing that posteriors become sharper and uncertainties smaller as bins refine. Correspondingly, the median
predicted uncertainty decreases monotonically (e.g., from∼22.3 to∼2.9µHz forν max, and from∼1.15 to∼0.1µHz
for ∆ν). However, the standard deviations of normalized residuals increase sharply beyond certain bin widths (e.g.,
from∼1.6 at 2µHz to>2.5 at 1µHz forν max), indicating underestimated uncertainties and more outliers. Trimmed
standard deviations (after removing the top and bottom 5% residuals) confirm that much of this excess spread arises
from a few outliers, while the mean and median normalized residuals develop noticeable biases for bins finer than this
limit.
Considering all diagnostics, bin widths of 2µHz forν max and 0.1µHz for ∆νrepresent the optimal balance between
resolution, precision, and calibration. Finer bins do not yield better accuracy (MdnSEalready plateaued) but intro-
duce systematic biases and a larger fraction of outliers, whereas coarser bins lead to broader, less precise posteriors.
These adopted bin sizes thus reflect the practical resolution limit achievable for one-monthTESS-like data within
the framework of our current model. Additionally, to verify that ∆νis optimally trained within the multi-output
configuration and that its optimal bin size is unaffected by the inclusion ofν max, we trained control models where ∆ν
is the only output parameter and thus received 100% weightage in training. As illustrated by the dashed brown curve
in Figure 12, theMdnSEfor the ∆ν-onlyTESS Modelis nearly identical to that of the multi-output model across
all evaluated bin sizes. This confirms that the training of ∆νis fully optimized within the multi-output framework.
Furthermore, the optimal bin size derived from this single-output model matches exactly with the multi-output model,
demonstrating that the determination of the ∆νbin size is not compromised by the presence ofν max as an additional
training target.

C.LARGE-SPACING INFERENCES FROM TESS MODEL
Our training sample for theTESS Modelis based on ∆νandν max measurements of red giants observed by Kepler
and K2, as detailed in section 2.3.1. Hence, this training set codifies the ∆ν−ν max relation for solar-like oscillators
(Stello et al. 2009) (∆ν∝ν 0.77
max) enabling determination of ∆νto 10-15% accuracy givenν max. The machine-learning
model could potentially leverage this and predict ∆νbased solely on this relation instead of identifying the pattern
associated with the large spacings. To verify this, we create three distinct sets of synthetics with 10, 20 and 30 percent
spread inν max on top of this relation, keeping other parameters the same as in table 1 (see appendix A for full details
on generating synthetics). Based on the ∆ν−ν max relation, 10, 20 and 30 percent spread inν max would correspond
to 7.7, 15.4 and 23.4 percent spread in ∆ν, respectively. Figure 14 shows the density plot for true vs predicted ∆νfor
reliable inferences on the synthetic set with 20 percent spread inν max and fig 15 shows the histogram of relative errors
for reliable predictions for all three synthetic sets (using the same quality thresholds as described in section 4.1). The
shaded regions highlight the ∆νspread expected from simple scaling using the ∆ν−ν max relation. Predictions based
solely on this scaling relation would result in ∆νvalues being uniformly distributed over the corresponding range.
However, the relative-error values in figure 15 indicate ∆νto be much more tightly constrained, peaking at zero, for
all three sets. Based on this, we conclude that theTESS Modelis not simply using the scaling relation to determine
∆ν- rather, it is predicting ∆νbased on the underlying frequency-separation pattern. However, we note that the
yield for reliable inferences drops with increasing spread in ∆ν−ν max values. This drop is likely due to increasing
differences in the of training sets (based on Kepler/K2 one-month segments, where most of the stars have under 10%
spread in ∆ν−ν max) and synthetic test sets (with 10, 20 and 30% spread). Nonetheless, the accuracy and precision
for the reliable inferences does not changing appreciably for synthetic sets with higher spreads (fig 15).
Furthermore, to explicitly verify that the model can learn and predict ∆νwithoutν max in the inputs, we trained
a model with a single output, ∆ν. Training data for this model were the same as those used for theTESS Model
and described in section 2.3.1. Figure 16 shows a comparison of predictions from this single output model and those
obtained using theTESS Model(which also hasν max as an output parameter) for the reliable set of 67,424 stars in
Kepler/K2-as-TESS test set (see section 4.1.1 for details). Nearly all inferences lie along the 1:1 line, which shows that
theTESS Modelcan identify the large-spacings pattern and predicts ∆νindependently ofν max.

E.EFFECTIVENESS OF FIRST-ORDER ROTATION FORMALISM
To verify the effectiveness of our currently implemented first-order rotation formalism (see appendix A), we created a
synthetic dataset with core and envelope rotation rates set to zero, while keeping all other parameters ranges the same

offset by the steep decline in accuracy.

synthetic dataset with no rotational splittings and observations. Thus, the implementation of a first-order description
