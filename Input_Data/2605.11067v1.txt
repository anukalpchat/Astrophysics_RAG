1. INTRODUCTION
Stars emit electromagnetic radiation at all wavelengths. Despite advances in technologies and the-
oretical knowledge, there is no telescope or detector to be able to collect all the photons from
a star. Instead, collecting photons from a limited wavelength range, such as the visual band, is
only for comparing the brightness of stars. Existence of a different band besides the visual, such
as the photographic or the blue magnitudes, had been noticed soon after usages of photographic
plates in astronomical photometry (Eker & Bakış 2025), but bolometric magnitudes represent-
ing stellar brightness at all wavelengths first introduced by Eddington (1926) in order to be used
when expressing his famous mass−luminosity relation, which he was inspired it from empirically
discovered mass−absolute visual magnitude relation discovered just few years ago independently
by Hertzsprung (1923) and Russell et al. (1923). Long after twelve years, Kuiper (1938) gave the
definition of bolometric correction for a star as the difference between its bolometric and visual
magnitudes as
BC=M Bol −M V =m Bol −V,(1)

2.1. Spectroscopic Data
Despite their low resolution (∆λ= 20Å),GaiaDR3 XP spectra have a great advantage that they
are free of atmospheric telluric lines (Griffin & Griffin 1973; Catanzaro 1997; Griffin 2000) because
Gaiasatellite operated outside the Earth’s atmosphere at the L2 point, which is 1.5 million km
away from the Earth directly opposite side of the Sun; another advantage of them for this study
is that their spectral range includes all the optical regions of stellar spectrum (3360 – 10200 Å)
covering almost three of theGaiabandpasses.
The small missing parts of the observed spectra at the shortest and longest wavelengths have been
noticed. The missing parts have been artificially completed by adding values one to the normalized
spectra to extend them to fit the wavelength limits of theGaiafilters. The artificially extended
parts of the normalized spectra and convoluted profiles are shown by dotted extensions in Figure 2.
This was done to minimize the negative effect of the missing part of the spectral coverage. The
cases with and without artificial extensions are checked, and it is seen that the differences in the
luminosity ratios are at the fourth digit after the decimal point, which are indeed negligible.
Unfortunately,GaiaXP spectra also suffered systemic errors that depend, mainly, on the color
of the object, especially below 4000 Å (Montegriffo et al. 2023). To overcome this problem, the
correction proposed in Huang et al. (2024) is applied. In this study, 88 of 128 stars were used from
the list of Y26, where only the stars had effective temperatures, radii, and spectra with S/N > 100,
resolution: R > 20000, and the wavelength range that included the visual band. The absence of some
bright stars (G≤6mag) inGaiaDR3 is a known selection effect caused by detector saturation
and processing limitations (Gaia Collaboration et al. 2023; Lindegren et al. 2021). Searching for
new stars with effective temperatures and radii was not intended, which would make us lose the
advantage of using a list of stars with internally consistent parameters established by the SED
models in Y26.

4. DISCUSSIONS
4.1. Why SpectroscopicBCsare more accurate
The about three times larger standard deviations of the data on the classicalBC−Teff functions
with respect to the standard deviations on the spectroscopicBC−Teff functions are given in Tables 8
and 9, respectively. Why areBC−Teff functions determined from the observed spectra much more
accurate than classical functions?
Based on a single observed entity (spectrum), it is natural for a spectroscopicBCvalue to be
more accurate than the classically computedBCvalue, which requires at least four observational
quantities, effective temperature and radius for calculating the absolute bolometric magnitude,
apparent brightness, and trigonometric parallaxes for calculating the absolute filtered magnitude,
which may also require another parameter, interstellar extinction, if the star is not sufficiently
nearby. Eker et al. (2021b) have already discussed the great advantage of using spectroscopicBC
values when computing standard stellar luminosities.
Especially after Y26, which explained how to obtain a spectroscopicBCfrom an observed spec-
trum, and this study, which used the same method to obtainGaiaSpectroscopicBCs, we can say
that today’s technology has reached a level of measuring effective temperature and a bolometric
correction of a star for a photometric band if its observed spectrum is wide enough to cover the
wavelength range of the filter and has sufficient resolution to distinguish certain spectral lines useful
for determining its effective temperature, and sufficiently high signal to noise ratio for minimizing
errors in determining spectral continuum and normalization. If the star has an accurate trigono-
metricGaiaparallax, one may even obtain the standard luminosity of the star from its apparent
