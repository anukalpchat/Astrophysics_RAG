ABSTRACT
We present measurements of the mass–metallicity relation (MZR) and fundamental metallicity re-
lation (FMR) at 1.4< z <7.0 using stacked JWST/NIRSpec spectra of 601 star-forming galaxies
from the JWST Advanced Deep Extragalactic Survey (JADES). Using the most up-to-date strong-line
metallicity calibrations based on high-redshift galaxies, we derive gas-phase metallicities from com-
posite spectra binned by stellar mass, redshift, and star-forming main sequence (SFMS) offset. We
find that the MZR slope evolves weakly fromz∼0 out toz∼5, withγ∼0.21±0.03, while the
normalization decreases smoothly with redshift at a rate ofdlog(O/H)/dz∼ −0.1 out toz∼4.
Beyondz≳5, the low-mass end continues to decline in metallicity while the high-mass end remains
broadly consistent with lower-redshift relations, producing a steeper overall MZR. We additionally find
evidence for a shallow anti-correlation between deviations from the MZR and SFMS at fixed stellar
mass atz∼1.4−5. This anti-correlation, albeit with weaker SFR coupling than observed locally,
suggests that an FMR is already beginning to emerge byz∼5. Comparisons with recent observa-
tions and cosmological simulations show broad agreement, though no single simulation simultaneously
reproduces the observed slopes and normalizations across all redshifts. Our results support a picture
in which bursty star formation and strong stellar feedback increasingly shape the regulation of galaxy
growth at high redshift, while also highlighting the need for substantially larger spectroscopic samples
to robustly constrain the evolution of galaxy scaling relations at high-redshift.

1.INTRODUCTION
A description of the chemical evolution of galaxies
across cosmic time is crucial to our understanding of
galaxy formation and growth. The gas-phase oxygen
abundance of a galaxy is sensitive to several key pro-
cesses such as nucleosynthesis via stars, the release of
metals into the interstellar medium (ISM) in supernovae
and stellar winds, the outflow of metals via galactic su-
perwinds, and accretion of both pristine and enriched

material from the intergalactic and circumgalactic me-
dia into the ISM (e.g., Dav´ e et al. 2017a; Tumlinson
et al. 2017).
A powerful tracer of galaxy chemical evolution is the
mass-metallicity relation (MZR), relating the gas-phase
oxygen abundance of a galaxy to its stellar mass. It
is well-established that these two galaxy properties are
tightly correlated in the local universe, highlighting the
role of galaxy halo mass in regulating the retention or es-
cape of metals from the ISM (e.g., Lequeux et al. 1979;
Tremonti et al. 2004). At high redshifts, and at stel-
lar masses below∼10 10.5 M⊙, above which thez∼0
MZR asymptotes, the MZR is characterized by a power

law. The normalization of the MZR in this lower-mass
regime has been observed to decrease significantly be-
tweenz∼0 andz∼3 (e.g., Sanders et al. 2021; Li
et al. 2023; He et al. 2024), and this decreasing evolu-
tion appears to slow down beyondz≳3 (e.g., Naka-
jima et al. 2023; Curti et al. 2024). On the other hand,
the slope of the MZR stays relatively constant out to
z∼3 (e.g., Sanders et al. 2021). In other regimes,
however, there is significant ambiguity on the behav-
ior of the MZR. In some studies, the slope is seen to
flatten out at even lower stellar masses (e.g., Li et al.
2023; He et al. 2024) and at higher redshifts (e.g., Curti
et al. 2024; Kotiwale et al. 2026), while others find a
steepening slope (e.g., Chemerynska et al. 2024). Even
among cosmological simulations, the predicted MZRs
show widely different slopes, normalizations, and red-
shift evolutions (e.g., Marszewski et al. 2024; Pallottini
et al. 2025; McClymont et al. 2026; Garcia et al. 2024,
2025). The diversity of observed MZRs and lack of con-
sensus among theoretical models point toward the need
for a larger, more representative sample across a wider
range of stellar masses and redshifts to accurately map
out the trend for the full galaxy population and track
its evolution at higher temporal resolution.
Locally, an even tighter correlation is observed when
incorporating galaxy star-formation rates (SFRs) into
the MZR, yielding what is often referred to as the fun-
damental metallicity relation (FMR) (e.g., Mannucci
et al. 2010). The FMR is thought to represent the
quasi-equilibrium state that star-forming galaxies tend
toward, as traced by the gas-phase metallicity, stellar
mass, and SFR (e.g., Dav´ e et al. 2012; Peng & Maiolino
2014). A major open question is whether this relation-
ship holds at earlier cosmic times and, if so, when this
regulatory framework becomes established in the time-
line of galaxy evolution. Early studies found that galax-
ies remained broadly consistent with the local FMR out
to cosmic noon (Sanders et al. 2021), supporting the
idea of a redshift-invariant relation. However, more re-
cent JWST-era surveys have begun to reveal systematic
offsets toward lower metallicities at fixed stellar mass
and SFR, beginning aroundz≳3 (e.g., Stanton et al.
2026) or evenz≳6 (e.g., Nakajima et al. 2023; Curti
et al. 2024). The exact nature of the relationship be-
tween stellar mass, metallicity, and SFR is not yet well
constrained at these early cosmic times.
A major limitation in studies of the MZR and FMR
at early cosmic times is the absence of high-resolution
spectroscopic samples that are as extensive and repre-
sentative as those available in the local universe out
to cosmic noon (e.g., Mannucci et al. 2010; Andrews
& Martini 2013; Sanders et al. 2021; Korhonen Cues-
tas et al. 2025). Recent JWST surveys have dramat-
ically expanded the accessible parameter space, prob-
ing galaxies across a wide range of stellar masses (down
to∼10 6 M⊙ in lensed fields; e.g., Chemerynska et al.
2024; Hsiao et al. 2026), environments (e.g., Li et al.
2025), and redshifts extending toz∼10 (e.g., Nishigaki
et al. 2025; Pollock et al. 2026). However, obtaining the
signal-to-noise necessary to measure faint emission lines
and derive quantities such as metallicity and SFR often
biases high-redshift samples toward the brightest, most
actively star-forming galaxies at fixed stellar mass. Al-
though some surveys explicitly aim to characterize and
mitigate these selection effects (e.g., Lewis et al. 2025),
current samples remain relatively limited in size com-
pared to lower-redshift surveys. The relatively small
sample size makes it difficult to robustly map out the
evolution of galaxy scaling relations across the full high-
redshift galaxy population at fine enough stellar mass or
temporal resolutions to adequately constrain models of
galaxy formation and growth.
As mentioned above, the gas-phase metallicity of a
galaxy is a crucial physical quantity used to study the
MZR and FMR. A robust approach to measuring metal-
licity is to determine the emissivities of the emission
lines arising from each ion species of a given chemical
element as well as the Balmer lines, enabling the con-
version from emission-line flux to abundance relative to
hydrogen (e.g., Peimbert 1967; Izotov et al. 2006). De-
termining these emissivities requires knowledge of the
Hiiregion electron temperature (T e) and density, the
former of which depends on the ability to robustly mea-
sure faint auroral emission lines such as [Oiii]λ4364 or
[Oii]λλ7322,7332 (e.g., Izotov et al. 2006; Luridiana
et al. 2015; P´ erez-Montero 2017). This method, often
referred to as the “direct” or “Te” method, is considered
the “gold standard” in studies of high-redshift galaxies.
However, these auroral emission lines are typically a
hundred times fainter than their brighter nebular coun-
terparts (e.g., [Oiii]λλ5008,4960, [Oii]λλ3727,3730),
rendering theT e method an impractical approach for
measuring metallicities in large, representative galaxy
samples at high redshift due to the very long integration
times that are required. As such, the ratios of strong,
rest-optical emission lines are often used to determine
metallicity in the absence of aT e measurement, where
these ratios are calibrated to samples of galaxies withTe-
based metallicity measurements (e.g., Pettini & Pagel
2004; Curti et al. 2017; Bian et al. 2018; Curti et al.
2020; Nakajima et al. 2022) or photoionization models
(e.g., Kewley & Dopita 2002; Dopita et al. 2016; Kewley
et al. 2019).

Prior to JWST, most common strong-line metallicity
indicators were calibrated using samples of local galax-
ies, in which the ISM conditions at fixed nebular metal-
licity do not resemble those found inz≳1 galaxies
(e.g., Steidel et al. 2014a; Shapley et al. 2015; Strom
et al. 2017; Topping et al. 2020a,b; Sanders et al. 2020;
Runco et al. 2021a; Clarke et al. 2026). A recent work by
Sanders et al. (2025) has compiled observations of galax-
ies at 1.3< z <10.6 from the AURORA survey as well
as several works in the literature to develop strong-line
metallicity relations calibrated to high-redshift galaxies,
enabling the robust determination of gas-phase oxygen
abundances atz≳1.
In this work, we use a sample of 601 star-forming
galaxies from the JWST Advanced Deep Extragalac-
tic Survey (JADES) (Eisenstein et al. 2023a; D’Eugenio
et al. 2025) to investigate the MZR and the FMR at
high redshift. This sample represents the largest spec-
troscopic study of main-sequence star-forming galax-
ies spanning 1.4< z <7. Leveraging the statistical
power of this dataset and employing updated metallicity
calibrations designed for high-redshift ISM conditions,
we construct high signal-to-noise composite spectra in
finely spaced stellar mass bins and narrower redshift in-
tervals than previous studies. This methodology enables
us to trace the evolution of the MZR at higher temporal
resolution across cosmic time and to test for the presence
of an FMR for the first time atz≳3.
The structure of the paper is as follows: in Section 2,
we detail the observations, data reduction, and spectral
fitting procedures to the JADES data, the derivation of
physical quantities, and the stacking methodology used
to generate composite spectra. In Section 3, we present
our analysis of the MZR and FMR. In Section 4, we com-
pare our results with previous studies and cosmological
simulations, and discuss the implications of our observed
trends on the timeline of galaxy assembly and evolution.
Section 5 summarizes our conclusions. Throughout this
paper, we assumeH 0 = 70 km s −1 Mpc−1, Ω m = 0.3,
ΩΛ = 0.7, and a Chabrier (2003) initial-mass function.
We adopt the solar abundances from Asplund et al.
(2021) in which 12+log(O/H) ⊙ = 8.69 and Z⊙ = 0.014.
2.OBSERVATIONS AND ANALYSIS
The data set and measurements in this work are based
on DR3 of JADES (Eisenstein et al. 2023a; D’Eugenio
et al. 2025) in the GOODS-N and GOODS-S fields. The
reduction and analysis of the JADES data used in this
work are presented in Clarke et al. (2025) (C25 here-
after), and we provide an overview in the following sub-
sections.
2.1.NIRCam and NIRSpec Observations
The JADES NIRCam observations used in this anal-
ysis were obtained from MAST 7. Specifically, we uti-
lized the photometric catalog, the contents of which
are described in Rieke et al. (2023b), Eisenstein et al.
(2023a,b), and Robertson et al. (2024). The JADES
catalog contains observations in several filters includ-
ing JWST/NIRCam F070W, F090W, F115W, F150W,
F200W, F277W, F335M, F356W, F410M, and F444W.
Additional observations were taken in the F182M,
F210M, and F444W filters from the FRESCO pro-
gram (Oesch et al. 2023), and F182M, F210M, F430M,
F460M, and F480M filters from the JEMS program
(Williams et al. 2023b). The photometric catalog also
utilizes HST imaging mosaics from Illingworth et al.
(2013); Whitaker et al. (2019), adding photometry in
the HST/ACS F435W, F606W, F775W, F814W, and
F850LP filters as well as HST/WFC3 F105W, F125W,
F140W, and F160W. From the photometric catalog, we
used theKRON CONVcatalog FITS extension, which con-
sists of photometry measured from F444W point-spread
function- (PSF) matched images using Kron apertures.
The description of the NIRSpec data and observ-
ing strategy can be found in Bunker et al. (2024) and
D’Eugenio et al. (2025). Observations were taken in the
R∼100 prism mode, theR∼1000 G140M/F070LP,
G235M/F170LP, and G395M/F290LP gratings, and the
R∼2700 G395H/F290LP grating. In this analysis,
we utilized measurements only from theR∼100 and
R∼1000 dispersers. For the prism and the G235M and
G395M grating observations, the data were downloaded
from the DAWN JWST Archive (DJA) 8. The data re-
duction procedure for the spectra in these dispersers is
described in de Graaff et al. (2025) and Heintz et al.
(2025). As described in C25, the G140M observations
were downloaded from the data release 3 sub-section on
the JADES website 9. To avoid self-subtraction of ex-
tended objects during the dithered-frame background
subtraction step, we performed a custom reduction of
115 extended objects, described in C25. The prism spec-
tra were flux calibrated by scaling to match the NIRCam
and HST photometry, and the emission lines were fit us-
ing the procedure in C25.

2.2.1.Stellar Population Parameters
Stellar population parameters were determined
through SED fitting of the photometry and the flux-
calibrated prism spectra with theprospectorcode
(Johnson et al. 2021). The SEDs were fit with both
stellar as well as nebular emission components from
Cloudy(Ferland et al. 2013; Byler et al. 2017). FSPS
was used as the stellar population synthesis model (Con-
roy et al. 2009; Conroy & Gunn 2010), adopting MIST
isochrones (Choi et al. 2016) and the MILES stellar li-
brary (S´ anchez-Bl´ azquez et al. 2006). The stellar metal-
licity and dust attenuation law were selected between
0.28 Z ⊙ + SMC (Gordon et al. 2003) or 1.4 Z ⊙ +
Calzetti et al. (2000) depending on which model yielded
the lowerχ 2 value in the SED fitting, as described in
C25 and Topping et al. (2025). The SEDs were fit using
a non-parametric star-formation history (SFH) with a
continuity prior (Leja et al. 2019). SFHs were divided
into eight time bins, with the most recent two bins span-
ning 0-3 Myr and 3-10 Myr in lookback time, and the
remaining six logarithmically spaced back toz= 20.
2.2.2.Emission Line Fitting, Reddening Corrections, and
SFR Measurements
Emission lines were fit simultaneously in both prism
and grating observations to account for overlapping
spectra and higher-order contamination in the medium-
resolution data. The flux-calibrated prism spectra con-
strained the absolute line fluxes, while the higher-
resolution grating spectra provided leverage on blended
features (e.g., Hαand [Nii]) by enforcing consistent line
ratios across both datasets. Redshifts were initially es-
timated from the brightest lines and then fixed for the
full fitting procedure. Emission lines were modeled as
Gaussian profiles superimposed on a continuum derived
from best-fit spectral energy distribution models, with
the continuum smoothed to match the prism resolution.
Line widths incorporated both instrumental resolution
and intrinsic velocity dispersion.
The dust reddeningE(B−V) was derived from the
decrements of at least two detected hydrogen recom-
bination lines, namely Paα, Paβ, Paγ, Hα, Hβ, and
Hγ. The intrinsic ratios were determined usingPyNeb
(Luridiana et al. 2015) under the assumption of Case B
recombination, electron temperatureT e = 15000 K, and
electron densityn e = 100 cm−3 (Osterbrock & Ferland
2006). With respect to Hβ, the intrinsic ratios of Paα,
Paβ, Paγ, Hα, and Hγare 0.30, 0.15, 0.09, 2.79, and
0.47, respectively. We also adopted a Milky Way dust
attenuation curve (Cardelli et al. 1989) to translate the
observed ratios toE(B−V). Unphysical or negative
E(B−V) values were truncated to zero.

2.3.Composite Spectra
2.3.1.Samples
We draw our JADES parent sample from D’Eugenio
et al. (2025), consisting of 4,086 total objects and 1,245
galaxies at 1.4≤z <7.0. In addition to the raw-data-
level cuts listed in C25, we first ensured that all systems
had specific SFRs (sSFRs) greater than 10 −11 yr−1 to
exclude quiescent galaxies. We then removed 210 galax-
ies without robust (>3σ) detections of Hα. In addition,
we removed 41 objects identified as AGN based on broad
Balmer components or [Nii]λ6585/Hα >0.5. An addi-
tional 10 objects were removed due to spurious features
near [Oii]λλ3727,3730 fitted as>3σdetections, leaving
a sample of 984 galaxies.
We further isolated a subsample of 601 star-forming
galaxies to create composite grating spectra for a stack-
ing analysis by requiring wavelength coverage of the
emission lines used for metallicity estimates, specifi-
cally [Oii]λλ3727,3730, [Neiii]λ3870, Hβ, [Oiii]λ5008,
[Nii]λ6585, and Hα. This requirement ensures that the
emission line fluxes measured from composite spectra
are representative of the galaxies contributing to each
stack. We name this full sample the “MZR sample”.
Following the star-forming main sequence analysis of
C25, the galaxies were then divided into four redshift
bins, namely 1.4≤z <2.7, 2.7≤z <4.0, 4.0≤z <5.0,
and 5.0≤z <7.0. The two lower-redshift bins were
defined to match those of analyses using ground-based
surveys such as MOSDEF (e.g., Sanders et al. 2021),
and the highest redshift bin 5.0≤z <7.0 represents
the merger of the 5< z <6 and 6< z <7 subsamples
from C25. As discussed in C25, the full sample appears

2.3.2.Stacking Procedure
Our binning schemes and stacking procedure largely
follow those of Sanders et al. (2018, 2021), and are also
described in Karthikeyan et al. (2026). At each redshift,
galaxies withM ∗ <10 8.5 M⊙ were grouped into a single
stellar-mass bin, while galaxies above this representa-
tive mass limit were divided into equal-number bins by
stellar mass. We refer to these composite spectra as the
M∗-stacks.
We first processed the grating spectrum for each
galaxy by scaling the G140M, G235M, and G395M grat-
ing spectra to the flux calibrated prism spectrum. We
calculated scaling factors between the line fluxes mea-
sured in the prism and grating spectra for the strong
emission lines [Oii]λλ3727,3730, Hγ, Hβ, [Oiii]λ5008,
Hα, [Nii]λ6585, Paγ, Paβ, and Paαrequiring that each
is detected at S/N≥3. The grating-wide scaling factor
was determined by the inverse-variance-weighted aver-
age of the emission lines that fall in the wavelength range
of the grating. The three grating spectra were then
stitched together, shifted into the rest frame, converted
from flux density to luminosity density, and normalized
to the non-dust-corrected Hαluminosity measured from
the prism spectrum.
With the processed grating spectra, we then generated
the composite spectra by re-sampling each spectrum
onto a common wavelength grid using theSpectRes
package (Carnall 2017) before calculating the median
flux density at each wavelength. The above stacking
process was repeated 100 times for each composite bin
(in, e.g., stellar mass) via Monte Carlo simulations to de-
termine the uncertainties of the bin edges and median
M∗, as well as to generate the associated error spectrum.
The 100 realizations of the composite spectrum for each
bin were created by allowing galaxies to shift between
mass bins according to the uncertainties on their M ∗,
bootstrap resampling within the bin to account for sam-
ple variance, and perturbing individual galaxy grating
spectra by their error spectra prior to median-stacking.
The resultant composite spectrum and error spectrum
represent the median flux and 1-σstandard deviation
at each wavelength. This stacking process was also ap-
plied to the SED models associated with each galaxy to
obtain a composite SED model spectrum for each bin.
For the FMR subsample, we repeated the stacking
procedure above to generate M∗-stacks, additionally cal-
culating the median SFR of each stack during the Monte
Carlo simulation stage. Furthermore, we created bins in
both stellar mass and offset from the SFR-M ∗relation
(i.e., Star Forming Main-Sequence, SFMS) at each red-
shift range relative to the trend reported in C25. We
determined the SFMS for the 5.0≤z <7.0 range
by applying the same methodology detailed in C25 for
the combined set of galaxies in the 5.0< z <6.0 and
6.0< z <7.0 samples reported in that paper. The best-
fit parameters for slope, normalization, and intrinsic
scatter, respectively, areα= 0.91 +0.16
−0.16,β N = 0.95+0.05

2.3.3.Emission Line Fitting
Emission-line fluxes were measured from single-
Gaussian fits to the stacked spectra. Closely spaced
emission lines (within 30 ˚A) and doublets with known
ratios were fit simultaneously, where their centroids and
width were kinematically tied. We also fit [Neiii]λ3870
and the blended H I (H8) and He I feature at 3890 ˚A
simultaneously.
In general, we fit linear models to the underlying con-
tinua in windows of roughly 20 ˚A to 100 ˚A blue- and
redwards of the emission lines. For Hydrogen recom-
bination lines and nearby emission lines, we accounted
for stellar absorption using the composite SED model
spectra associated with each stack. We scaled the SED
model to the composite spectrum by determining a lin-
ear scaling function in continuum windows around the
emission lines. The uncertainties on line fluxes were de-
termined through Monte Carlo simulations where the
composite spectra were perturbed by the error spectra
500 times.

Following S21, we determined gas-phase metallicities
by considering a combination of the strong emission line
ratios O3, O32, and Ne3O2 that are detected in the
spectra of individual galaxies and in the stacked spec-
tra. These three line ratios contain emission lines of
only hydrogen andα-elements, the latter of which are
produced by core-collapse supernovae and thus trace the
gas-phase oxygen abundance more directly. These line
ratios are therefore not susceptible to evolving relations
such as the nitrogen-to-oxygen ratio (N/O) if N2 were
used. Due to the degenerate nature of O3 in the typical
ranges of metallicities found in our sample, we required
the detection of at least one additional line ratio (O32 or
Ne3O2) in order to break the degeneracy in the spectra
of individual galaxies. In such spectra, a sole detection
of O32 or Ne3O2 was also allowed for metallicity deter-
mination. In our stacked spectra, all three line ratios O3,
O32, and Ne3O2 are available in the mass-representative
range, enabling robust measurements using this full set
of strong-line calibrations.

The scatter of O3 ratios exhibits a decreasing trend
with increasing mass in the lowest redshift bin, with the
trend becoming increasingly flat with M ∗ as redshift in-
creases. We find a decreasing trend in O32 and Ne3O2
with increasing M ∗ across all redshift ranges, whereas
N2 increases with increasing M ∗. R23, on the other
hand, does not show strong variations with respect to
M∗ across our redshift ranges. In general, we recover
the commonly observed trends of metallicity-sensitive
strong-line ratios with mass, finding that most ratios,
with the exception of O3 and R23, show strong correla-
tions with mass.
The mild trends in the O3 and R23 ratios with stel-
lar mass can be explained by the double-valued na-
ture of these calibrations, in which the line ratios are
anti-correlated with metallicity above a characteristic
“turnover metallicity,” below which the ratios are posi-
tively correlated. For the O3 and R23 calibrations, the
majority of our sample lies in this range of “turnover”
metallicities, resulting in the observed flat trend of these
ratios with stellar mass (e.g., Steidel et al. 2014b). The
turnovers in the O32 and Ne3O2 calibrations, however,
occur at very low metallicities (12 + log(O/H)≲7.4;
e.g., Nakajima et al. 2023; Sanders et al. 2025), es-
sentially behaving as monotonic relations in the metal-
licity range spanned by our sample. For example, some
of the highest O32 and Ne3O2 ratios in the MZR sam-
ple of log(O32) = 0.7 and log(Ne3O2) =−0.3 corre-
spond to a metallicity value of 12 + log(O/H)∼8.0,
representing some of the lowest metallicities in our sam-
ple. Coincidentally, the O3 and R23 ratios peak at
12 + log(O/H)∼8.0. Thus, most of our sample lies in
the flat part of the O3 and R23 calibrations, with a
small fraction of objects populating the high-metallicity
branch where smaller ratios correspond to higher metal-
licities. This effect is especially evident in the R23
calibration from S25, which has a wider and flatter
peak. For a typical uncertainty ofδlog(R23)∼0.05,
the metallicity inferred from the R23 calibration spans
7.5≲12 + log(O/H)≲8.4, leading to great degeneracy.

3.2.Mass-Metallicity Relation
In Figure 4, we present the best-fit metallicities vs.
M∗ at each redshift range for the MZR sample, where
we recover a positive trend in all panels and a decreasing
normalization in metallicity towards higher redshift. We
applied the same procedure described in Section 2.4 to
the S21 MOSDEF points shown by star markers. As the
median redshift of the S21 MOSDEFz∼2.3 sample
is roughly 0.2 higher than our 1.4≤z <2.7 sample,
applyingdlog(O/H)/dz=−0.11 from S21 shifts the
metallicity of the S21 MOSDEF stacked points in this
redshift range up by∼0.02 dex. After this correction,
the metallicity measurements of our 1.4≤z <2.7 and
2.7≤z <4.0 stacks are in good agreement with those
of thez∼2.3 andz∼3.3 MOSDEF stacks from S21.
We note that the distribution of galaxies in the high-
mass end of the 1.4≤z <2.7 sample clips at 12 +
log(O/H)= 8.6, the upper bound of the range of metal-
licities over which the calibrations of S25 are valid. This
effect is driven by the low O3, O32, and Ne3O2 ratios
that extend below the calibration limits. The opposite
is true for the low-mass end of the 5.0≤z <7.0 sample
where data points populate very low metallicities. These
galaxies exhibit high O32 and Ne3O2 ratios that lie be-
yond the region covered by the quadratic parametriza-
tion of the calibrations. As a result, the minimum metal-
licity of the calibration range is assigned to these galax-
ies for O32 and Ne3O2, thus bringing down the overall
best-fit metallicity values during theχ 2 minimization
procedure. Although the linear parameterizations of
O32 and Ne3O2 extend into the regime of these high line
ratios and would therefore alleviate this issue, we still

3.3.2.Anti-correlation between SFR and metallicity offsets
It is clear that the MZR and SFMS evolve with red-
shift (see Section 3.2 and e.g., Speagle et al. 2014; C25).
An important question, therefore, is whether the under-
lying physical connection between star formation and
chemical enrichment is already in place at early cosmic
times. Rather than focusing solely on the global evo-
lution of these scaling relations, investigating second-
order trends provides insights into the physical pro-
cesses that regulate fluctuations about them (e.g., Dav´ e
et al. 2017b; Torrey et al. 2018). In particular, we test
whether galaxies offset above the SFMS tend to exhibit
systematically lower metallicities at fixed stellar mass,
as expected from the local FMR.
With the suggestion of a potentially different FMR
at high redshift based on the results in Figure 6
and Section 3.3.1, we investigate this relationship us-
ing the offsets in the SFMS and MZR of the FMR
M∗-∆SFR stacks. Figure 7 presents the SFR devi-
ations from the SFMS from C25 and Section 2.3.4,
∆ log(SFR/M⊙ yr−1), and metallicity deviations from
the fitted MZR from Section 3.2, ∆ log(O/H), in the
corresponding redshift ranges. Uncertainties on these
offsets were estimated via Monte Carlo resampling, in
which SFRs, metallicities, and the parameters of the
SFMS and MZR were perturbed within their uncertain-
ties over 1000 realizations.
We fit a linear relation to these offsets within each
redshift bin, with the form
∆ log(O/H) =ν×∆ log(SFR/M ⊙ yr−1) +δ O/H,0 (5)
whereνis the slope andδ O/H,0 is the MZR offset at
∆ log(SFR/M⊙ yr−1) = 0. We allowed for a non-zero
intercept because the galaxies in the FMR subsample,
which have individually measured dust-corrected SFRs,
are not guaranteed to span the same parameter space
as the galaxies used to define our fiducial MZR, and
therefore may not symmetrically scatter around it. Bar-
ring slight offsets inδ O/H,0, this parameterization can be
rewritten as ∆ log(O/H)∝∆ log(SFR) ν.
Atz≲5, we find a shallow but significant anti-
correlation between metallicity and SFR, with slopes of
ν=−0.13±0.05,ν=−0.08±0.04, andν=−0.09±0.06
in the 1.4≤z <2.7, 2.7≤z <4.0, and 4.0≤z <5.0
bins, respectively. This anti-correlation is seen in both
the low and high mass stacks, denoted by the smaller
and larger marker sizes in Figure 7. We additionally
report a fit excluding the high-mass, high-SFR stack in
the 4.0≤z <5.0 bin, motivated by the lack of UV-
bright objects in the high-mass end of this sample within
this redshift range. Indeed, this high-mass, high-SFR
point appears to be biased high in metallicity at fixed
∆ log(SFR/M⊙ yr−1), which is consistent with a picture
where galaxies with lower SFRs display higher metallic-
ities at fixed mass. Removal of this point steepens the

14
slope toν=−0.12±0.08, but the trend still remains
shallower than that observed atz∼0 ofν=−0.27
(S21), as is the case with the slopes of the other two red-
shift bins. We also indicate the best-fit anti-correlation
of the MOSDEFz∼2.3 stacks from S21 for reference,
whereν=−0.19. We note, however, that this rela-
tion was adopted directly from that work, which em-
ployed different methodologies, metallicity calibrations,
and SFR conversions than those used in our analysis;
therefore, direct comparisons should be made with cau-
tion. Using the updated metallicity measurements and
MZR fits to the S21 MOSDEF data, and under the
assumption that the SFMS offsets are not affected by
the constant scalar adjustment of−0.32 dex (see Sec-
tion 2.3.4), we obtainν∼0.15 for thez∼2.3 stack,
which is in closer alignment to that of our 1.4≤z <2.7
bin.
In the highest redshift bin 5.0≤z <7.0, we do not
find clear evidence for an anti-correlation when consid-
ering all stacked points since all four points are consis-
tent with one another in ∆ log(O/H) within their errors.
Isolating the high-mass points recovers a tentative anti-
correlation withν=−0.10±0.14, though not statisti-
cally significant given the large uncertainties and limited
data points. Notably, the low-mass, low-SFR stack ex-
hibits significantly lower metallicity than all other stacks
in this redshift bin, and thus drives the flattening of the
best-fit line. Qualitatively, this behavior is consistent
with the trend seen in the MZR in Section 3.2, where
the steepening of the MZR slope in the 5.0≤z <7.0 bin
is primarily driven by the low-mass end, while the high-
mass end is seen to broadly fall in the regime spanned
by thez∼3.3 andz∼4.5 MZR fits. However, given
that each stack is composed of only∼13 galaxies on
average, we do not possess the numbers to robustly test
this hypothesis.
Overall, we find anti-correlations between deviations
from the SFMS and MZR in our three lowest-redshift
bins, though the trends are weaker than those observed
in the local Universe. The presence of this relation sug-
gests that the physical processes regulating star forma-
tion, gas flows, and chemical enrichment were already
becoming established byz∼5.
3.3.3.Constructing an FMR based on high-zgalaxies
Motivated by the presence of an anti-correlation be-
tween SFR and metallicity residuals atz≲5, and by
the systematic offset of our high-redshift sample relative
to the local FMR, we applied the standard approach of
optimizing the projection parameterαto minimize the
scatter in the M ∗Z–SFR relation (e.g., Mannucci et al.
2010; Andrews & Martini 2013; Stanton et al. 2026).
Because this analysis required both individual metal-
licity and individual SFR measurements, the resulting
subsample of the FMR galaxies consisted of 56, 76, and
50 galaxies in the 1.4≤z <2.7, 2.7≤z <4.0, and
4.0≤z <5.0 bins, respectively, for a total sample of
182 galaxies. To assess the representativeness of this
subsample to the full FMR sample, we compared their
SFR–M∗ distributions within each redshift bin. We find
that the rolling median SFRs of the subsamples are bi-
ased high by≲0.2 dex below∼10 9.5 M⊙ relative to the
full FMR sample, while the rolling median trends agree
well above 109.5 M⊙.
We constructed a grid ofαvalues from 0 to 0.6 in
steps of 0.01 and fit a linear relation usinglinmix(Kelly
2007) to determine the best-fit parameters and intrinsic
scatter (σint) for eachα. The best-fitαwas defined as
the value that minimized this scatter. Figure 8 shows
the resulting projections of the FMR for each redshift
bin, with insets displaying the intrinsic scatter as a func-
tion ofα. The best-fit values ofαare roughlyα∼0.26
for 1.4≤z <2.7,α∼0.38 for 2.7≤z <4.0, and
α∼0.28 for 4.0≤z <5.0. The typical uncertainty
onσ int is∼0.05, implying that although the intrinsic
scatters themselves are statistically significant, the vari-
ations of≲0.02 (seen in the inset plots) are not. Given
the small error bars on the individualµand metallic-
ity measurements, the large uncertainties inσ int likely
stem from the small sample sizes in each redshift bin.
Even when all three redshift bins are merged to form a
larger 1.4≤z <5.0 sample, the variations inσ int stay
at∼0.02, while the uncertainties marginally improve
to∼0.04. We therefore caution against drawing strong
conclusions from the exact value of the best-fitαor in-
ferring redshift evolution between redshift bins. That
being said, we note that theseαvalues are systemati-
cally smaller than those derived from stacked analyses
(α∼0.55–0.66; e.g., Curti et al. 2020; Sanders et al.
2021; Andrews & Martini 2013), but broadly consistent
with the lower values reported in studies based on in-
dividual galaxy measurements (α∼0.2–0.3; e.g., Man-
nucci et al. 2010 and subsequent work).
Although a non-zeroαreduces the scatter relative
to the MZR (orα=0), the improvement is modest, at
∼10% in the lowest two redshift bins and∼3% for
4.0≤z <5.0. The degree of this tightening is small
compared to the typical improvements of∼30−70%
in the local Universe (Mannucci et al. 2010; Andrews &
Martini 2013; Curti et al. 2020). In some cases, par-
ticularly for 1.4≤z <2.7 and 4.0≤z <5.0, larger
values ofα(e.g.,α= 0.60) increase the scatter, indi-
cating that a strong SFR dependence is not favored by
the data. Overall, the typical intrinsic scatters in our

4.DISCUSSION
4.1.Comparing our sample to past studies
In this work, we analyzed the evolution of the MZR
and FMR at high redshift, adding to the growing body
of studies enabled by JWST (e.g., Nakajima et al. 2023;
Curti et al. 2024; Chemerynska et al. 2024; Sarkar et al.
2025; Stanton et al. 2026). A key advantage of our anal-
ysis is the use of stacked spectra, which allows us to de-
rive metallicities without requiring significant detections
of fainter emission lines (e.g., Hβ, [Oii] and [Neiii])
to perform dust attenuation corrections and estimate
metallicities for individual galaxies. This methodology
removes a common source of selection bias toward galax-
ies with elevated SFRs relative to the SFMS and corre-
spondingly lower metallicities at fixed stellar mass. Our
sample is not only more representative of the underlying
star-forming galaxy population, as seen from the com-
parison to the SFMS in Figure 2, but also the largest
currently available for studies of the MZR and FMR at
z≳2.
The large sample size allows us to divide the data
into relatively fine redshift and stellar mass bins. Ad-
jacent bins are typically separated by∼0.5 dex in me-
dian stellar mass, enabling us to trace the evolution of
these relations with greater resolution than many pre-
vious studies. While some recent works probe broader
redshift and mass ranges, their smaller sample sizes of-

4.2.Comparing our MZR to past studies
We compare our MZR fits to past studies in Figure 9.
As discussed in Section 3.2, we measure an MZR slope
ofγ∼0.21±0.03, which holds across 1.4< z <5,
and which falls within the range of values reported in
literature. In particular, our results agree closely with
studies adopting strong-line metallicity calibrations sim-
ilar to ours, including S21 using the updated calibrations
from S25. Slightly steeper slopes have been reported by
Nakajima et al. (2023) (γ= 0.25±0.03 for 4< z <10),
He et al. (2024) (γ= 0.26±0.04 atz∼2.88), and Khos-
tovan et al. (2025) (γ= 0.27±0.04 atz∼2.28), though
all consistent with our results within 1.5σ. Other works
span a wider range, from the steeper relations of Stanton
et al. (2026) (γ= 0.29±0.01 atz∼3.2) to the flatter
slopes found by Curti et al. (2024) (γ= 0.18±0.03 for
3< z <6). Within a given study (e.g., Sanders et al.
2021; Li et al. 2023; Stanton et al. 2026), the slope of
the MZR appears to stay roughly the same across these
redshifts.
Atz≳5, however, our measured MZR steepens rela-
tive to the lower redshift bins toγ= 0.41, which is in
contrast to many studies that find either little evolution
in slope or even flattening toward high redshift (e.g.,
Nakajima et al. 2023; Curti et al. 2024). For instance,
Sarkar et al. (2025) reportγ∼0.28, 0.23, and 0.21 at
z∼5, 7, and 9, respectively. Kotiwale et al. (2026) also
find a flatγ= 0.12±0.08 for 5< z <7 based on direct-
Te measurements of metallicities. On the other hand,
Chemerynska et al. (2024) measure a comparably steep
slope ofγ= 0.39±0.02 at 6< z <8.
Similar to the slope of the MZR at lower redshifts, the
normalization of our relations in the lower 1.4< z <4.0
range are in broad agreement with those in literature,
particularly among works using line diagnostics dom-
inated by O3, O32, and Ne3O2. For instance, our
recalculated MZR of the MOSDEF stacks from S21,
as reported in Table 4, haveZ 9 = 8.26±0.02 and
Z9 = 8.18±0.04 atz∼2.08 andz∼3.24 after ac-
counting for redshift evolution. Stanton et al. (2026)
reportZ 9 = 8.14±0.01 atz∼3.2. These measure-
ments are consistent within 2σuncertainty of our re-

17
sults at 1.4< z <4.0. On the other hand, studies that
mainly employ the directT e-based method to measure
metallicities find lower normalizations at fixed M ∗ (e.g.
Z9 = 8.17±0.06 atz∼2.28 from Khostovan et al. 2025).
This discrepancy may arise because faint [Oiii] auro-
ral emission lines required forT e-based metallicities are
more readily detected in low-metallicity galaxies, where
higher electron temperatures enhance line emissivities.
In the 4.0< z <5.0 range, our MZR normalization
does not decrease much from the 2.7< z <4.0 rela-
tion. This normalization is over 0.2 dex higher com-
pared to thez= 4.76 relation of Curti et al. (2024)
withZ 9 = 7.93 (inferred from their reported values of
Z8 = 7.75 andγ= 0.18). These discrepancies may
arise from differences in sample selection. For example,
Curti et al. (2024) probe lower stellar masses (down to
∼10 7 M⊙) and target galaxies that lie systematically
above the SFMS at fixed mass by about 0.5 dex. If en-
hanced SFRs correspond to lower metallicities at fixed
mass, as we demonstrate in Section 3.3.2, such selec-
tion effects could contribute to lower observed metallic-
ity normalizations. However, even if we shift the Curti
et al. (2024) MZR upwards by 0.1 dex in metallicity to
account for this anti-correlation at 4< z <5, we cannot
fully erase this 0.2 dex discrepancy.
Atz≳5, many past studies appear to agree on
the metallicity normalizations, all converging at 12 +
log(O/H)∼7.8 at 10 8.5 M⊙ (Nakajima et al. 2023;
Sarkar et al. 2025; Stanton et al. 2026). Due to the lower
stellar masses and thus lower metallicity ranges probed
by these studies, some of them adopt different sets of
strong-line calibrations. For example, Nakajima et al.
(2023) and Sarkar et al. (2025) primarily use R23 and/or
O3 in the lower-branch regime, where O32 and Ne3O2
instead plateau. Interestingly, the low-mass end of our
5.0< z <7.0 MZR is broadly consistent with these pre-
vious measurements, whereas the high-mass end aligns
more closely with the 2.7< z <4.0 and 4.0< z <5.0
relations. These features of the 5.0< z <7.0 MZR
reflect a combination of physical effects: rapid chemi-
cal maturation of higher-mass galaxies prior toz∼5,
bringing them closer to their lower redshift counterparts
in metallicity, while lower-mass systems at these early
epochs are still far from equilibrium due to strong gas
accretion and outflows that hinder the buildup of met-
als. However, because the 5.0< z <7.0 relation is con-
strained by only two stacked points, the current data do
not yet allow us to concretely verify this possible mass-
dependent chemical enrichment history.
Regardless of the behavior at the highest redshifts,
we find clear evidence that the MZR slope evolves only
weakly fromz∼4.4, while the normalization exhibits
smooth evolution starting atz∼3.24. The inferred
normalization evolution,dlog(O/H)/dz=−0.10±0.02,
is highly consistent with previous measurements from
S21 and Li et al. (2023), who find comparable rates of
metallicity decline with redshift. Beyondz≳3.24, how-
ever, we do not observe significant further decreases in
the overall normalization, in agreement with Nakajima
et al. (2023), who similarly report little evolution over
4< z <10. This mild evolution contrasts with studies
such as Sarkar et al. (2025) and Stanton et al. (2026),
which infer continued metallicity declines toward higher
redshift, with rates ofdlog(O/H)/dz=−0.06±0.01 and
a total decrease of∼0.14 dex fromz∼3.2 toz∼5.5,
respectively.

general envelope spanned by the simulations, although
no single model simultaneously reproduces both the ob-

Atz <5, different simulations capture different as-

particularly aroundM ∗ ∼10 9−9.5 M⊙, but predicts sub-

at fixed stellar mass. EAGLE and SIMBA generally

both the normalization and shape of the observed rela-

EAGLE exhibit a flat relation at low masses followed
by a steeper rise aboveM ∗ ∼10 9.25 M⊙. Indeed, if we
average the flat and steep slopes of the EAGLEz∼6
MZR, the resultant averaged slope would qualitatively
resemble that suggested by our 5< z <7 measurements.
The simulations also differ in their predicted redshift
evolution in normalization. Most models show relatively
smooth decreases in metallicity normalization toward
high redshift, while EAGLE predicts a partial slowing
of this evolution at early times. Atz >5, our measure-
ments are in comparatively good agreement with the

4.4.A weak FMR at high redshifts
In the local universe, galaxies are thought to exist in a
quasi-equilibrium state in which stellar feedback, as well

19
as gas inflows and outflows, collectively regulate the re-
lationship between stellar mass, metallicity, and SFR, as
described by the FMR (e.g., Mannucci et al. 2010; An-
drews & Martini 2013; Curti et al. 2020). As a logical
extension, considerable effort has been devoted to de-
termining whether this regulatory relationship remains
invariant across cosmic time, and if so, when it becomes
established. In the era of JWST surveys, an emerg-
ing picture has begun to develop where high-redshift
galaxies earlier thanz∼4 are systematically offset to-
ward lower metallicities at fixed stellar mass and SFR
compared to the local FMR (e.g., Nakajima et al. 2023;
Curti et al. 2024). Our results support and strengthen
this growing evidence for an evolving FMR across cos-
mic time.
One of the key findings of this paper is the presence of
a statistically significant anti-correlation between SFR
residuals relative to the SFMS and metallicity residu-
als relative to the MZR within individual redshift bins
spanningz∼2–5. Previously, using high signal-to-noise
stacked spectra from MOSDEF, S21 (and Sanders et al.
2018) reported evidence for such an anti-correlation at
z∼2.3. In contrast, studies based on individual galax-
ies, such as Korhonen Cuestas et al. (2025) atz∼2.3
and Stanton et al. (2026) out toz∼5.5, do not recover
statistically significant anti-correlations. Our analysis
therefore provides the first evidence from high signal-
to-noise stacked spectra that this relationship persists
beyondz∼2.3. The existence of this anti-correlation
demonstrates that a connection between star formation
activity and chemical enrichment remains established
within the galaxy population at each epoch. The trend
is present across both low- and high-mass systems and
is especially pronounced in the lower-redshift bins. Nev-
ertheless, the slope of the relation is systematically shal-
lower than observed locally, indicating that metallicity
depends less strongly on SFR at fixed stellar mass in
the early universe. Notably, while we do not recover
this anti-correlated relation in the full 5.0≤z <7.0
bin, the two high-mass stacks display a negative trend
similar to those of the lower-redshift bins, though con-
sistent with zero slope within the 1σuncertainties. This
marginal evidence adds to our discussion in Section 4.2
of the mismatch in the behavior of the MZR at high- and
low-masses in the 5.0≤z <7.0 range. The galaxy pop-
ulation at this redshift appears to exist in two different
evolutionary stages — one where the physical processes
known to regulate galaxy growth at lower redshifts begin
to be established in galaxies with higher stellar masses,
and another at lower masses where turbulent processes
still dominate galaxy assembly as seen at even higher
redshifts.
Another piece of evidence for an evolving M ∗-Z-SFR
relation comes from ourµ α-based analysis of the FMR.
We find that the value ofαthat minimizes the intrinsic
scatter in the MZR is consistently lower than the val-
ues derived from stacked SDSS galaxies ofα∼0.55−
0.66 (e.g., Andrews & Martini 2013; Curti et al. 2020;
Sanders et al. 2021), though broadly consistent with the
original Mannucci et al. (2010) result ofα= 0.32, as well
asα= 0.33 at high redshift from Stanton et al. (2026).
Moreover, incorporating SFR produces only a marginal
reduction in scatter (∼3−10%) compared to the sub-
stantial tightening observed atz∼0 (≳50%; Mannucci
et al. 2010; Andrews & Martini 2013; Curti et al. 2020).
These findings are also qualitatively consistent with re-
cent cosmological simulations, most of which find that
αevolves with redshift and that the coupling between
metallicity and SFR weakens toward earlier cosmic times
(e.g., Garcia et al. 2025). However, some recent studies
report little to no evidence for an FMR at high redshift
and low stellar masses (e.g., Korhonen Cuestas et al.
2025), and even indications of a trend opposite to the
conventional formulation (Laseter et al. 2025). Mean-
while, S21 find a factor of 1.3–2 decrease in scatter at
z∼2.3 and 3.3, similar to the improvement seen at
z∼0. While this result appears in contrast with ours,
we note that the MOSDEF sample probes systemati-
cally higher stellar masses than those considered in this
work. In a recent study, Laseter et al. (2025) suggest
that the FMR weakens towards lower stellar masses and
may even reverse at the lowest masses, even atz∼0.
Since our sample extends to lower stellar masses, our
weaker evidence for an FMR may reflect mass depen-
dence in the M ∗-Z-SFR relation. This interpretation is
consistent with a scenario in which low-mass galaxies at
high redshift have not yet settled into the tight equilib-
rium relation observed in more massive systems.
A possible explanation for the weakened coupling be-
tween stellar mass, metallicity, and SFR at high red-
shift is that the physical processes governing star forma-
tion and chemical enrichment become increasingly de-
coupled. Using the IllustrisTNG simulation suite, Tor-
rey et al. (2018) showed that the local FMR emerges
when offsets from the SFMS and MZR vary coherently
on similar timescales. However, high-redshift galax-
ies are observed to experience more bursty, feedback-
driven star formation histories, causing SFRs to fluctu-
ate on relatively short timescales (e.g., Faucher-Gigu` ere
2018; Clarke et al. 2025; Cole et al. 2025; Perry et al.
2025). In contrast, the gas-phase metallicity may evolve
more gradually, on timescales comparable to the dy-
namical time of the dark matter halo, particularly if
outflows remove gas with metallicities similar to that of

20
the ISM (Torrey et al. 2018). This mismatch in vari-
ability timescales would naturally weaken the observed
coupling between SFR and metallicity at fixed stellar
mass. Additionally, the substantially larger gas fractions
present in high-redshift galaxies may buffer or dilute the
metallicity response to pristine gas inflows during peri-
ods of elevated star formation (Somerville & Dav´ e 2015;
Dav´ e et al. 2017b). In this picture, stochastic star for-
mation and strong outflows may represent the typical
conditions in galaxies rather than exceptional transient
states. Such a scenario is qualitatively consistent with
both observations and simulations indicating that high-
redshift galaxies possess large gas reservoirs, elevated
SFRs, and strong feedback-driven outflows (e.g., Hay-
ward & Hopkins 2017; Sparre et al. 2017; Tacconi et al.
2020; Tacchella et al. 2020; Simmonds et al. 2025).
4.5.The required parameters for future MZR/FMR
studies at high redshift
Altogether, recent works reveal an increasingly diverse
picture of chemical enrichment and galaxy evolution,
with different studies probing distinct regions of stellar
mass, star formation activity, and cosmic time. Cur-
rent observational studies of galaxy chemical evolution,
enabled by JWST, analyze samples that span a large
dynamic range in stellar mass, reaching even as low as
106 M⊙ and as early asz= 10 (e.g., Chemerynska et al.
2024; Stanton et al. 2026; Pollock et al. 2026). However,
the samples analyzed comprise a relatively small number
of galaxies in comparison with ground-based observa-
tional surveys such as FMOS-COSMOS, MOSDEF, and
KBSS-MOSFIRE, for which the large sample size en-
ables a fine sampling of galaxy properties in both stellar
mass and redshift (Zahid et al. 2014; Sanders et al. 2021;
Topping et al. 2021; Korhonen Cuestas et al. 2025). The
comparison of the current literature with works at cos-
mic noon highlights the need for a large representative
sample star-forming galaxies with metallicity estimates
across cosmic time.
Our results suggest that our sample sizes atz∼1.4 to
z∼5.0 can be sufficient to constrain low-order relations
such as the MZR. However, higher-order analyses, such
as deviations from the SFMS and MZR, as well as mea-
surements of the intrinsic scatter of the FMR, require
substantially larger samples. Considering the MZR, for
example, matching the signal-to-noise of each stack to
those of S21 by requiring at least 50 galaxies per stack,
while also constructing three stacks per redshift range to
robustly constrain the MZR slope, would require∼150
galaxies per redshift range. Alternatively, reaching a fine
stellar mass resolution ofδlog(M ∗/M⊙)∼0.3 would re-
quire approximately five stacks per redshift bin; even
with a more modest 30 galaxies per bin, this would sim-
ilarly demand a minimum of∼150 galaxies per red-
shift range. Achieving such sample sizes, even across
a mass range of only 10 8.5−10 M⊙, would therefore re-
quire roughly a factor of 2–3 increase in the available
data. Expanding to a broader dynamic range in stellar
mass would further increase the required sample size.
Ultimately, a sample size at least as extensive as ex-
isting ground-based surveys at cosmic noon is needed.
An extended sample would provide sufficient mass and
temporal resolution to robustly trace galaxy evolution
through cosmic time, as well as constrain simulations
and models of galaxy growth.
5.CONCLUSION
In this study, we have used the JADES DR3 dataset
to analyze the star-forming galaxy MZR and FMR in
the redshift range 1.4< z <7 for galaxies atM ∗ >
108.5 M⊙. We implement the novel strong-line calibra-
tions from S25 and conduct a robust stacking analysis
to 601 (418) galaxies across four bins in redshift and
14 (10) bins in stellar mass. This work leverages the
largest sample atz≳2 to date, providing insights into
the chemical evolution of the high-redshift galaxy pop-
ulation. We summarize our main conclusions as follows:
1. We fit the MZR in the redshift ranges 1.4≤z <
2.7, 2.7≤z <4.0, 4.0≤z <5.0, and 5.0≤
z <7.0 (see Table 4). The slope of the MZR
stays constant between 1.4≤z <5.0 atγ∼0.21
and steepens toγ= 0.41 in the 5.0≤z <7.0
range. The normalization decreases fromZ 9 ∼
8.5 atz∼0 to 8.29 and 8.17 atz∼2.08 and
z∼3.24, corresponding to a smooth evolution of
dlog(O/H)/dz=−0.10±0.02. The decrease in
normalization fromz∼3.24 toz∼4.40 (Z 9 =
8.15) of 0.02 dex is very mild. While we obtain
Z9 = 8.06 for the 5.0≤z <7.0 relation, the high-
mass end matches up with the MZRs atz∼3.24
andz∼4.40, suggesting that the low-mass end
drives the steepening of the MZR in this highest
redshift range.
2. We find that thez≳2 FMR is offset below the
z∼0 FMR by∼ −0.15 dex, suggesting a redshift-
dependent evolution of the FMR. This finding is in
contrast with previous works that suggest a non-
evolving FMR with redshift out toz∼3 (S21),
but is in agreement with recent works suggest-
ing that galaxies may exhibit lower metallicities
at fixed SFR andM ∗ than would be suggested by
thez∼0 FMR (e.g., Nakajima et al. 2023; Curti
et al. 2024).

21
3. We recover statistically significant anti-
correlations between SFMS offsets and MZR off-
sets in the 1.4≤z <2.7, 2.7≤z <4.0, and
4.0≤z <5.0 redshift bins, detecting this anti-
correlation for the first time atz≳3. The slopes
ofν=−0.13±0.05,ν=−0.08±0.04, and
ν=−0.09±0.06 are shallower than that in the lo-
cal Universe (ν∼ −0.27). A relationship between
SFR and metallicity appears to be established by
these epochs, albeit with a weaker coupling than
observed atz∼0.
4. We estimate the FMR projection parameter to be
α= 0.26−0.38, falling below the more typically
assumed value ofα∼0.6 of the local Universe.
Accordingly, the decrease in the MZR scatter that
occurs when incorporating galaxy SFRs (i.e., con-
structing the FMR) is marginal, varying between
3-11%, as opposed to the up to 70% decrease in
scatter in the local Universe (e.g., Andrews & Mar-
tini 2013; Curti et al. 2020). This lower value
ofαsuggests a weaker anti-correlation between
SFR and metallicity at fixed stellar mass than is
typically assumed. However, this lowerαvalue
agrees well with studies that estimate this param-
eter from individual, rather than stacked, galaxy
measurements.
