ABSTRACT
The cosmic ray ionization rate (CRIR) is a key parameter governing the physical, chemical and thermal evo-
lution of the interstellar medium. The primary technique for measuring the CRIR in diffuse molecular clouds
relies on observations ofH +
3 . Previous analyses of these observations have derived the CRIR under the as-
sumption of steady-state chemistry. Here, we investigate the effect of time-dependent chemistry on the inferred
CRIR fromH +
3 observations. We perform 3D MHD simulations with coupled chemistry and driven turbulence.
Following procedures similar to those used in the literature to analyzeH +
3 observations, we conduct mock
CRIR measurements by post-processing our simulations with different values of the CRIR to obtain steady-
state abundances ofH 2 andH +
3 . By comparing those with the abundances from time-dependent chemistry, we
determine the best-fitting value of the CRIR. We find that the abundances of bothH2 andH +
3 are higher in time-
dependent chemistry simulations than in the steady-state case, especially in low-density regions. Furthermore,
the inferred CRIR under the steady-state assumption is a factor of∼2−5higher than the true CRIR, with a
median value ofζ inferred/ζtrue ≈3. This bias increases with stronger magnetic fields, weaker FUV radiation
fields, and stronger turbulence. Accounting for time-dependent chemistry, we report an average CRIR perH2 of
ζH2 = 2×10 −17 s−1 from theH +
3 observations. The CRIR is consistent with a constant value over the column
density range ofN= (2−6)×10 21 cm−2.
1.INTRODUCTION
Cosmic rays (CRs) are the primary source of ionization in
dense molecular clouds and prestellar cores at values ofA V
greater than a few, where the interstellar ultraviolet radiation
field is attenuated. CRs are involved in many of the important
chemical reactions in the interstellar medium (ISM) by ion-
izing gas-phase molecules (Wakelam et al. 2010; Vasyunin
et al. 2017). On dust grains, CRs may catalyze reactions in
the solid phase (Shingledecker & Herbst 2018), and facili-
tate transfer of material from the solid phase back to the gas
phase (Hasegawa & Herbst 1993). In addition, by affecting
the ionization fraction, the CRs regulate the rate of non-ideal
MHD effects. This affects the timescale for star formation
(Mouschovias et al. 2006), the size and longevity of proto-

planetary disks (Zhao et al. 2016), and the rate of dust coag-
ulation (Silsbee et al. 2020; Guillet et al. 2020). Interestingly,
many of the CR-induced effects are nearly proportional to the
cosmic ray ionization rate (CRIR), and thus the influence of
cosmic rays in the ISM is often parameterized by the CRIR
of molecular hydrogen,ζ H2.
The first attempts to investigate the CRIR used near-Earth
measurements of the energy spectrum of CRs, estimating a
ζH2 ≈10 −17 s−1 (Spitzer & Tomasko 1968). In the subse-
quent decades, attempts to quantify the ionization rate have
focused on chemical modeling of different molecular ions
(O’Donnell & Watson 1974; Black & Dalgarno 1977; Fed-
erman et al. 1996). The CRIR has been inferred usingH +
3 ,
OH+,H2O +,ArH+in diffuse molecular clouds (Indriolo
et al. 2015; Neufeld & Wolfire 2017a; Bacalla et al. 2019),
and usingHCO +,DCO +,N 2H+,N2D + in dense molec-
ular cores (Caselli et al. 1998; Redaelli et al. 2021; Pineda
et al. 2024). These measurements yield a value of CRIR in
the range of∼10 −18 −10 −15 s−1. Recently, JWST observa-
tions ofH 2 rovibrational line emission provide a new method
of measuring CRIR. However, this method is limited so far
to one cloud, Barnard 68, withζ H2 = 1.7×10 −16 s−1 (Bialy
et al. 2026).

Among all the observational techniques of deriving CRIR
from molecular ions,H +
3 has a particularly simple chemistry
in diffuse molecular gas, and thus is a robust way to mea-
sure CRIR that is less subject to the uncertainties in chemical
modeling (McCall et al. 2003). Indriolo et al. (2007) used
observations ofH +
3 from absorption line spectroscopy to in-
fer an average value ofζH2 = 4.6×10 −16 s−1 in a sample of
14 sightlines through diffuse molecular gas. In doing so, they
made a number of simplifying assumptions - namely that the
gas density and temperature were constant along the line of
sight, and that the free electrons in the gas are provided by
carbon, which is assumed to be all in the form of C +. The
gas density was estimated in most cases based on analysis
of the relative level populations of C 2 (Sonnentrucker et al.
2007). Indriolo & McCall (2012) used similar methods as
Indriolo et al. (2007), and expanded the number of sight-
lines withH +
3 detections to 21, obtaining an average value
ofζ H2 = 3.5×10 −16 s−1.
To improve on the assumption of uniform chemistry and
temperature along the line of sight in Indriolo & McCall
(2012), Neufeld & Wolfire (2017b) used a constant-density
1D PDR model with a slab geometry, accounting for varia-
tions in gas temperature and chemical abundances with the
column densityN H. They then re-analyzed the 12 lines of
sight which the gas density was estimated from C2 level pop-
ulations in Indriolo & McCall (2012), and found an average
valueζ H2 = 5.3×10 −16 s−1, as well as evidence for very
strong attenuation of the cosmic ray flux with increasing col-
umn density.
Recently, GAIA dust extinction measurements have en-
abled estimates of the 3D density structure in the local in-
terstellar medium (ISM) (Green et al. 2019; Lallement et al.
2022; Edenhofer et al. 2024). Using the 3D gas density
maps in Edenhofer et al. (2024), Obolentseva et al. (2024)
re-analyzed several sightlines using the 3D-PDR model (Bis-
bas et al. 2012), taking into account the spatial variations of
FUV radiation field, temperature, and chemistry. They found
a reduction in the mean ionization rate by nearly a factor of
10, with an average ofζ H2 = 6×10 −17 s−1. Most of this re-
duction arose because the density estimates from extinction
mapping were considerably lower than previous estimates
from C 2 level populations. This conclusion was supported
by the work on Neufeld et al. (2024), who used updated cal-
culations of the collisional de-excitation rates for rotational
states of C2 to revise many of the previous density estimates
downwards. Expanding on Obolentseva et al. (2024), Indri-
olo et al. (2026) increased the total number of sight lines from
7 to 16, obtaining a mean ofζ H2 = 5.3×10 −17 s−1 with the
standard deviation of2.5×10 −17 s−1.
One important aspect that has not been considered in the
previous studies of CRIR measurements usingH +
3 is the ef-
fect of time-dependent chemistry. In particular,H 2, the pre-
cursor ofH +
3 , is likely to have non-steady-state abundances
in diffuse atomic and molecular clouds whereH+
3 is observed
to constrain the CRIR. The formation timescale ofH 2 can be
longer than the dynamical timescale in these environments
(Glover & Mac Low 2007; Gong et al. 2017, 2018, 2020;
Joshi et al. 2019; Hu et al. 2021; Godard et al. 2023). The
current modeling of CRIR, however, assumes thatH 2 and
H+
3 are both in chemical steady-state. We note that, even
under the assumption of chemical equilibrium, the presence
of strong compressive turbulence in the diffuse interstellar
medium may affect H 2 abundance (Bialy et al. 2019). The
origin of this effect, leading to systematically higher val-
ues compared to equilibrium, is the nonlinearity of H 2 self-
shielding in local density perturbations produced by the tur-
bulence.
The goal of this paper is to investigate the effect of time-
dependent chemistry on CRIR measurements fromH+
3 by us-
ing magneto-hydrodynamic simulations of turbulent molecu-
lar clouds with chemistry. This paper is organized as follows.
Section 2 describes the method for our numerical simulations
and CRIR mock observations. Section 3 states the results
from our analysis. Section 4 discusses the implications of
our results on the observations of CRIR. Finally, Section 5
summarizes the main conclusions from this paper.
2.METHODS
2.1.Numerical Simulations
We conduct simulations with coupled MHD and chem-
istry using the publicly available Athena++ code (Stone
et al. 2020). The chemistry module in Athena++ with self-
consistent heating and cooling is implemented in Gong et al.
(2023). We perform 3D simulations of the multiphase ISM
with driven turbulence using a Cartesian grid.
2.1.1.Chemical Network
We use the simplified carbon and oxygen network for
atomic and molecular ISM in Gong et al. (2017). This net-
work is widely used and well tested, has been implemented
in Athena++ (Gong et al. 2023), and is publicly available.
It has the advantage of being relatively simple with a low
computational cost, while still accurately capturing the main
chemical and thermal processes in the atomic and molecular
ISM. This network includes 18 atomic and molecular species
of the elementsH,He,C,O, andSi, and 50 reactions. Heat-
ing and cooling processes from chemical reactions, FUV ra-
diation, and atomic and molecular line cooling are included.
The FUV radiation field was calculated using the six-ray ra-
diation transfer module, which performs ray tracing along
Cartesian grids to the edge of the simulation box.

2.1.2.Turbulence Driving
To induce and maintain turbulence, we inject stochas-
tic velocity perturbations in Fourier space using large-scale
Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) forcing, as commonly adopted in
multiphase ISM simulations (Kritsuk et al. 2018; Ho et al.
2025). The forcing acts on modes withk/k min ∈[1.5,2.5]
using a parabolic weighting that peaks atk/k min = 2, where
kmin ≡2π/Lcorresponds to the box scale. We construct the
driving field as an equal mixture of solenoidal and compres-
sive components with correlation timet corr = 2 Myr. The
forcing amplitude is calibrated such that, in steady state, the
velocity dispersionu rms ≈5.0 km,s −1, in agreement with
the observed velocity dispersion of the ISM on the length
scale of25 pc(Koley 2023).
2.1.3.Initial and Boundary Conditions
To represent diffuse molecular-cloud conditions, we ini-
tialize a cubic domain with a Cartesian grid of side length
L= 50 pc. The initial gas density is uniform with number
density of hydrogen nuclein= 10 cm −3, corresponding to a
mean hydrogen column densityN=nL≃1.5×10 21 cm−2.
We impose an initial uniform magnetic fieldB0 =B 0 ˆz. The
boundary condition is periodic in all directions for the MHD
variables. The six-ray approximation is used to model the
external irradiation (Gong et al. 2023). We apply an inci-
dent FUV radiation field on all six faces of the domain and
propagate the radiation inward along the Cartesian coordi-
nates, attenuating the radiation field by dust- and molecular-
shielding. We adopt a constant CRIR throughout the simula-
tion domain.
The elemental abundances are set to be typical values of
solar neighborhood ISM, with 0.1 for helium,1.6×10 −4 for
carbon (Sofia et al. 2004),3.2×10 −4 for oxygen (Savage &
Sembach 1996), and1.7×10 −6 for silicon (Cardelli et al.
1994). The initial temperatures and species abundances are
set by evolving the chemistry and associated heating/cooling
to equilibrium at the start of the calculation. We run each
simulation for26 Myr, corresponding to approximately5
times the eddies turnover time at the forcing scale, to en-
sure the turbulence is fully developed and in statistical steady
state.

RecentH +

modeling of the full line-of-sight structure rather than the
single-zone steady-state estimator in Eq. (4). In particular,
Obolentseva et al. (2024); Indriolo et al. (2026) combine
parsec-resolution 3D dust maps constraining local density
structure along each sightline with 3D-PDR calculations to
predictH 2 andH +

matches the observed ratio ofN H2 andN H+

simulations. This inferred CRIR can then be directly com-

simulations.
2.2.1.Post-processing with Steady-state Chemistry
Following the method of deriving steady-state chemi-
cal abundances from density structures in the analysis of
H+
3 observations (Obolentseva et al. 2024; Indriolo et al.
2026), we construct steady-state chemical abundances by
post-processing our simulations with time-dependent chem-
istry. We select the final simulation snapshot and hold the
gas density and velocity fixed. We then integrate the chemi-
cal rate equations, temperature evolution, and radiation trans-
fer forward in time for1 Gyrto ensure that the chemistry
has reached a steady-state with no advection of chemical
species or dynamical evolution of the gas. We adopt the
same incident FUV field strength as in the time-dependent
simulation, and vary the CRIR of molecular hydrogen over
the rangeζ p ∈[2×10 −18,5×10 −16] s −1, sampled at
12 evenly logarithmically spaced values. For the HCR run,
we additionally extend the post-processing range ofζ p up
to2.43×10 −15 s−1. For each choice ofζ p, we obtain the
steady-stateH 2 andH +
3 abundances used in our mock CRIR
inference (Section 2.2.3).

Why is theH 2 abundance higher with time-dependent
chemistry in diffuse molecular gas than with steady-state
chemistry? This arises from the nonlinearity ofH 2
self-shielding, which decreases the photo-dissociation rate
steeply as the column density ofH 2 increases above
N(H 2)≳10 19 cm−2 (Draine & Bertoldi 1996). When
H2 gas is brought from dense to diffuse gas by turbulent ad-
vection, it can be protected from photo-dissociation by self-
shielding, enablingH 2 to survive longer than the dynami-
cal timescale in low-density regions in which it would not
form efficiently with steady-state chemistry. The same phe-
nomenon of increasedH 2 abundance in diffuse molecular
clouds with time-dependent chemistry has been seen in other
simulations (Joshi et al. 2019; Hu et al. 2021; Godard et al.
2023).
In dense gas withn≳100 cm −3 andN≳10 21 cm−2, the
chemical timescale is comparable or shorter than the dynam-
ical timescale, and the time-dependent chemical abundances
approach the steady-state solution.

4.4.Comparison with the Voyager Measurements
Our estimated true mean ionization rate is slightly be-
low what would be produced by the local cosmic ray en-
ergy spectrum measured by the V oyager probes (Cummings
et al. 2016; Stone et al. 2019). Cummings et al. (2016)
estimated a primary ionization rate of atomic hydrogen of
ζp = (1.01−1.09)×10 −17 s−1. This estimate is a lower
bound, since it neglects the contribution of particles with en-
ergies less than 3 MeV (the lowest energy measured with
V oyager). Using the standard relation (Glassgold & Langer
1974b) that the total ionization rate of molecular hydrogen
is 2.3 times the primary ionization rate of atomic hydro-
gen, this corresponds to an ionization rate of approximately
ζH2 = 2.4×10 −17 s−1. If we use the more accurately deter-
mined ratio of secondary to primary ionization of 1.0 (Ivlev
et al. 2021), and keep the ratio of the ionization cross-section
of molecular hydrogen to that of atomic hydrogen equal to
1.53 (Glassgold & Langer 1974b), then we obtain a value of
approximatelyζ H2 = 3.1×10 −17 s−1. We reiterate that
this is a lower bound. Using a spectrum similar to V oyager,
and taking into account shielding by intervening material,
(Indriolo et al. 2026) find an ionization rate between 3 and
5×10 −17 s−1 at column densities between 1 and 6×10 21
cm−2.
5.CONCLUSIONS
In this paper, we investigate the effect of time-dependent
chemistry on the CRIR inferred fromH +
3 observations. We
perform 3D MHD simulations of the atomic and molecu-
lar ISM with coupled chemistry and driven turbulence. To
assess the assumption of steady-state chemistry adopted in
the original analysis ofH +
3 observations (Obolentseva et al.
2024; Indriolo et al. 2026), we post-process our simula-
tions with time-dependent chemistry and construct steady-
state chemical abundances over a range of CRIR values.
Mock CRIR measurements are obtained along each simu-
lation sightline based on the observational procedure of se-
lecting the CRIR for which the steady-state abundance ratio
NH+
3
/NH2 matches the time-dependent (“observed”) value.
This inferred CRIR (ζinferred) is then compared to the “true”
CRIR (ζ true) adopted in the time-dependent simulations to
quantify the observational bias. Our main findings are sum-
marized as follows:
1. The abundances of bothH +
3 andH 2 are higher in
time-dependent chemistry simulations than the steady-
state values (Figures 1 and 2), with the difference
being most pronounced in low-density regions (n≲
100 cm−3). This enhancement is caused by the advec-
tion ofH 2 from dense to diffuse gas, combined with
the efficient self-shielding ofH 2. Due to its short reac-
tion timescales,H +
3 remains in almost perfect chemical
equilibrium withH 2.
2. The column density ratioN H+
3
/NH2 is higher in time-
dependent chemistry simulations than in the steady-
state case (Figure 3), due to the presence ofH+
3 andH 2
in lower density regions with time-dependent chem-
istry.
3. The inferred CRIR under the steady-state assumption
is a factor of∼2−5higher than the true CRIR in
time-dependent chemistry simulations (Figures 4 and
6), with a median value ofζ inferred/ζtrue ≈3(Ta-
ble 2). This implies that properly accounting for time-
dependent chemistry yields both a lower average value
and a smaller dispersion of CRIR from observations.
4.ζ inferred/ζtrue is higher along sightlines with lower
column densities, where the time-dependent effect is
more prominent (Figure 5).

10
5.ζ inferred/ζtrue increases with stronger magnetic fields,
weaker FUV radiation field, and stronger turbulence
(Figure 6). Our fiducial simulation produces a distri-
bution ofζ inferred/ζtrue similar to that in the lower-
resolution run, demonstrating numerical convergence.
6. Accounting for time-dependent chemistry, we report
an average CRIR perH 2 ofζ H2 = 2×10 −17 s−1 from
theH +
3 observations, lower than the value given by the
original analyses in Obolentseva et al. (2024) and In-
driolo et al. (2026). Our value of CRIR is close to that
implied by the V oyager measurement of the CR spec-
trum (Cummings et al. 2016).
7. Time-dependent chemistry can account for most of the
observed dispersion in the CRIR, as well as the trend
with column density in the original analysis under the
steady-state assumption. The resulting CRIR is consis-
tent with a constant value in the column density range
ofN= (2−6)×10 21 cm−2.
