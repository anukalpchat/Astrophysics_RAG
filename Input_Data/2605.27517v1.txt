ABSTRACT
Context. Multi-wavelength surveys place cloud dispersal at ∼ 1–5 Myr after massive-star emergence, before the first
core-collapse supernovae. Whether a cloud disperses, re-collapses, or leaks Lyman-continuum (LyC) photons depends
on how pre-supernova winds, radiation pressure, and photoionised-gas pressure ( PH ii) couple to the swept-up shell.
Aims. We introduce Trinity, a one-dimensional thin-shell code that succeeds warpfield and follows stellar feedback
across flexible cloud density profiles.
Methods. Trinity evolves the bubble-shell structure under winds, supernovae, direct and dust-reprocessed radiation
pressure, PH ii, and gravity. A phase-aware driving prescription uses the larger of the hot-bubble and photoionised-gas
pressures in the energy-driven phase, and PH ii plus direct ram pressure in the momentum-driven phase. The initial
cloud may be uniform, a piecewise power law, or a Bonnor–Ebert sphere. Shell structure, hot-bubble cooling, photon
absorption, and LyC escape are solved with the dynamics.
Results. We validate the code against analytic wind and photoionisation limits and explore runs with cloud mass
Mcloud = 10 5–106.5 M⊙, core density ncore = 10 3–104 cm−3, and star-formation eﬀiciency ε = 0.01–0.30. Including PH ii
changes the dynamics measurably, increasing the shell radius by ∼ 17% at 10 Myr in the fiducial validation run. In a
higher-eﬀiciency example, the energy-driven phase lasts less than 1 Myr, radiation pressure remains sub-dominant, and
PH ii remains dynamically important after the transition to momentum-dominated evolution. Density structure changes
both phase durations and outcomes: at fixed Mcloud, ncore, and ε, homogeneous and shallow profiles re-collapse, while
a steep ρ ∝ r−2 cloud keeps expanding. In a dispersing grid, Bonnor–Ebert clouds reach cloud dispersal time τdisp
roughly ∼ 55% later than homogeneous clouds.
Conclusions. The first results show that both PH ii and cloud structure are important for a complete dynamical treatment:
PH ii alters late-time shell expansion, while density structure shifts dispersal thresholds and emergence times even when
the stellar population is fixed. Trinity provides a highly eﬀicient, interpretable framework for mapping feedback
dominance across cloud parameter space, providing useful tool to study resolved ii regions.

1. Introduction
Giant molecular clouds (GMCs) form stars ineﬀiciently.
Across nearby galaxies, integrated cloud-scale star forma-
tion eﬀiciencies are roughly a few per cent to ten per cent
(Lada et al. 2010), and parent clouds disperse within 1–
5 Myr once massive stars emerge ( Chevance et al. 2020;
Kim et al. 2022). The first core-collapse supernovae (SNe)
arrive only after ∼ 3–4 Myr, hinting that cloud clearing
is caused by pre-supernova feedback (e.g., photoionisation,
stellar winds, and radiation pressure; see Krumholz 2014;
Dale 2015; Reissl et al. 2018). The remaining question is
which of these channels dominates, when, and for which
cloud properties.

LEGUS (Legacy ExtraGalactic UV Survey; Calzetti et al.

The cloud dispersal time is not universal; cluster mass
is one of the variables it depends on, with more massive
clusters emerging from their natal gas earlier than lower-
mass ones ( Pedrini et al. 2026). McQuaid et al. (2024) finds
that low-mass clusters in NGC 4449 remain embedded for
5–6 Myr, while more massive clusters expose their surround-
ings by ∼ 4 Myr. The mechanism behind cloud-clearing also
changes with scale. Barnes et al. (2021) analyses ∼ 5800 ii
regions across 19 galaxies and find that most are overpres-
sured and expanding. Direct radiation pressure dominates
mostly in the most compact, embedded, or luminous envi-
ronments: in 30 Doradus it exceeds photoionised gas pres-
sure within ∼ 10 pc of the central cluster ( Lopez et al. 2011,
2014), whereas in Galactic regions with r ≲ 0.5 pc dust-
reprocessed infrared radiation pressure dominates roughly
84 % of cases ( Olivier et al. 2021). Beyond the compact
phase, photoionised gas pressure usually takes over ( Pathak
et al. 2025b). Resolved studies that connect pressure terms
to candidate ionising stellar populations recover this hierar-
chy and begin to map its dependence on metallicity, cluster
mass, and evolutionary state ( Barnes et al. 2022; Scheuer-
mann et al. 2023; Rowland et al. 2024; Barnes et al. 2026;
Scheuermann et al. 2026). Direct cross-matching of star-
cluster catalogues with ii-region catalogues further con-
strains how many ionising photons leak into the diffuse
ionised gas ( Teh et al. 2023; Scheuermann et al. 2026).
The observational target is therefore no longer limited
to measuring cloud lifetime; rather, it is to understand
how much feedback couples to the natal cloud, how much
leaks out, and what medium the first supernova encounters.
The same pre-supernova clearing physics sets the environ-
ment into which supernovae explode ( Watkins et al. 2023;
Barnes et al. 2023). Radiation-hydrodynamic calculations
show that winds and photoionisation carve low-density cav-
ities tens of parsecs across, reducing the density of the sur-
rounding interstellar medium (ISM; Fichtner et al. 2024).
If the expanding cavity has already reached the edge of
the cloud, the supernova expands in a semi-confined fash-
ion through low-density channels rather than spherically
into an intact cloud; this can amplify small-scale turbu-
lence ( Lau & Bonnell 2025). Modelling the pre-SN phase
correctly is therefore a precondition for modelling SN feed-
back at all.
Existing tools cover different slices of this problem.
Semi-analytic codes such as warpfield (Rahner et al.
2017, 2019) follow winds, radiation pressure, supernovae,
cooling, and gravity on cloud scales and can survey
broad parameter ranges eﬀiciently. warpfield-emp cou-
ples warpfield to cloudy (Ferland et al. 2017) and po-
laris (Reissl et al. 2016) to predict emission lines from
evolving ii regions ( Pellegrini et al. 2020), while toddlers
(Kapoor et al. 2023, 2024) computes emission libraries for
star-forming regions by coupling similar evolution models
to the radiative transfer code skirt (Baes et al. 2011).
Jiménez et al. (2026) includes the metallicity dependence
of the wind–cooling balance, identifying a standing-shell
regime in low-metallicity clouds. Three-dimensional sim-
ulations follow feedback with similar physical fidelity but
at much higher computational cost (see Walch et al. 2015;
Girichidis et al. 2016; Smith et al. 2020; Grudić et al. 2021).
The parameter space of the problem is large: the cloud
mass, mean density and density profile, the cluster mass and
the metallicity can all have a significant impact on the evo-
lution of the feedback-driven cavity. Mapping this param-
eter space statistically requires an eﬀicient forward model
that follows every form of pre-SN feedback self-consistently,
and produces outputs that align directly with resolved ob-
servations. Thus Trinity is built for that role: it cou-
ples winds, supernovae, direct and dust-reprocessed radia-
tion pressure, photoionised gas pressure ( PH ii), and gravity
through a continuous energy- to momentum-driven bubble
evolution. In this first paper of the Trinity series, we intro-
duce two changes relative to warpfield. First, PH ii enters
the shell equation of motion explicitly and combines with
the hot-bubble pressure Pb. Second, the initial cloud is not
restricted to simple homogeneous density profiles; uniform,
power-law, and Bonnor–Ebert structures can be compared
at different cloud mass and star formation eﬀiciency.
This study is structured as follows: Section 2 describes
the Trinity model. Section 3 validates against analytic
limits and warpfield, Sect. 4 demonstrates the diagnos-
tic suite, and Sect. 5 collects the limitations before Sect. 6
concludes.
2. The TRINITY model
Trinity1 is a one-dimensional, spherically symmetric thin-
shell code 2 that follows the expansion of a feedback-driven
bubble and swept-up shell into a giant molecular cloud. It
builds on the warpfield framework ( Rahner et al. 2017,
2019), with the extensions noted in Sect. 1: an explicit
photoionised-gas-pressure term in the shell equation of mo-
tion, a phase-aware combination of PH ii and the bubble
pressure across the energy- to momentum-driven transition,
and support for Bonnor–Ebert profiles alongside uniform
and power-law clouds. The code solves a coupled system
of ordinary differential equations for the bubble radius Rb,
bubble velocity vb, and bubble thermal energy Eb, sub-
ject to forces from stellar winds, supernovae, direct and
dust-reprocessed radiation pressure, photoionised gas pres-
sure, and self-gravity of the shell and enclosed cluster. At
each time step, a hydrostatic shell-structure calculation de-
termines the ionisation-front position, absorbed radiation
fraction, and infrared optical depth, so that the coupling
between radiation and gas is solved self-consistently rather
than prescribed.
Trinity is written in Python and uses the SciPy
solve_ivp interface to the LSODA stiff integrator ( Hind-
marsh 1983; Petzold 1983). Stellar wind luminosities, mass-
loss rates, ionising photon rates, and bolometric luminosi-
ties are drawn from starburst99 population synthesis ta-
bles (Leitherer et al. 1999), though the modular input inter-

2.2. Bubble structure
A wind-blown bubble has four zones (Fig. 1; Castor et al.
1975; Weaver et al. 1977): (a) a freely streaming hyper-
sonic stellar wind out to the wind termination shock at
Rts; (b) the hot bubble — a region of shocked stellar wind
(T ∼ 107 K) bounded by Rts and the outer contact discon-
tinuity at Rb; (c) a thin, dense, cold shell of swept-up ISM;
and (d) the undisturbed GMC or ISM.
The hot bubble is collisionally ionised at T ∼ 106–108 K
and suﬀiciently rarefied that Lyman-continuum (LyC) pho-
tons traverse it without absorption, since recombinations
are negligible at those temperatures. Ionising radiation
therefore reaches the swept-up shell at Rb unattenuated,
and the photoionised region always surrounds the bub-
ble, extending from Rb outward to the ionisation front at
Rif ≥ Rb.
The bubble evolves through three dynamical phases de-
pending on whether its internal energy is conserved or radi-
ated away ( Weaver et al. 1977; Mac Low & McCray 1988):
an early energy-driven phase in which the overpressured in-
terior drives the shell ( R ∝ t3/5); a transition phase when
radiative losses drain the thermal reservoir on a sound-
crossing timescale; and a momentum-driven phase in which
the shell coasts on the accumulated momentum of winds
and supernovae (see Sect. 2.7). Density gradients and pho-
toionised gas pressure shift the timing and sharpness of each
transition in real clouds ( Rahner et al. 2017, 2019).

The driving pressure Pdrive in Eq. ( 10) may receive con-

2.4.1. CIE and non-CIE regime
The dominant cooling mode for plasmas of solar metallicity
within the temperature range of 104–107 K is by metal line
transitions. At high temperatures, the plasma can be as-
sumed to be in collisional ionisation equilibrium (CIE; T ≳
105.5 K), meaning an optically thin plasma where collisional
processes dominate, and the cooling/heating timescale tcool
is longer than the ionisation/recombination timescale. In
this regime Λnet reduces to a one-parameter function of
temperature (for fixed abundances), so a tabulated cooling
curve interpolated in T is enough.
Our choice of the temperature at which the transi-
tion from non-CIE to CIE cooling occurs follows simple
timescale arguments: the timescale on which gas radiates
thermal energy is tcool ∼ U (dU /dt)−1 ∝ T /Λ(T ) at fixed
density, and the cooling function Λ(T ) peaks near 105–5.5 K
owing to metal-line emission (from e.g. Fe, O; see Schure
et al. 2009). By contrast, the ion fractions relax approxi-
mately on the recombination time trec ∼ αeff(T )−1, with
αeff(T ) ∝ T −η, 0.5 ≲ η ≲ 1.5 (Verner & Ferland 1996)
so that trec lengthens as T rises. For temperatures be-
low the peak in the cooling function, one therefore ex-
pects trec ≳ tcool: the gas cools faster than charge states
can re-equilibrate, and the ionization state lags the in-
stantaneous temperature (non-CIE; see Sutherland & Do-
pita 1993; Gnat & Sternberg 2007; Oppenheimer & Schaye
2013). At temperatures above the peak, however, Λ(T ) de-
clines and tcool grows rapidly; the inequality reverses, and
CIE becomes a good approximation.
This simplification breaks down whenever a strong pho-
toionising radiation field is present or when the gas un-
dergoes rapid thermal or dynamical evolution. Both condi-
tions are generically satisfied inside ii regions and feedback-
driven bubbles: the central cluster continuously irradiates
the surrounding gas with a time-evolving ultraviolet spec-
trum, and the bubble interior can cool rapidly once Eb be-
gins to drain. Under non-CIE conditions, the charge-state
distribution is set by competition between photoionisation,
collisional ionisation, and recombination, and Λnet depends
explicitly on four parameters: the gas temperature T , the
gas density n, the ionising photon flux ϕi, and the cluster
age tage (Rahner et al. 2019). The density enters because
it modulates the recombination rate, and the cluster age
matters because older clusters produce softer spectra that
are less effective at ionising metals. At high temperatures
(T ≳ 105.5 K) the collisionally ionised fractions dominate
even in the presence of a strong radiation field, so non-CIE
corrections are small.

2.8. Stopping criteria
The bubble has two natural endpoints. In one, gravity wins
and the shell re-collapses; in the other, the bubble con-
tinues to expand until it dissolves into the ambient ISM.
We evolve each model until one of two stopping criteria is
met. The first is a temporal cutoff set by the lifetime of
the massive stars that supply the momentum and energy,
carried through the latest plausible supernovae; the second
is a spatial cutoff at which the structure ceases to be a co-
herent shell and instead merges with its surroundings. To
this end, we adopt fiducial thresholds of tmax = 30 Myr and
Rmax = 500 pc.

At this deliberately low ε, the feedback budget sits near
the dispersal threshold. The density profile therefore con-
trols the shell trajectory by setting how fast the swept-
up mass grows. While Rb < R core, all four clouds present
the same central density to the bubble, so the early tra-
jectories overlap in Fig. 4. Once the shell leaves the core,
the runs diverge. In the homogeneous cloud, the shell en-
counters a mass reservoir that grows rapidly with radius,
Msh ∝ R3. The bubble remains compact, the shell loads
mass eﬀiciently, and the energy-driven phase lasts 0.84 Myr.
In the steep αρ = −2 cloud, the density falls fast enough
that the shell mass grows only linearly with radius outside
the core. The shell therefore loads much less mass at large
radii, accelerates down the density gradient, and leaves the
energy-driven phase after 0.19 Myr.
The Bonnor–Ebert sphere and the αρ = −1 power law
fall between these limits, with energy-phase durations of
0.44 Myr and 0.26 Myr, respectively. The Bonnor–Ebert
profile tracks the αρ = −1 run closely because, over the
radii sampled by the shell, its envelope has a similar effec-
tive slope. The outcomes then split cleanly. Three profiles
turn around and re-collapse on timescales of tcoll = 1 .5–
2.7 Myr. Only the αρ = −2 cloud keeps expanding through-
out the 4 Myr runtime 7. The reason is simple: in the steep
profile, the shell reaches the low-density envelope before
gravity and inertia can trap it. A steeper density profile
therefore lowers the minimum ε required for dispersal at
fixed Mcloud, ncore, and Rcore.
Three broader implications follow. First, in this
marginal case, the cloud outcome is decided before super-
novae dominate. The first core-collapse supernovae occur
after ∼ 3–4 Myr; by then, the re-collapsing models have
already turned around, while the dispersing model has al-
ready escaped the dense cloud. Winds, radiation pressure,
and photoionised-gas pressure therefore determine the fate
of the cloud in this experiment. Second, the re-collapse
times of 1.5–2.7 Myr and the dispersal time of the expand-
ing model fall in the same Myr-scale window inferred from
CO-to-Hα spatial decorrelation measurements in nearby
galaxies (Chevance et al. 2020; Kruijssen et al. 2019). Trin-
ity produces this range by changing only the density pro-
file at fixed Mcloud and ε. Third, the existence of a critical
slope between αρ = −1 and −2 means that the minimum
star-formation eﬀiciency for cloud dispersal is not a single
number. It depends on cloud structure. Extragalactic mea-
surements of cloud-scale eﬀiciencies necessarily average over
a distribution of density profiles, and population predic-
tions, such as bubble size distributions compared with the
PHANGS-JWST census in NGC 628, should marginalise
over that distribution rather than assume a canonical ho-
mogeneous cloud or a single power-law slope ( Watkins et al.
2023).

5. Limitations
5.1. Geometry and initial conditions
Real regions do not evolve so cleanly. Density gradients
drive blister and champagne flows ( Tenorio-Tagle 1979;
Geen et al. 2020), turbulent clouds open low column den-
sity escape channels through which hot gas vents preferen-
tially, rather than pushing isotropically on the whole shell
(Harper-Clark & Murray 2009; Lancaster et al. 2024). Our
1D treatment averages over this angular structure and re-
places it with a single effective radius.
Spherical symmetry is a better approximation than it
might at first appear. Many observed ii regions are approx-
imately round in projection: the Rosette Nebula ( Celnik
1985), the Orion Veil shell ( Pabst et al. 2019), RCW 120
(Luisi et al. 2021), and a wide variety of Galactic infrared
ring nebulae ( Churchwell et al. 2006). Extragalactic sur-
veys show the same mixture of order and complexity: most
young, compact ii regions in nearby spirals are well de-
scribed by circular or mildly elliptical apertures ( Hannon
et al. 2022; Barnes et al. 2026), though older superbubbles
increasingly deviate from sphericity as galactic shear and
bubble merging take over ( Watkins et al. 2023). The Mag-
ellanic Clouds provide a nearby laboratory for this range of
morphologies. MCELS and DeMCELS ( Smith & MCELS
Team 1999; Points et al. 2024) map the ionised gas across
the LMC at parsec-scale resolution, from relatively simple
shells and superbubbles to the highly structured 30 Do-
radus complex. The same machinery may also prove useful
for compact star-forming systems at high redshift, where
JWST now resolves dense young stellar complexes and
bound clusters at z ≳ 8 (Adamo et al. 2024). Extending
the code to that regime requires adapting the input physics
to primordial ISM conditions, including reduced metallic-
ity, modified cooling, and potentially top-heavy IMFs, all
of which we defer to future work.
The cloud density profile ρ(r) is set at t = 0 , and ma-
terial beyond the expanding bubble does not evolve. It nei-
ther collapses, fragments, forms new stars, nor accretes onto
the central cluster during the run. This is a reasonable ap-
proximation when the cloud is being dispersed: the expand-
ing bubble crosses each radial annulus faster than the lo-
cal dynamical time, so the molecular gas has no chance
to restructure before being swept up. Observed GMCs are
broadly consistent with turbulent or quasi-equilibrium sup-
port on cloud scales ( Heyer et al. 2009; Mac Low & Klessen
2004), whereas dense sub-structures can be formally sub-
virial ( Kauffmann et al. 2013). For these cases, coupling
the shell dynamics to a live treatment of cloud self-gravity
and turbulent evolution is left to future work.
5.2. Gravity beyond the natal cloud
Our gravity term in Eq. 19 includes only the young cluster
and the swept-up shell. We neglect the pre-existing stel-
lar component of the host galaxy: disc stars older than the
natal cloud whose enclosed mass adds to the inward force
as the shell expands. On natal-cloud scales this omission is
small, because the shell encloses little of the surrounding
disc. It becomes less secure once Rb reaches tens of par-
secs. For a local old-stellar surface density Σ⋆, the enclosed
background stellar mass scales as Mold(Rb) ∼ πR2
bΣ⋆, so it
can become comparable to or larger than M⋆ at Rb ∼ 50–

5.3. Bubble interior: missing physics
We compute bubble energy losses with the conductive-
evaporation prescription following Weaver et al. (1977).
The resulting cooling rate assumes classical electron heat
conduction across a smooth, spherical contact disconti-
nuity. Three-dimensional simulations and analytic models
show that Kelvin–Helmholtz and Rayleigh–Taylor insta-
bilities can shred the contact discontinuity into a corru-
gated, fractal-like surface; the enlarged interface enhances
turbulent mixing and radiative cooling relative to a smooth
spherical area (e.g. Lancaster et al. 2021, 2024). A 1D code
cannot resolve this geometry directly; it can only absorb the
effect into an effective cooling prescription. In the absence
of such a subgrid enhancement, Trinity likely keeps the
hot bubble over-pressured for too long. We therefore inter-
pret the time at which our models reach the momentum-
driven phase as an upper limit when turbulent mixing dom-
inates the real interface.
In Trinity, three pieces of feedback physics are cur-
rently omitted from the dynamics. The first is magnetic
support. At the cloud densities in our grid ( n ∼ 102–
103 cm−3), the empirical magnetic-field scaling compiled by
Crutcher (2012) gives order-of-magnitude magnetic pres-
sures around Pmag/kB ∼ 105 K cm−3, with substantial
cloud-to-cloud scatter. This pressure lies below the ther-
mal pressure of photoionised gas for the fiducial models
considered here, and below the early wind ram pressure in
our model outputs. The approximation weakens at the up-
per end of the density grid, where Pmag ∝ n4/3 (Crutcher
2012).
Second, we include cosmic-ray ionisation in the cooling
tables, but neglect cosmic-ray pressure in the shell momen-
tum equation. This is a reasonable approximation here, as
the smooth Galactic cosmic-ray background does not exert
a considerable net force on a spherical shell; only a cosmic-
ray pressure gradient across the bubble would contribute
to the equation of motion. Such gradients can matter on
galactic scales and in supernova-driven environments, but
they are not part of the pre-supernova, single-cloud mod-
els considered here ( Grenier et al. 2015; Vink 2012). Pre-
supernova acceleration at stellar-wind termination shocks
remains possible, but its impact depends on eﬀicient con-
finement and on the cluster/wind geometry; it is there-
fore unlikely to dominate the shell dynamics in the ordi-
nary GMC regimes modelled here (e.g. Morlino et al. 2021;
Bednarek 2024).
We also neglect turbulent pressure inside the ionised
gas. In the Orion Nebula, the non-thermal velocity dis-
persion is subsonic: Arthur et al. (2016) find σturb ≈ 4–
5 km s−1, below the ionised sound speed ci ≈ 11 km s−1.
Radiation-hydrodynamic simulations of ii regions in tur-
bulent molecular clouds reach a comparable subsonic state
within a few sound-crossing times ( Medina et al. 2014). This
approximation should be safest for compact and interme-
diate ii regions. It becomes weaker for giant regions such
as 30 Doradus, where observed line widths are supersonic
and cluster winds, shells, and large-scale flows shape the
gas kinematics ( Melnick et al. 2021; García-Vázquez et al.
2023).
5.4. Dust and shell leakage
We scale the dust cross-section linearly with metallicity
and ignore both grain sublimation and the evolution of
the grain-size distribution. Sublimation does destroy grains
within rsub of the cluster, but only a negligible fraction
of the total dust mass is lost in the regime modelled here
(Draine 2011b). Our prescription is nevertheless a good ap-
proximation during the dense embedded phase, when the
shell column density is high and the dust is well coupled
to the gas. It will overestimate the radiation-pressure boost
factor (1 + τIR) at late times in luminous, low-density en-
vironments where cumulative grain depletion reduces τIR
below its initial value.
We also assume a closed shell throughout (i.e., covering
fraction Cf = 1 ). The code does not evaluate fragmenta-
tion criteria or transition to a leaky-shell regime. Once the
shell opens, hot gas escapes, ionising photons leak out, and
the shell captures less of the available momentum. Harper-
Clark & Murray (2009) showed for Carina that the dy-
namics depend on the sky-averaged covering fraction rather
than on the detailed shape of individual holes, which makes
a parametrised Cf(t) a sensible next step. Implementing an
evolving covering fraction and propagating it into f LyC
esc will
be deferred to future work.
5.5. Treatment of photoionised gas pressure
Resolved observations of ii regions show that the dominant
pressure term depends on size and evolutionary state. The
crux of the problem is therefore how photoionised gas pres-
sure should enter the equation of motion, not whether it
matters. Our construction in Section 2.3 reduces, but does
not remove, the coupling between PH ii and Pb. The inner-
edge density depends on Pb, the hydrostatic shell structure
depends on that boundary value, and Rif enters Eq. ( 24)
through the resulting geometry. The recombination-balance
estimate is therefore less directly tied to Pb than the local
shell density at Rif, but it is not an independent two-radius
wind-bubble and ii-region model. Evolving Rb and Rif as
separate dynamical variables would provide that treatment;
we leave this extension to the next paper in this series.
Existing semi-analytic treatments make different
architectural choices. One approach starts from a
photoionisation-driven ii-region solution and adds winds,
radiation pressure, gravity, or photon breakout as correc-
tion terms to the same ionisation-front dynamics ( Geen
et al. 2020). A different approach, followed by Lancaster
et al. (2025), carries separate radii for the wind-bubble
boundary Rb and the ionisation front Rif, then evolves the
wind bubble and photoionised region as coupled parts of
one feedback bubble through force balance and ionisation–
recombination equilibrium. The third approach ( Rahner
et al. 2017, 2019; Jiménez et al. 2026) sits closer to the
single-radius class: PH ii enters the hydrostatic shell struc-
ture through the inner boundary condition at the shell face,

PH ii explicitly in the shell equation of motion, but keeps a

6. Summary and conclusions
We have presented Trinity, a one-dimensional spheri-
cal thin-shell model for the expansion of feedback-driven
bubbles in molecular clouds. The code succeeds warp-
field (Rahner et al. 2017, 2019) and follows winds, su-
pernovae, direct and dust-reprocessed radiation pressure,
photoionised-gas pressure, and gravity in a single time-
dependent calculation. The central design choice is the
phase-aware driving pressure (Sect. 2.3): in the energy-
driven phase the shell is driven by the larger of the
hot-bubble and photoionised-gas pressures, while in the
momentum-driven phase the pressure support comes from
photoionised gas plus the direct ram pressure of freshly in-
jected wind and supernova ejecta.
In the low-eﬀiciency test cloud ( Mcloud = 10 6 M⊙,
ncore = 10 3 cm−3, ε = 0 .01), Trinity reproduces the adi-
abatic Weaver wind solution while the bubble is energy-
driven (Sect. 3). At late times, the full Trinity trajectory
lies between the pure photoionised and pure energy-driven
limits, showing that both ionised-gas pressure and ram pres-
sure play an important role in the bubble evolution. Includ-
ing PH ii increases the radius by ∼ 17% at 10 Myr relative
to the warpfield-equivalent run in this test, and the sepa-
ration continues to grow as the cooled bubble loses thermal
support.
Trinity also returns the shell dynamics, the force bud-
get, and ionising-photon budget on a single time grid. At
higher eﬀiciency ( ε = 0 .10, M⋆ = 10 5 M⊙), the energy-
driven phase lasts less than 1 Myr before cooling pushes the
solution into momentum-dominated evolution (Sect. 4.1).
Radiation pressure remains sub-dominant in this run, while
photoionised-gas pressure continues to provide a measur-
able part of the driving after the transition. The LyC es-
cape fraction is not a direct cloud-dispersal diagnostic, but
it records a second clock: the time at which ionising photons
begin to leak into the surrounding ISM. Trinity therefore
separates dynamical clearing from photon leakage, which
is essential for comparing cloud-scale feedback models with
H ii-region surveys and galaxy-scale LyC escape prescrip-
tions.
The density-profile experiments show that cloud struc-
ture can decide the outcome at fixed cloud mass, central
density, cluster mass, and metallicity. Of the four pro-
files tested at fixed Mcloud and ncore (uniform, αρ = −1,
αρ = −2, and a truncated Bonnor–Ebert sphere), only
the αρ = −2 cloud disperses within the 4 Myr runtime;
the other three turn around and re-collapse on 1.5–2.7 Myr
timescales (Sect. 4.2). The minimum eﬀiciency for cloud
destruction at fixed Mcloud and ncore therefore depends on
cloud structure, and pre-supernova feedback decides the
outcome before the first core-collapse supernovae arrive.
The emergence-time experiment reinforces the same
point in a regime where every cloud disperses. At fixed
(Mcloud, ε) and central density, Bonnor–Ebert clouds dis-
perse about 55% later than homogeneous clouds because
they place the same mass over a larger physical radius
(Sect 4.3). The density profile thus shifts the inferred emer-
gence clock by order unity even when feedback is strong
enough to clear every model. Across the grid, τdisp lies in the
range 0.3–3 Myr, below the delay to the first core-collapse
supernovae. Population predictions, including comparisons
with the bubble size distribution measured in the cold ISM
(Watkins et al. 2023), should therefore marginalise over a
realistic distribution of cloud profiles rather than adopt a
single canonical geometry.
The present implementation has clear limits. Trin-
ity averages over angular structure and cannot resolve
champagne flows, blister breakouts, or the corrugated
wind–bubble interface produced by Kelvin–Helmholtz and
Rayleigh–Taylor instabilities. The treatment of PH ii also
remains intermediate between single-radius shell models
and a fully coupled two-radius wind-bubble plus H ii-region
model. These limitations define future development steps:
an evolving covering fraction, an environmental confine-
ment through non-zero PISM, and a two-radius treatment
of Rb and Rif.
The main conclusion of this first paper is that feed-
back dominance cannot be inferred from the stellar pop-
ulation alone. It depends on how the feedback channels
couple to the shell, when the hot bubble cools, how much
ionising radiation leaks out, and how mass is arranged
inside the natal cloud. Trinity is designed to measure
these quantities directly, and can be used to eﬀiciently
map the wind-, photoionisation-, radiation-, and supernova-
dominated regimes across cloud parameter space and to
compare the predicted dispersal and leakage timescales
with resolved observations.

Appendix A: Cluster feedback input tables
Trinity reads the cluster-integrated feedback budget from
a single time-resolved table, computed in advance for a sin-
gle stellar population (SSP) cluster of mass M⋆ and metal-
licity Z. For each tabulated age t we require the ionising
photon rate Qi(t), the bolometric luminosity Lbol(t), split
at 13.6 eV into ionising and non-ionising components Li(t)
and Ln(t), the wind and supernova mechanical luminosi-
ties Lw(t) and LSN(t), and the corresponding momentum
injection rates ˙pw(t) and ˙pSN(t). For this paper, we gener-
ate the default tables with starburst99 (SB99; Leitherer
et al. 1999, 2014; Hawcroft et al. 2025), run with a Kroupa
(2001) IMF over 0.1–100 M⊙ and Geneva tracks at the re-
quested metallicity and rotation regime.
Nevertheless, Trinity is not tied to specific stellar pop-
ulation synthesis code. So long as atmospheric models are
taken into account (e.g., for Qi, ˙M v∞; see Hillier & Miller
1998; Pauldrach et al. 2001; Smith et al. 2002), any combi-
nation of synthesis code and atmospheric models that de-
livers the eight quantities above on a common time grid
can be substituted, including BPASS ( Eldridge et al. 2017;
Stanway & Eldridge 2018), which adds binary mass trans-
fer, and SLUG ( da Silva et al. 2012; Krumholz et al. 2015),
which samples the initial mass function (IMF) stochasti-
cally.
Appendix B: Validity of the ZAMS assumption
Trinity starts every simulation with a fully formed stellar
population at t = 0, placed on the zero-age main sequence
(ZAMS): the cluster mass M⋆ = ε Mcloud is distributed
across a Kroupa IMF, and starburst99 (Leitherer et al.
1999) supplies the feedback tables from that moment on-
ward. We do not model pre-main-sequence (PMS) evolu-
tion. The error this introduces depends on two things: how
long PMS contraction takes for a star of given mass, and
which mass range actually generates the feedback. We ad-
dress them in turn.
To answer the first, we use the MESA Isochrones and
Stellar Tracks library (MIST v1.2; Choi et al. 2016; Dot-
ter 2016), built on the Mesa stellar evolution code ( Pax-
ton et al. 2011). Models at solar metallicity and zero rota-
tion are adopted to match the starburst99 Geneva non-
rotating tracks used elsewhere in this paper. The choice is
also conservative for the present purpose: changing the ro-
tation prescription affects the detailed massive-star tracks,
but the feedback budget still comes from stars that reach
the main sequence on timescales short compared to cloud
clearing.
Figure B.1 (top panel) shows six MIST evolutionary
tracks covering Minit ∈ [0.5, 60] M⊙, with the PMS segment
drawn dashed and ZAMS marked by a filled circle. A 60 M⊙
star reaches the ZAMS in roughly 17 kyr, a 30 M⊙ star in
32 kyr, and a 10 M⊙ star in roughly 190 kyr. A 0.5 M⊙ star,
by contrast, takes about 160 Myr to contract, longer than
the host GMC itself survives. The ZAMS assumption there-
fore misrepresents low-mass stars; whether that misrepre-
sentation matters depends on how much feedback they pro-
duce.

Appendix C: Constraints on the cloud parameter
space
Not every combination of cloud parameters ( Mcloud, ncore,
Rcore, αρ) yields a physically realisable initial condition.
Trinity enforces two constraints before accepting a model.
First, the cloud radius Rcl, fixed by requiring the inte-
grated mass profile to equal the prescribed cloud mass
Mcloud, must remain within the observed range of Galac-

Rcl ≤ 200 pc as a conservative upper cutoff ( Solomon et al.
1987; Miville-Deschênes et al. 2017). Second, the density at
the cloud boundary must remain above the ambient ISM
value, n(Rcl) ≥ nISM. For power-law profiles with slope
αρ < 0, this condition gives

Appendix D: The cloud–ISM density discontinuity
In many idealised cloud models, the cloud–ISM transition
is treated as a step function at r = Rcloud (e.g. Rahner et al.
2017). In our model grid this jump often spans several or-
ders of magnitude in density. When solving the dynamical
equations for quantities such as vb and Eb, LSODA adapts
its step size to maintain the requested local error tolerance.
At the discontinuity, the solver may attempt to reduce the
step size excessively and terminate with an Unexpected
istate error.
Whether this happens depends on the shell speed at the
crossing. In our tests, the failure appears when the shell
reaches the cloud edge at roughly ≳ 10 km s−1: a slow shell
traverses the density jump in many small accepted steps,
whereas a fast shell encounters the jump too abruptly for
the adaptive integrator to regularise. When this happens,
LSODA exits at the last successful step and Trinity then
hands the solution to the transition and momentum phases.
The run therefore still reaches completion and returns a full
Rb(t) history, but the exact timing and location of the phase
transition become unreliable.
We avoid this numerical error by replacing the dis-
continuity with a hyperbolic-tangent bridge across Rcloud
(Fig. D.1),

Appendix E.2: The trinity_reader API
Analysis scripts access Trinity output through the
trinity_reader module, which wraps the raw JSONL
files in a NumPy-oriented interface analogous to
astropy.io.fits. Opening a dictionary.jsonl file
returns a TrinityOutput object that behaves like an
ordered sequence of snapshots, supports iteration and
integer indexing, and automatically converts JSON lists
back to NumPy arrays on load. Table E.3 summarises the
main methods.
