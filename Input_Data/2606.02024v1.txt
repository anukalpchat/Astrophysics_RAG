ABSTRACT
The production sites of high-energy neutrinos in blazar jets remain poorly constrained. In this
work, we investigate the physical conditions required for eﬀicient neutrino production by combining
radio-constrained jet properties with multi-zone emission modeling. We show that eﬀicient neutrino
production requires an external radiation field stronger than the magnetic field in the jet frame.
This environment not only enhances the eﬀiciency of photohadronic interactions but also suppresses
synchrotron radiation from secondary pairs, thereby avoiding overshooting the hard X-ray data. Such
conditions can be achieved in regions near or within the broad-line region. However, assuming a
single emission zone, these conditions are generally inconsistent with the double-bump flux ratio of
the observed broadband emission. This implies that the neutrino-emitting region should be physically
separated from the dominant electromagnetic emission zone. We further show that such a scenario can
be realized either if the jet completes its acceleration within sub-parsec scales or if the bulk Lorentz
factor is intrinsically large, both of which appear uncommon based on current observations. These
results provide a natural explanation for the rarity of blazar-neutrino associations and highlight the
importance of constraining jet structure at small scales to identify promising neutrino-emitting blazars.

1. INTRODUCTION
The detection of the first astrophysical PeV-scale neu-
trinos by IceCube Neutrino Observatory in 2013 marked
the advent of high-energy neutrino astronomy and trig-
gered extensive efforts to identify their origins ( IceCube
Collaboration 2013; M. G. Aartsen et al. 2013). The
nearly isotropic distribution of these neutrinos across
the sky suggests an extragalactic origin ( M. G. Aart-
sen et al. 2014, 2017a; K. Fang et al. 2024). Among
the various candidates, the blazar jets has emerged as

a promising candidate. This perspective was strength-
ened on 22 September 2017, when IceCube reported
the first association between a high-energy neutrino IC-
170922A and an extragalactic source, namely the blazar
TXS 0506+056, with a significance ∼ 3σ ( IceCube Col-
laboration et al. 2018a). Since then, a number of blazars
have been identified as potential neutrino point sources,
though only a few associations reaching significances of
≳ 3σ (e.g., IceCube Collaboration et al. 2018b; M. G.
Aartsen et al. 2020). Furthermore, the currently iden-
tified neutrino candidate blazars represent only a minor
fraction of the overall blazar population. It also has
been suggested that the spectral energy distributions
(SEDs) and broad-band properties of neutrino candidate

blazars do not exhibit significant differences compared
to those of other blazars ( P. Padovani et al. 2022; S.
Paiano et al. 2023; M. Karl et al. 2023). This raises a
fundamental question of what physical properties dis-
tinguish neutrino-emitting blazar jets from the majority
of apparently neutrino-quiet blazars.
Population studies have sought to identify which elec-
tromagnetic bands may serve as diagnostics or pre-
dictors of bright high-energy neutrino emission. The
production of ultra-high-energy secondary particles in
hadronic processes naturally links γ-ray emission to neu-
trino radiation. However, no clear correlation has been
established for the γ-ray bright blazars so far ( M. G.
Aartsen et al. 2017b; M. Huber 2019; R. Abbasi et al.
2022). In contrast, possible correlations have been sug-
gested in the radio ( A. Plavin et al. 2020; A. V. Plavin
et al. 2021; T. Hovatta et al. 2021; A. V. Plavin et al.
2023, cf., R. Abbasi et al. 2025) and hard X-ray ( A. V.
Plavin et al. 2024) bands. Recent work further suggests
that blazars exhibiting extreme jet beaming, as inferred
from radio observations, are statistically more likely to
be associated with high-energy neutrinos ( A. V. Plavin
et al. 2025). Population studies suggest that blazars as a
class cannot fully account for the diffuse neutrino back-
ground ( M. G. Aartsen et al. 2017b; M. Huber 2019;
R. Abbasi et al. 2022, 2024). This implies that only a
minority of blazars are eﬀicient neutrino emitters, sug-
gesting substantial diversity in neutrino production abil-
ity among different blazar jets. Understanding whether
such diversity originates from differences in intrinsic jet
physical properties requires confronting theoretical mod-
els with observational constraints.
On the theoretical side, the one-zone jet model, widely
adopted in blazar studies, has been extensively used
to reproduce multiwavelength (MWL) electromagnetic
emission and to account for the observed neutrino flux
(e.g., K. Murase et al. 2014; C. D. Dermer et al. 2014;
A. Keivani et al. 2018; M. Cerruti et al. 2019; S. Gao
et al. 2019). However, constraints from hard X-ray
data strongly suppress the synchrotron radiation of sec-
ondary pairs, whose luminosity would be comparable to
the neutrino emission. Consequently, even under opti-
mistic results, one-zone models face challenges to repro-
duce the observed neutrino flux ( W.-J. Li et al. 2026).
In other words, the one-zone model struggles to con-
vincingly explain the associations, unless they are all
attributed to statistical effects such as the Eddington
bias ( N. L. Strotjohann et al. 2019). Therefore, hard
X-ray constraints imply that reproducing the observed
neutrino flux requires the neutrino-emitting region to
be spatially separated from the zone responsible for the
MWL SED ( R. Xue et al. 2019).
Building on this insight, multi-zone approaches, such
as the inner–outer blob jet model ( R.-Y. Liu et al. 2019;
R. Xue et al. 2019; Z.-R. Wang & R. Xue 2021; R.
Xue et al. 2021), have been proposed to enhance the
model-predicted neutrino flux while remaining consis-
tent with hard X-ray constraints. In this framework,
high-energy neutrinos are predominantly generated in
a compact inner region where the Compton dominance
(Y ) is assumed to be much greater than unity. Un-
der such conditions, secondary pairs (produced by π±
decay, Bethe–Heitler pair production, and γγ annihila-
tion) mainly cool via external Compton (EC) scattering
rather than synchrotron radiation, thereby alleviating
the hard X-ray constraints ( R. Xue et al. 2019). The
drawback of multi-zone models is the introduction of ad-
ditional free parameters, and the required Y ≫ 1 condi-
tion is often realized only through fine-tuning. Whether
the condition Y ≫ 1 can naturally arises in blazar jets
remains an open and critical question for establishing a
robust physical explanation of the neutrino–blazar asso-
ciations.
In this work, we focus on employing radio-constrained
jet parameters to locate regions capable of achieving
Y ≫ 1, thereby identifying potential sites of high-energy
neutrino production. Although radio observations pri-
marily trace parsec-scale regions, they provide robust
constraints on global jet beaming and magnetic field
strength, which can be extrapolated to constrain the
properties of the inner dissipation regions. Since the
Y parameter depends on the ratio of external photons
to magnetic energy densities, independent radio con-
straints on the jet beaming factor and magnetic field
strength directly test whether the extreme condition
Y ≫ 1 can be physically realized. If such a condition can
be realized under realistic jet parameters, it would nat-
urally identify the jet regions capable of eﬀicient high-
energy neutrino production, thereby providing a physi-
cally motivated framework for locating the neutrino pro-
duction sites. This paper is organized as follows. In
Sect. 2, we establish a theoretical framework to evaluate
the external photon fields and jet physical parameters
using multi-wavelength and radio observations. This
framework is subsequently applied to TXS 0506+056
and PKS 1502+102 in Sect. 3 to identify potential neu-
trino production sites and perform self-consistent SED
modeling. We then extend the analysis to a broader
blazar population based on the MOJA VE sample in
Sect. 4, where we construct a proxy for Y and identify
promising neutrino-emitting candidates. Our conclu-
sions are presented in Sect. 5. Throughout the paper,
the cosmological parameters H0 = 69 .6 km s −1Mpc−1,

2. METHOD
There is growing evidence that low- and intermediate-
synchrotron-peaked (LSP/ISP) blazars ( A. A. Abdo
et al. 2010), as well as masquerading BL Lac objects
(mas BL Lacs; P. Giommi et al. 2013), are more likely to
be connected with high-energy neutrinos ( C. Righi et al.
2019; A. Plavin et al. 2020; T. Hovatta et al. 2021; A. V.
Plavin et al. 2021; P. Padovani et al. 2022; S. Paiano
et al. 2023; M. Karl et al. 2023; A. V. Plavin et al. 2023;
A. Azzollini et al. 2025; I. Viale et al. 2026). The key
factor is that jets in LSP/ISP blazars and masquerad-
ing BL Lacs are generally believed to be embedded in
abundant external photon fields, whose crucial role in
neutrino production has been highlighted in many the-
oretical studies ( R. Xue et al. 2019, 2021; Z.-J. Wang
et al. 2024, 2025; X. Rodrigues et al. 2024, 2026; W.-J.
Li et al. 2026), as their energy densities are boosted in
the jet comoving frame, thereby enhancing the eﬀiciency
of the pγ interaction. In this section, primed quantities
are measured in the jet comoving frame, unless specified.

2.3. Klein-Nishina Suppression and Numerical
Evaluation of Y
With Γ(rdiss) and B′(rdiss) estimated as described
above, one can identify regions along the jet where the
condition Y ≫ 1 is satisfied. Observational constraints
from the hard X-ray spectrum can be used for cali-
bration, thereby constraining the minimum value of Y
(hereafter Ymin) required to suppress synchrotron radia-
tion from secondary pairs without overshooting the hard
X-ray data. Since secondary pairs and external photons
are both highly energetic, EC scattering occurs in the
deep Klein–Nishina (KN) regime, where the EC to syn-
chrotron flux ratio departs from the simple energy den-
sity scaling and becomes non-linear. To account for the
KN suppression in the EC cooling of secondary pairs, we
adopt the numerical correction proposed by R. Schlick-
eiser & J. Ruppel (2010). Moreover, because the hard
X-ray coverage and the secondary pairs spectral shape
vary among sources, it is not straightforward to deter-
mine analytically which part of the hard X-ray spectrum
most strongly constrains the synchrotron emission from
secondary pairs. Therefore, a numerical evaluation with
observational calibration is required to determine Ymin.
The differential spectra of γ-ray photons, pairs, and
neutrinos produced in the photopion ( pγ) production
and BH processes are obtained with analytical expres-
sions developed in S. R. Kelner & F. A. Aharonian
(2008). The differential spectrum of pairs generated
from the internal γγ annihilation is evaluated as well
(F. A. Aharonian et al. 1983). The spectra of these
secondary pairs, produced through the above processes,
are governed by the spectra of the parent relativis-
tic protons and the external target photons. As de-
scribed in Sect. 2.1, the spectral shape of external pho-
ton fields is fixed a priori; therefore, the relativistic pro-
ton spectrum must be assumed. In the numerical cal-
culations, following standard expectations for diffusive
shock acceleration ( L. O. Drury 1983), we adopt a pro-
ton spectral index of −2 and fix the proton Lorentz fac-
tor in the range 1 ≤ γp ≤ 107. The maximum proton
Lorentz factor mainly affects the spectra peak of the sec-
ondary pairs emission, whereas the synchrotron emission
in the X-ray band is produced predominantly by com-
paratively lower-energy pairs. Therefore, in the context
of interpreting the ∼ 1 PeV neutrino event, the choice
γp,max = 10 7 is reasonable and conservative.
In the numerical evaluation of Ymin, the value of δ at
different jet position is derived from δC using Eq. ( 5).
Then the proton injection luminosity is adjusted to
match the observed neutrino flux, after which B′ is var-
ied to scan Y and obtain the corresponding secondary-
pair emission spectra. This approach isolates the impact
of B′ variations on U ′
B and thus Y , while keeping the
deep KN effect essentially fixed. In contrast, changing
δ would complicate the interpretation of Ymin due to its
effects on both synchrotron spectral features and KN
effects. For each Y , we calculate the secondary pairs
emission spectra and determine Ymin that remains con-
sistent with the hard X-ray constraints.
3. APPLICATION TO INDIVIDUAL SOURCES
In this section, we apply the framework developed
above to two representative blazars, TXS 0506+056 and
PKS 1502+102. They provide ideal case studies for our
method for two reasons. First, both of them have VLBI
radio measurements that independently constrain B′
and δ. Second, they have quasi-simultaneous multiwave-
length SEDs, which reduce the ambiguity introduced by
state-dependent variability.

However, observations suggest that the above require-
ments are expected to be met only in exceptional cases.
Radio studies of nearby jetted AGN find the transi-
tion from parabolic to conical geometry typically oc-
curs at ∼ 1–10 pc, i.e., generally beyond RBLR (Y. Y.
Kovalev et al. 2020), suggesting that AGN jets with
rtran < R BLR are uncommon. It is worth noting that
this observed transition scale of 1–10 pc is broadly con-
sistent with the typical Bondi radius ( K. Asada & M.
Nakamura 2012; Y. Y. Kovalev et al. 2020). In this con-
text, while the rtran required in the “large- ΓC” scenario
can be comparable to the Bondi radius, the requirement
of rtran ∼ 0.01 pc in the “small- rtran” scenario demands
a geometry transition at a location significantly smaller
than this characteristic physical scale. Notably, none of
the ten nearby jetted AGNs in the sample of Y. Y. Ko-
valev et al. (2020), which exhibit these standard tran-
sition scales, are included in the IceCat-1 catalog ( R.
Abbasi et al. 2023). This absence further supports the
idea that a large rtran is generally unfavorable for neu-
trino production, unless compensated by an exception-
ally large ΓC. However, the “large- ΓC” scenario itself
represents an extreme case in the blazar population, as
blazars with ΓC comparable to PKS 1502+102 consti-
tute only a small fraction of the known population (e.g.,
I. Liodakis et al. 2018). Consequently, whether a jet
undergoes an uncommon early geometry transition or
possesses extreme relativistic beaming, the conditions
required for eﬀicient neutrino emission remain diﬀicult
to fulfill. This naturally explains why only a limited sub-
set of blazars has been identified as high-energy neutrino
sources at present.
3.3. SED Modeling and Implications for the Neutrino
Events
Building on the results above, the potential neutrino
production regions for both TXS 0506+056 and PKS
1502+102 are likely situated near or within the BLR,
where Y typically reaches values of ∼ 102–103. How-
ever, their SEDs show that the ratio of the high-energy
peak to the low-energy peak flux generally does not
exceed ∼ 10. This discrepancy implies that a single-
zone scenario cannot simultaneously account for both
the neutrino emission and the broadband SED, and in-
stead requires the introduction of an additional leptonic
dissipation region.
For the association between TXS 0506+056 and IC-
170922A, pronounced multi-wavelength variability from
optical to TeV bands was observed around the neutrino
arrival time ( IceCube Collaboration et al. 2018a), sug-
gesting that the neutrino production is closely linked
to the electromagnetic activity. This temporal coinci-
dence indicates that the neutrino-emitting region and
the broadband radiation zone are likely physically con-
nected. Here we consider a spine–layer jet model ( G.
Ghisellini et al. 2005), in which a fast spine is surrounded
by a slow layer. Limb-brightened structures observed
on ∼ 100 pc scales ( E. Ros et al. 2020) provide evidence
for a structured jet with transverse velocity stratifica-
tion in TXS 0506+056, suggesting that such a structure
may also extend to sub-pc scales relevant to neutrino
emission. In this scenario, the spine region, charac-
terized by a larger bulk Lorentz factor, experiences a
strongly boosted external radiation field from the BLR,
leading to eﬀicient neutrino production. In contrast, the
surrounding layer, moving with a lower Lorentz factor,
sees a significantly reduced external photon energy den-
sity. In the modeling, the evaluation of synchrotron,
synchrotron self-Compton (SSC), and EC emissions (in-
cluding photons from the AD, HC, BLR, DT, and the
other component) from primary electrons and secondary
pairs in both the spine and layer follows G. Ghisellini
et al. (2005). For the hadronic processes in the spine,
we calculate the differential spectra of γ-ray photons
from neutral pion decays, pairs, and neutrinos follow-
ing S. R. Kelner & F. A. Aharonian (2008). Besides,
the pairs coming from internal γγ pair production are
also calculated following F. A. Aharonian et al. (1983).
The absorption of γ-ray escaping from the jet will be
absorbed by the extragalactic background light (EBL).
We account for this attenuation in the GeV–TeV band
using the EBL model of A. Domínguez et al. (2011).

photons implies that the dissipation region cannot be
located within the BLR due to severe γγ absorption,
we place the neutrino-emitting region just outside the

Overall, the choice of geometry for each source is
driven by both observational and structural considera-
tions. Observationally, the coincident multi-wavelength
flare during IC-170922A favors a co-spatial structure
(spine-layer) for TXS 0506+056, while the low state
of PKS 1502+106 during IC-190730A favors a spatially
separated structure (inner-outer). These choices are also
consistent with the underlying jet acceleration profile:
with rtran ≪ RBLR the jet reaches its terminal Γ be-
fore crossing the BLR, making transverse stratification
natural; with rtran ≳ RBLR the jet is still accelerating,
so longitudinal segmentation arises naturally from the
radial Γ gradient. In a general structured-jet picture
both can coexist, and the two sources represent limiting
cases.

This unified framework can be directly applied to large
blazar samples and offers a systematic way to connect
population studies with detailed modeling of individual
sources.
5. CONCLUSION
In this work, we investigate the physical condi-
tions required for neutrino production in blazar jets by
combining radio-constrained jet properties with multi-
zone emission modeling. Our results suggest that the
neutrino-emitting region is tightly constrained to lie
near the BLR, where the external radiation field can
be suﬀiciently enhanced in the jet comoving frame.

Y > Y min can be satisfied either by a small transition
distance rtran, allowing the jet to reach a suﬀiciently
large bulk Lorentz factor on sub-pc scales, or by an
intrinsically large ΓC. However, both conditions ap-
pear to be uncommon: radio observations suggest that
rtran < R BLR is rare, while blazars with extremely large
ΓC constitute only a small fraction of the population.
This naturally explains the rarity of confirmed blazar–
neutrino associations.
While our primary focus has been on the highly
beamed blazar population, the methodology developed
here is versatile and can be extended to misaligned ra-
dio galaxies, provided that high-resolution radio obser-
vations are available to constrain the jet kinematics and
viewing angles. Future observations, especially high-
resolution radio measurements that can constrain jet
structure and dynamics, together with improved neu-
trino statistics, will be essential to further test this sce-
nario and refine the localization of neutrino production
sites in relativistic jets. In particular, tighter constraints
on jet geometry at sub-pc scales may provide a practi-
cal avenue for identifying promising neutrino-emitting
blazar candidates.

A. SECONDARY PAIRS SPECTRA WITH DIFFERENT V ALUES OF Y
In Fig. 6, we present representative spectra of the emission from secondary pairs for PKS 1502+102, corresponding
to a dissipation location at rdiss = 5 RS (AD-dominated case) and a BLR-dominated case. These examples explicitly
illustrate that the spectral shapes of the secondary emission differ significantly between the two scenarios. Specifically,
the synchrotron radiation from secondary pairs, which are produced via the BH process (dotted curves) and internal
γγ absorption (dash-dotted curves), is primarily constrained by the hard X-ray data. In the AD-dominated case, the
spectrum of secondary pairs exhibits a slope that closely matches the observed hard X-ray data points, resulting in a
relatively low Fν/FX, as reflected by the lower black horizontal line in the bottom panel of Fig. 1. By contrast, in the
BLR-dominated case, the low-energy hard X-ray data points already impose a stringent constraint on the secondary
emission component. The predicted spectrum does not align with the observed slope, which in turn leads to a higher
Fν/FX, corresponding to the higher red horizontal line in the bottom panel of Fig. 1. These differences highlight
how the distinct spectral shapes of secondary pairs in the two scenarios translate directly into markedly different
observational constraints.

C. DATA ANALYSIS OF SWIFT-XRT
J1733-1304 (also known as NRAO 530) is a highly active X-ray blazar well studied in the literature (e.g., L. Foschini
et al. 2006). There are more than 100 visits from the Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory ( N. Gehrels et al. 2004).
Considering that the corresponding neutrino event was detected on 25th January 2016, we collected the closest data
observed by Swift-XRT from April 2016 (for details, see Table 5). The XRT photon counting mode data were analyzed
using the FTOOLS software version 6.35. First, the events were cleaned with the xrtpipeline task adopting the
standard quality cuts. We then extracted the source spectra from a circular region with a radius of 20 pixels, and the
background spectra from a larger circle (50 pixels) in a blank area. In the spectral analyses, the ancillary response
files from the CALDB database were compiled using xrtmkarf. Given the limited exposure times of the snapshots, the
absorption column density was set to the Galactic value (i.e. 1.67 × 1021 cm−2, HI4PI Collaboration et al. 2016), and
we performed a joint analysis of the three observations to determine the X-ray photon spectral index. We then carried
out an individual analysis for each observation by fixing the photon index derived from the joint analysis. The results
of the spectral analyses were also listed in Table 5.
