ABSTRACT
We report here the discovery and characterization of a high-mass transiting brown dwarf in a close-in or-
bit around its host star, TOI-7154. Initially, the host star was identiﬁed as an exoplanetary candidate from
the TESS photometry data. Later, with the mass measurements from the RV follow-up using the PARAS-2
and TRES spectrographs, the companion is found to be sub-stellar in nature. TOI-7154, is a G-type main-
sequence metal-rich star metallicity [Fe /H] = 0.154+0.077
−0.075 dex, e ﬀective temperature Teﬀ = 5564+100
−110 K, mass
M⋆ = 0.939+0.047
−0.043 M⊙, radius R⋆ = 0.949+0.032
−0.030 R⊙, and surface gravity log g = 4.456+0.036
−0.036. With the joint analy-
sis of the TESS photometry and the PARAS-2 and TRES radial velocities we found that TOI-7154b orbits its
host star in P = 8.860073 ± 0.000029 d, eccentric (e = 0.2482 ± 0.0024) orbit and its radius is smaller than that
of Jupiter ( Rb = 0.827+0.040
−0.037 RJ). With a mass near the hydrogen-burning boundary ( Mb = 71.7+2.4
−2.2 MJ) which
separates brown dwarfs from very low-mass stars, TOI-7154b occupies a critical position in the regime for
probing the transition between sub-stellar and stellar objects. The system is very old, with its age estimated to
be 7.2+3.9
−3.6 Gyr by MIST isochrones, while Galactic kinematics indicate an age of ∼ 4 − 5 Gyr. Our tidal evolution
simulations indicate a stellar dissipation factor of Q′
⋆ ≲ 106. Since the presence of any companion is currently
ruled out by observations, the presence of eccentricity in this old system is, therefore, indicative of it having
stellar-like fragmentation origins.

1. INTRODUCTION
Brown Dwarfs (BDs) are sub-stellar objects with masses
insuﬃcient to sustain stable hydrogen fusion in their interi-
ors. They are generally deﬁned to have masses in the range
∼ 13 − 80 MJ, where the lower limit corresponds to the onset

of deuterium burning (D. S. Spiegel et al. 2011) and the upper
limit approximates the threshold of sustained hydrogen burn-
ing, which lies between ∼ 75 − 80 MJ depending on metal-
licity and evolutionary models ( I. Bara ﬀe et al. 2002; S. B.
Dieterich et al. 2014). These boundaries blur the distinction
between massive giant planets & low-mass BDs and the high-
mass BDs & low-mass stars ( C. M. Persson et al. 2019; K.
Barkaoui et al. 2025). BDs, therefore, occupy a mass regime
in which both planetary and stellar formation mechanisms
may operate, making them crucial for understanding the tran-
sition between star and planet formation. Current theoretical
and observational evidences indicate that BDs do not form
via a single mechanism. Instead, their formation pathways
appear to depend on their masses. Studies suggest that the
high-mass BDs ( ≳ 40 − 50 MJ) are formed through turbulent
fragmentation of molecular clouds, analogous to the forma-
tion of low-mass stars ( M. R. Bate 2009; G. Chabrier et al.
arXiv:2605.23469v1  [astro-ph.EP]  22 May 2026

2014; B. Ma & J. Ge 2014). In contrast, low-mass BDs may
also arise from disk-based processes such as gravitational in-
stability (A. G. W. Cameron 1978; A. P . Boss 1997) or core
accretion mechanism (J. B. Pollack et al. 1996; B. Ma & J. Ge
2014; K. Kratter & G. Lodato 2016) as giant planets. These
formation pathways are expected to leave observable dynam-
ical imprints, including di ﬀerences in orbital eccentricities,
period distributions, periodeccentricity relations, and spinor-
bit obliquity ( B. Ma & J. Ge 2014; B. P . Bowler et al. 2020;
T. Gan et al. 2025a).
Beyond their physical properties, the orbital properties of
close-in transiting BDs can provide critical insights into their
formation and dynamical histories. Disk-driven migrations
typically produce low-eccentricity orbits due to strong eccen-
tricity damping in the protoplanetary disk ( P . Goldreich & S.
Tremaine 1980; H. Tanaka & W. R. Ward 2004). In con-
trast, BDs formed through star-like cloud fragmentation at
wide separations may subsequently undergo dynamical ex-
citation via KozaiLidov oscillations ( Y . Kozai 1962; M. L.
Lidov 1962; Y . Wu & N. Murray 2003), planet-planet or
planet-companion scattering ( S. Chatterjee et al. 2008; M.
Nagasawa et al. 2008), before migrating inward and expe-
riencing strong tidal interactions with the primary star. As
a result, moderate eccentricities ( e ∼ 0.1 − 0.4) at short pe-
riods ( P ≲ 10 d) can preserve signatures of high-eccentricity
migration or ongoing tidal circularisation ( T. Mazeh 2008; S.
Matsumura et al. 2010).
Tidal interactions further shape the orbital evolution of
close-in sub-stellar companions. Since BDs are dense ob-
jects, their tidal evolution can di ﬀer signiﬁcantly from that
of giant planets or low-mass stars ( G. I. Ogilvie 2014; A. J.
Barker 2020). There are a plethora of results that could be
attributed to the examination of tidal interactions, that would
have otherwise remained a mystery. One such example is the
discrepancy between the observed and predicted radii of tran-
siting exoplanets and brown dwarfs ( B. Jackson et al. 2008,
2009; B. A. Henderson et al. 2024) which can be attributed to
the e ﬀect of tidal heating, the observed narrow range of ec-
centricity in close-in exoplanets compared to the broad range
for wide-orbit systems caused by the tidal circularisation of
close-in systems, and the evolution of orbital separation due
to tides which eventually leads to the accretion of the planet
by the host star after crossing the Roche limit ( B. Jackson
et al. 2008, 2009). Thus, the observed eccentricities and tidal
decay timescales of close-in BDs can put constraints on their
formation mechanisms, past dynamical interactions, and or-
bital evolution.
One of the prominent features of the BD population is
the brown dwarf desert – a scarcity of BDs with short or-
bital periods ( Porb ≲ 10 − 100 d) around FGK stars ( G. W.
Marcy & R. P . Butler 2000; D. Grether & C. H. Lineweaver
2006; J. Sahlmann et al. 2011). Although more than 2000
BDs have been identiﬁed through wide-ﬁeld imaging sur-
veys ( W. R. Johnston 2015), only ∼ 50 transiting BDs are
currently known (e.g. C. M. Persson et al. 2019; I. Mire-
les et al. 2023; K. Barkaoui et al. 2025). Transiting BDs
are rare, but important, as they o ﬀer a unique opportunity to
directly measure mass and radius through combined photo-
metric and high-resolution spectroscopic observations, inde-
pendent of the sub-stellar evolutionary models. It is helpful
in constraining the BD mass-radius relation and their subse-
quent evolutionary models. Despite their rarity, the synergy
between space-based photometric surveys, such as CoRoT
(M. Auvergne et al. 2009), Kepler (W. J. Borucki et al. 2010),
TESS (G. R. Ricker et al. 2015), and ground-based radial ve-
locity (RV) spectrographs, such as HARPS ( F. Pepe et al.
2002b), PARAS ( A. Chakraborty et al. 2014a), TRES ( G.
F˝ urész2008), FIES ( J. H. Telting et al. 2014), and others,
have improved the transiting BD population in recent years.
TESS, in particular, provides continuous high-precision light
curves for bright nearby stars, enabling the detection of tran-
siting BD companions across a wide mass range.
In this work, we report the discovery and characterisa-
tion of TOI-7154b, a transiting close-in high-mass BD in
an eccentric orbit detected with the TESS photometry data
and conﬁrmed with high-resolution RV observations from the
PARAS-2 and the TRES spectrographs. The rest of the paper
is organized as follows: Section 2 describes the photometric,
spectroscopic, and speckle imaging observations; Section 3
elaborates the various analyses of the host star and the sys-
tem along with the joint modeling of the light curves and
RVs; and Section 4 presents the newly detected BD in the
context of BD evolutionary models and the tidal evolution of
the system. It also discusses the possible formation pathways
of the BD. In Section 5, we provide a brief summary of our
results.

2.2.2. Speckle interferometry with SPeckle Polarimeter
TOI-7154 was observed on March 13, 2025 with the
SPeckle Polarimeter (SPP) on the 2.5-m telescope at the
Caucasian Observatory of Sternberg Astronomical Institute
(SAI) of Lomonosov Moscow State University. A low-noise
CMOS detector, Hamamatsu ORCA-quest ( I. A. Strakhov
et al. 2023a), was used for the observations. The atmospheric
dispersion compensator was active, which allowed us to use
the wide Ic band, with an angular resolution of 0 .083′′. Long
exposure atmospheric seeing was 1.01′′ at the moment of ob-
servation. We calculated the ACF to estimate the detection
limits depending on distance to the star. Then we calculated
the standard deviation of the latter in annular regions having
radii ranging from 0 .05′′ to 1.7′′ and width of 0 .06′′. We as-
sumed that the potential companion would be detectable if its
signal in ACF was larger than 5 standard deviations. The de-
tection limit in ACF computed this way was converted into
the magnitude di ﬀerence, at distances 0 .25′′ and 1 .0′′ from
the star, which amounted to ∆ = 3.5m and 5.3m, respectively.
For further details of this procedure see I. A. Strakhov et al.
(2023b). The bottom panel in Figure 2 presents the contrast
curve and the corresponding ACF obtained using the SPP .
2.3. Reconnaissance Spectroscopy and RV F ollow-up
2.3.1. PARAS-2 Observations
The RV follow-up of TOI-7154 was carried out using
the PARAS-2 ( A. Chakraborty et al. 2018, 2024) spectro-
graph attached to the 2.5-m telescope at the PRL Mount
Abu Observatory, Gurushikhar, Rajasthan, India. PARAS-
2 is a ﬁber-fed high-resolution ( R ≈ 110000) cross-dispersed
echelle spectrograph kept under highly stable environmen-
tal conditions. The spectrograph works in the visible wave-
length range of 3800 − 6900. The spectrograph utilizes the
Uranium-Argon (UAr) hollow cathode lamp for wavelength
calibration and simultaneous referencing ( S. Baliwal et al.
2024; R. Sharma & A. G. Chakraborty 2021). More details
about the spectrograph can be found in A. Chakraborty et al.
(2018, 2024). We conducted a total of 20 spectroscopic ob-
servations of TOI-7154, spanning 24 days from March 17,
2025, to April 10, 2025, with an exposure time of 3600 sec-
onds for each observation. These spectra were extracted us-
ing the PARAS-2 pipeline, which is brieﬂy explained later
in the section. The RV errors are calculated using the tech-
niques mentioned in P . Chaturvedi et al. (2016, 2018). All
the details of RVs, timestamps, and their corresponding er-
rors are presented in Table 4. The PARAS-2 data reduction
pipeline, written in the IDL language, is an upgraded version
of the PARAS pipeline ( A. Chakraborty et al. 2014b), which
4

employs the REDUCE package (N. E. Piskunov & J. A. V alenti
2002) for extracting cross-dispersed echelle spectra. Using
calibration and science frames, the pipeline performs optimal
spectral extraction through bias and ﬂat corrections, order
tracing via REDUCEs clustering algorithm, and scattered-light
estimation. Cosmic rays are removed using the Laplacian
edge detection method ( P . G. van Dokkum 2001), and spec-
tra are extracted with a swath-based decomposition approach.
Wavelength calibration is achieved using a Uranium line-list
(R. Sharma & A. G. Chakraborty 2021), where a four-degree
polynomial is ﬁtted per order to derive the wavelength solu-
tion. The spectra are then cross-correlated with a G2-type
mask following A. Baranne et al. (1996) and F. Pepe et al.
(2002a). More details on the PARAS-2 pipeline can be found
in S. Baliwal et al. (2024).
2.3.2. TRES Observations
We also obtained 8 spectra of TOI-7154 between Septem-
ber 08, 2025, and October 30, 2025, using the Tillinghast
Reﬂector Echelle Spectrograph (TRES; G. F˝ urész(2008)) on
the 1.5 m Tillinghast Reﬂector telescope on Mount Hopkins,
Arizona, USA. TRES is a ﬁber-fed echelle spectrograph with
a resolving power of R ≈ 44,000 that operates in the wave-
length range of 390910 nm. The observations were obtained
in sequences of three stellar exposures surrounded by ThAr
calibration exposures through the same ﬁber, to assist with
the removal of cosmic rays. The total exposure times were
in the range 1600 s to 2520 s and yielded S /N ratios per res-
olution element between 26 and 37. The spectra were ex-
tracted using the procedures outlined in L. A. Buchhave et al.
(2010). Multi-order relative velocities were derived by cross-
correlating each observed spectrum order by order against a
master template constructed by combining all the TRES ob-
servations of TOI-7154. The estimated error for each ob-
servation was derived from the scatter of the RVs across the
echelle orders for that observation. The RVs acquired with
TRES spectra, along with their respective errors, are listed in
Table 4.
3. ANAL YSIS AND RESULTS
3.1. Stellar parameters from spectral synthesis
The two high Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) spectra ac-
quired on September 8, 2025, and September 30, 2025, with
TRES were used for estimating the physical parameters of
the stars, e.g., e ﬀective temperature Teﬀ, Metallicity [Fe /H],
surface gravity log g, and projected stellar rotational veloc-
ity v sin i⋆. The SNR per resolution element of these spectra
was 34.7 and 37 .9, respectively. These parameters were de-
rived by applying the Stellar Parameter Classiﬁcation (SPC)
software (L. A. Buchhave et al. 2012; A. Bieryla et al. 2021)
to individual TRES spectra, and the ﬁnal parameters were
obtained by taking the weighted average of the parameters
obtained from these two spectra. The SPC utilizes a li-
brary of synthetic spectra spanning the wavelength range
of 5020 − 5320, centered on the Mg-b triplet. With SPC,
we found Teﬀ = 5589.0 ± 50.0 K, [Fe /H] = 0.14 ± 0.08 dex,
log g = 4.54 ± 0.10, and v sin i⋆ = 3.30 ± 0.5 km/s.
3.2. Rotation period of the star
To determine the rotation period of the host star, we
searched for long-term ground-based photometric observa-
tions from surveys such as SuperW ASP (D. L. Pollacco et al.
2006). However, no archival data was available for TOI-
7154. Since the star was observed in multiple TESS sectors
spanning a baseline of approximately 1460 days, we utilized
these datasets to search for periodic signals distinct from the
orbital period of the companion. We used the detrended
ﬂux ( DET or KSPSAP) from several sectors. The light curves
were analyzed without additional long-term trend removal,
after excluding contaminated portions as described in Sec-
tion 2.1. After masking the transits, we applied a Generalized
Lomb-Scargle (GLS) periodogram to the combined dataset
but did not detect any signiﬁcant periodicity. The presence
of substantial data gaps, caused by contamination from scat-
tered sunlight, likely limited the sensitivity of the GLS al-
gorithm to long-term signals. Therefore, we estimated the
stellar rotation period using the projected rotational veloc-
ity derived from the SPC analysis (see Section 3.1, v sin i⋆ =
3.30 ± 0.5 km/s) and the stellar radius ( R⋆ = 0.949+0.032
−0.030 R⊙,
see Section 3.4). Assuming the star is viewed equator-on,
this results in a rotation period of 13 .5 ± 2.0 d, which gives us
the maximum limit of the period to be ≈ 15.5 d.
3.3. Periodogram analysis
We computed the GLS periodogram for the combined
PARAS-2 and TRES RV data, shown in the upper panel of
Figure 3. The theoretical 0.1%, 1%, and 10% false-alarm
probability (FAP) levels are indicated by horizontal dotted
lines. We adopted the 0.1% FAP level as the threshold for
identifying signiﬁcant periodicities. The periodogram shows
two prominent peaks above this threshold: a dominant peak
at ∼ 0.89 days and a secondary peak at ∼ 8.5 days. The latter
is consistent with the orbital period derived from the TESS
light curve ( P ∼ 8.86 days), indicating that it corresponds to
the true physical signal. The ∼ 0.89 day peak and a weaker
peak near ∼ 1.13 days correspond to aliases of the orbital fre-
quency, forb . Speciﬁcally, the ∼ 0.89 day and ∼ 1.13 day sig-
nals correspond to the + forb and − forb aliases, respectively.
To verify this interpretation, we subtracted the ∼ 8.5 day sig-
nal using a best-ﬁt sinusoidal model and computed the peri-
odogram of the residuals (middle panel of Figure 3). In the
residual periodogram, all signiﬁcant peaks, including those at
∼ 0.89 and ∼ 1.13 days disappear, conﬁrming that they orig-
inated from the same underlying periodicity. The spectral

3.4. Global modeling
We characterized the TOI-7154 system using the
EXOFASTv2 package10 (J. D. Eastman et al. 2019), a mod-
eling and ﬁtting suite written in the IDL language that simul-
taneously ﬁts the RV and transit data. It also derives stellar
properties by performing spectral energy distribution (SED)
ﬁtting (K. G. Stassun & G. Torres 2016) in combination with
MIST stellar evolutionary tracks ( J. Choi et al. 2016). The
code utilizes a Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) frame-

3.5. System Age
Since the uncertainties associated with age estimation from
MIST isochrones are large ( ≥ 50%), we employed several
other techniques to constrain the system’s age.
3.5.1. Age estimation using Gyrochronology
We use the derived rotation period Prot and Gaia BP and
RP colors (Table 1), following the empirical gyrochronol-
ogy relations of R. Angus et al. (2019) implemented via the
stardate package11. This yielded a gyrochronological age
of 1 .41 ± 0.34 Gyr. The relatively young age inferred from
rotation contrasts with expectations based on the stars e ﬀec-
tive temperature and surface gravity. One possible explana-
tion is that the empirical relations of R. Angus et al. (2019)
are calibrated primarily for single, isolated stars. Given the
presence of a close-in brown dwarf companion of ≈ 72 MJ in
an 8.86-day orbit, tidal or orbital interactions may have spun
up the star, resulting in an anomalously short rotation period
and, consequently, a younger gyrochronological age ( P . F. L.
Maxted et al. 2015; J. C. Lurie et al. 2017).
3.5.2. Age estimation using Galactic kinematics
To derive the system’s age using galactic kinematics, we
followed the method described in T. Bensby et al. (2014),
wherein we utilize the radial velocity estimates obtained us-
ing the PARAS-2 data along with the position, proper mo-
tion, and distance estimates from Gaia DR3 ( Gaia Collab-
oration et al. 2023b). We calculate the Galactic space ve-
locity components – U, V and W, for TOI-7154 using the
gal_uvw function of the Pyastronomy.pyasl python mod-
ule. We also applied corrections relative to the Local Stan-
dard of Rest (LSR) by taking the Sun’s Galactic space veloc-
ity components ( U⊙, V⊙, W⊙) = (11.1, 12.24, 7.25) km/s from
T. Bensby et al. (2014), where we follow the convention of
U being positive towards the Galactic center, V being posi-
tive in the direction of Galactic rotation and W being posi-
tive towards the North Galactic Pole. Using this approach,
we obtained the Galactic spatial velocity of TOI-7154 as
ULSR = −18.102 ± 0.36 km/s, VLSR = −3.407 ± 0.29 km/s and
WLSR = 14.927 ± 0.19 km/s.
Furthermore, we borrowed the nomenclature scheme and
built up on the procedure in ( T. Bensby et al. 2014) using
the values of asymmetric drift velocities – Uasym and Vasym,

to ﬂatten and eventually reverses as stable hydrogen fusion
becomes possible. Throughout its life, the mass of a BD re-
mains nearly constant over time, but the radius evolves sig-
niﬁcantly as it cools and contracts. Y ounger BDs can retain
radii close to that of Jupiter, whereas older objects ( ≳15 Gyr)
contract to radii below ∼ 1 RJ (I. Baraﬀe et al. 2003; A. Bur-
rows et al. 2011; G. Chabrier et al. 2023).

4.3. F ormation scenario
TOI-7154b, with a mass of 71 .7+2.4
−2.2 MJ, is likely to have
formed via star-like processes, opposed to the mechanisms
for giant planet formation. In such a case, its evolution can
be compared to the formation of close-in stellar binaries.
Most binaries are expected to form through fragmentation
(e.g., M. R. Bate et al. 1995; P . Kroupa 1995; K. M. Krat-
ter & C. D. Matzner 2006; C. J. Clarke 2009; M. Moe & R.
Di Stefano 2017) at separations greater than 10 AU ( A. P .
Boss 1986; M. R. Bate 1998). Close binaries are, therefore,
thought to originate at wider separations and subsequently
migrate inward. In environments with high density of stellar
sources, tidal capture ( W. H. Press & S. A. Teukolsky 1977)
and dynamical interactions ( J. R. Hurley et al. 2007; A. Sol-

measurements of spin-orbit obliquity for TOI-7154 system,
it is di ﬃcult to comment on the exact formation scenario

also lead to the formation of these short-period BDs around

where stellar-like formation mechanisms is proposed.
The eccentricity distribution of BDs has been used to probe
their origins; B. Ma & J. Ge (2014) identiﬁed a transition
near ∼ 42.5 MJ, below which objects follow the eccentric-
ity distribution of massive planets, and above which they
exhibit a broader distribution resembling that of stellar bi-
naries. Subsequent studies have also broadly supported this
trend (e.g., N. Grieves et al. 2017; F. Kiefer et al. 2021). In
Figure 10, we show the mass–eccentricity distribution of the
known transiting BDs, color-coded with the orbital period. It
is evident that the higher-mass BDs ( ≳ 42.5 MJ) showcase a
broader range of eccentricity, while the BDs with the highest
eccentricities are found at longer orbital periods, consistent
with more e ﬃcient tidal circularization at short orbital peri-
ods. TOI-7154b lies in the high-mass regime with moderate
eccentricity, which indicates incomplete tidal circularization,
as discussed in Section 4.2.

5. SUMMARY
We report the discovery and detailed characterisation of
a high-mass brown dwarf transiting the metal-rich G-type
main-sequence star TOI-7154. The system was initially
ﬂagged as a transit candidate by NASAs TESS mission, and
subsequent high-precision radial velocity measurements ob-

APPENDIX
A. VON ZIEPEL-LIDOV -KOZAI SIMULA TIONS
We present an ensemble of secular simulations carried out to explore the parameter space in which migration could be possible.
The theoretical construct for these simulations is adopted as discussed in C. Petrovich (2015a), wherein the interaction potential
between the inner and outer orbits is expanded up to octupolar order in the ratio of their semi-major axes. We model the tidal
dissipation in the inner binary using the equilibrium tide model as per P . P . Eggleton et al. (1998). We also include apsidal
precession due to general relativity and rotational deformation. In addition, we use the empirical stability criteria from C.
Petrovich (2015b) to check for the stability of the system. It should be noted that when the inner orbit is very eccentric, the brown
dwarf is assumed to be tidally disrupted ( a(1 − e) < 2.7R∗ ∼ 0.013 AU, see J. Guillochon et al. (2011) for details).
Figure A.1. The time evolution of eccentricity (top panel), semi-major axis (middle panel) and inclination (bottom panel) of a brown dwarf
perturbed by a 1 M⊙ companion. The colors show the semi-major axis of the companion, while the dashed black lines mark the observed
semi-major axis and eccentricity of TOI-7154b in the middle and top panels, respectively. The brown dwarf depicted in green does not migrate
because the companion resides in a wide orbit. In contrast, the brown dwarf shown in red is tidally disrupted when the companion is close-in, as
the secular perturbation timescale is short compared to the tidal circularizationn timescale. Finally, the case shown in blue undergoes successful
migration. Initially, the brown dwarf is placed in a circular orbit at a distance of 10 AU from the host star, and with the eccentricity of the
companion. The initial mutual inclination between the orbits is set to 85 ◦. The tidal love number and quality factors of the brown dwarf are
kp = 0.5 and Qp′ = 105, respectively. The tidal quality factors for the star are Q′
⋆ = 106, Q′
⋆ = 107 and Q′

panels, respectively.

axis corresponds to the initial semi-major axis of the outer orbit (in AU). Conﬁgurations that are unstable are marked with black circles, while
tidal disruptions are indicated by black squares. Runs in which the brown dwarfs do not migrate are shown with crosses, and those that success-
fully migrate are represented by triangles. The color scale denotes the ﬁnal semi-major axis of the brown dwarf. These results demonstrate that
close-in companions lead either to instability or tidal disruption, whereas systems with wide-orbit companions do not experience migration.
Notably, a solar-mass companion at approximately 1000 AU can facilitate high-eccentricity migration of the brown dwarf. The initial semi–

In general, similar to Figure A.1, close-in companions either lead to unstable systems or tidal disruptions, while systems with
very wide-orbit companions do not experience migration. In the intermediate regime, however, brown dwarfs can migrate close
to their host stars. The scales depend on the tidal deformability of the host star and the brown dwarf. It should also be noted
that for Q⋆ = 106, a larger fraction of the ensemble undergoes successful migration (shown by triangles) compared to Q⋆ = 108.
This shows that stronger tidal impacts may lead to higher migration probability in a given population ensemble for a system like
TOI-7154, as stronger tidal inﬂuence leads to faster tidal dissipation and subsequent circularisation leading to migration, before
disruption can happen.
We’d like to highlight that these simulations are not exhaustive and do not cover the full parameter space, for instance, the
initial inclination is ﬁxed at 85 ◦ and a closer-in companion could migrate without disruption if the initial inclination were lower.
Fully exploring this broad parameter space would require a more rigorous analysis that is beyond the scope of the current work.
