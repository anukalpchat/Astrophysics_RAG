ABSTRACT
IGR J20084+3221 is a previously unclassified Galactic source first detected byINTEGRAL.Chandraobservations led to possi-
ble classifications of either a magnetic Cataclysmic Variable (mCV) or high mass X-ray binary (HMXB) based on the hardness
of its spectrum. Here, we report follow-up observations taken byXMM-Newton,NuSTARand the Hale Telescope at Palomar
Observatory. Based on these observations, we conclude that IGR J20084+3221 is most likely an Intermediate Polar (IP) type
mCV . Timing analysis of the X-ray data found a significant peak period ofP=635.0±0.4 s, which we interpret to be the
spin period of the white dwarf (WD). The X-ray spectrum is well fit to an absorbed Bremsstrahlung model with components
accounting for partial covering, reflection, and a fluorescent Fe-line, all typical for an IP. The optical spectrum shows clear
emission lines, consistent with emission dominated by an accretion disk. We find counterparts to the source across the optical
and infrared (IR) bands, and, despite uncertainties in the distance and extinction, we estimate that the source is too faint in the
IR to be an HMXB. Given the evidence pointing towards an IP classification, we fit the X-ray spectrum to a post-shock region
model where we find a WD mass ofM=1.09 +0.12
−0.11M⊙, larger than the average mass for a WD in an mCV .

1 INTRODUCTION
Cataclysmic Variables (CVs) are binary systems containing a main
sequence donor star and an accreting white dwarf (WD). Of partic-
ular interest for their copious hard X-ray emission are the magnetic
CVs (mCVs). Intermediate Polars (IPs) are a class of mCVs in which
the magnetic field of the WD truncates the accretion disk within a
critical magnetospheric radius. Within this radius, the accreted mate-
rial flows along the WD magnetic field lines, ultimately falling onto
the magnetic poles of the WD and emitting X-rays in the process
(see e.g., K. Mukai 2017 for a review).
Hard X-ray surveys conducted by the International Gamma-Ray
Astrophysics Laboratory (INTEGRAL) have successfully expanded
the known population of mCVs (R. A. Krivonos et al. 2022).INTE-
GRALhas proven particularly successful at detecting IPs with the
highest mass WDs. IGR J14091−6108 and IGR J18434−0508, for
example, have WD mass estimates approaching the Chandrasekhar
limit (J. A. Tomsick et al. 2016; J. Gerber et al. 2024). The average
mass of WDs in CVs is consistently found to be greater than that
of non-accreting WDs, with ¯MWD,CV ∼0.8M ⊙ and ¯MWD,isolated ∼
0.5−0.7M ⊙ (A. W. Shaw et al. 2020; M. Zorotovic et al. 2011).
The reason behind this discrepancy remains debated. Some models

suggest that novae on the surface of the WD should typically ex-
pel more mass than is accreted, leading to an overall decrease in the
mass of accreting WDs (O. Yaron et al. 2005). Others, however, find
that WDs can experience net mass growth from accretion and novae
(S. Starrfield et al. 2020). The lack of consensus on the evolution of
these high-mass WDs necessitates their continued discovery, classi-
fication, and characterization.
Efforts to classifyINTEGRALsources involve follow-up soft X-
ray observations in order to successfully localize the source, en-
abling the identification of optical and infrared (IR) counterparts.
Multiwavelength photometry, coupled with X-ray spectroscopy, can
then be used to classify the source (J. A. Tomsick et al. 2020).
This classification schema was used to identify severalINTEGRAL
sources as IPs, including IGR J14091−6108, IGR J18007−4146,
IGR J15038−6021, and IGR J18434−0508 (J. A. Tomsick et al.
2016; J. Hare et al. 2021a; B. M. Coughenour et al. 2022; J. A. Tom-
sick et al. 2023; J. Gerber et al. 2024). Here, we present a similar
observing and analysis program for IGR J20084+3221 (hereafter,
IGR J20084).
IGR J20084 was first detected in the 14-yearINTEGRALcata-
log in the 17−60 keV band (R. A. Krivonos et al. 2017). Follow
upChandraobservations by J. A. Tomsick et al. (2021) confidently
identified the counterpart CXOU J200844.1+321818 with position
R.A.=20 h08m44.14s,Dec=32 ◦18′18.3′′. Using this more precise

2 OBSERV ATIONS AND DATA REDUCTION
IGR J20084 was observed using bothNuSTARandXMM-Newton
beginning on UTC 2022 May 28 and UTC 2022 May 29, respec-
tively. An optical spectrum was taken by the Double Spectrograph
on the Hale Telescope at Palomar Observatory on UT 2022 May
30. Data from each ofNuSTAR’s Focal Plane Modules (FPMA and
FPMB) as well as each ofXMM-Newton’s EPIC cameras (MOS1,
MOS2, and pn) were reduced to obtain lightcurves and spectra. The
X-ray analysis was done with HEASoft version 6.35.2. Details of
the X-ray observations are summarized in Table 1, and information
on the optical observation is described in Section 2.3.
2.1XMM-Newton
The EPIC/MOS and EPIC/pn data were reduced using theXMM-
NewtonScience Analysis Software version 22.1.0. The MOS1/2 and
pn event lists were filtered using the standard filtering expressions,
“(PATTERN<=12)&&(PI in [200:12000])&&#XMMEA_EM.”
and “(PATTERN<=4)&&(PI in [200:15000])&&(FLAG==0),”
respectively.1 To account for the possibility of soft proton contam-
ination, we filtered the MOS1/2 and pn event lists using the his-
togram method of theespfilttool. This filtering removes an addi-
tional∼40% of the observation’s good time intervals. The number
of counts per frame were roughly 0.02, 0.21, and 0.21 for pn, MOS1,
and MOS2, respectively, which suggests that pile-up is not an issue
(see e.g., Figure 5 in P. Jethwa et al. 2015). Next, we extracted source
and background spectra/event files from the fully filtered MOS1/2
and pn event lists. The sources were extracted from circular regions
of radii 15 ′′. The MOS1 background was extracted from an annu-
lar region centered on the source with an outer radius of 100 ′′ and
an inner radius of 32.5 ′′. Circular background regions (each of ra-
dius 100 ′′) in a nearby, source-free area were used for MOS2 and
pn in order to avoid the edge of the detector chip. Finally, response
matrices were generated using thermfgenandarfgentools.
1 See https://www.cosmos.esa.int/web/xmm-newton/sas-threads#.
2.2NuSTAR
TheNuSTARobservation’s Level 1 event files were produced using
NUSTARDAS v2.1.1 and CALDB 20220215. The high level data
files were then extracted using thenuproductsscript. The input
source regions were circular with radii of 40 ′′ for both FPMA and
FPMB. The FPMB background was extracted from a 3.5′ ×5.0 ′ rect-
angular region on the same detector chip as the source. On FPMA,
the source was within a region of stray light, so a smaller, circular
background region (with a radius of 42.4′′) was used to appropriately
subtract the stray light.
2.3 Palomar
IGR J20084 was observed with the Double Spectrograph (DBSP)
on the 200 ′′ Hale Telescope at Palomar Observatory on UT 2022
May 30. We obtained a single 700 s observation using the 1.5′′ wide
slit at the parallactic angle. The night suffered from clouds and high
humidity. We processed the data using standard procedures, obtain-
ing relative flux calibration from spectrophotometric standard star
observations obtained during UT 2022 May 24-28.

4 X-RAY SPECTRAL ANALYSIS
We binned theNuSTARandXMM-Newtonspectra using the
ftgroupphatool such that each bin had a minimum signal-to-noise
ratio of 5. We fit the joint spectra above 1 keV , cutting offtheNuS-
TARspectra at∼30 keV once the background began to dominate.
To fit the spectra, we used the XSPEC package version 12.15.0d
(K. A. Arnaud 1996). Each model fit to the spectra included atbabs
component to account for absorption due to the interstellar medium
(ISM). Thetbabsmodel uses ISM abundances defined in J. Wilms
et al. (2000) and cross sections defined in D. A. Verner et al. (1996).
Additionally, to account for variation between the different instru-
ments, we applied a multiplicative constant to each model. We froze
the constant to 1 for theNuSTARFPMA spectrum and left it free to
vary for the others.
We began with several phenomenological models to characterize
IGR J20084’s spectral properties before moving on to more physi-
cally motivated models. A simple power-law model alone was un-
able to fit the spectra well (χ 2/ν=766/303). We added a Gaus-
sian component due to the clear sign of an Fe line around 6−7
keV in the residuals. The inclusion of a Gaussian greatly improved
the fit statistic withχ 2/ν=448/300. The best fit photon index was
Γ =1.24±0.03 with a Gaussian line energy ofE line =6.46±0.03 keV
(these and all subsequent confidence intervals are 1σand are calcu-
lated using XSPEC’serrorcommand, unless otherwise stated). We
also attempted a thermalAPECmodel designed for emission from
optically thin plasmas (A. Foster et al. 2023). TheAPECmodel both
including and excluding the Gaussian component failed to constrain
the abundance and temperature.
We next moved on to more physically motivated models.
Bremsstrahlung emission is often associated with IPs as a cooling
mechanism for the accreted material while it falls onto the WD sur-
face (V . Suleimanov et al. 2016). We first fit the spectra with a ther-
mal Bremsstrahlung model and Gaussian component which again
failed to constrain the temperature and did not greatly improve the
χ2 from the power-law fit (χ2/ν=427/300). IPs are often modeled
with a partial covering factor that accounts for absorption by the
accretion “curtain" formed as the material funnels along the WD’s
magnetic field lines (K. Mukai 2017). The inclusion ofpcfabsim-
proved the fit (χ 2/ν=321/298) and gave a best-fit temperature of
kT=35.7 +6.7
−5.2 keV. The coverage fraction was fit tof=0.60±0.03
and hydrogen column density fit toN H,pc =(1.7 +0.4
−0.3)×10 23cm−2.
The Fe fluorescence line atE∼6.4 keV is due to neutral or only
slightly ionized Fe and is a common feature in the spectra of mCVs.
Physically, the 6.4 keV line in an mCV can be attributed to both
the accretion column and scattering offof the surface of the WD
(H. Ezuka & M. Ishida 1999). The presence of the 6.4 keV line sug-
gests that an additional reflection component may be necessary to
model the spectrum. We therefore applied thereflectmodel from
P. Magdziarz & A. A. Zdziarski (1995) to the Bremsstrahlung con-
tinuum. When left to freely fit, therel refl parameter (equivalent to
the fraction of solid angle subtended by the reflector) fit to unphys-
ically high values (i.e.rel refl >1). We therefore, froze the reflection
scaling factor to 1. The inclination was likewise poorly constrained
extending across the parameter space from cosi=0.45−0.95, so
we froze the inclination to cosi=0.45. In thereflectmodel, both
the general abundance parameter and the iron abundance parame-
ter are with respect to solar metallicities. We therefore equated the
two parameters, resulting in a best-fit abundance ofZ=0.15 +0.24
−0.12 Z⊙

We find that the best-fit 3−30 keV flux is 7.0±0.2×10 −12

do so, we replaced the Bremsstrahlung component with the post-

5 OPTICAL SPECTRUM AND MULTIW A VELENGTH
PHOTOMETRY
J. A. Tomsick et al. (2021) identified the multiwavelength counter-
parts to IGR J20084 using a precise X-ray localization obtained with
Chandra. The source is detected from optical to IR wavelengths and
we present the broadband multiwavelength photometry in Table 3.
The uncertain distance and extinction towards the source led J. A.
Tomsick et al. (2021) to suggest that it could either be an mCV or
HMXB.
We obtained an optical spectrum of this counterpart which is
shown in Figure 5. The spectrum is fairly red, consistent with the
broadband photometry and relatively large inferredN H from the X-
ray spectral fitting. There are clear hydrogen (Hα,Hβ, and Paschen
lines), He I, and Ca II triplet emission lines detected in the spectrum,
but no convincing He II lines are observed. TheHαequivalent width
(EW) is∼36 Å and FWHM∼14 Å. There also do not appear to be
any strong absorption features, except for a diffuse interstellar band
(DIB) feature and atmospheric oxygen. The strong and numerous
emission lines detected in the spectrum suggest that it is dominated
by emission from the accretion column and/or disk. While the spec-
trum does appear red, there are no strong Na I D lines, which are
typically associated with Galactic dust extinction.
The X-ray absorbing column density derived from the spectral fits
is aboutN H ≈1.8(2)×10 22 cm−2 (see Table 2). This corresponds to
anA V ≈8 orE(B−V)≈2.6 for an assumedR V =3.1, based
on the relation of T. Güver & F. Özel (2009). Using themwdust
package (J. Bovy et al. 2016) and 3D extinction map of G. M. Green
et al. (2019), this corresponds to a distance of 5.2 kpc, with a range
between 4-6.5 kpc based on the uncertainties inN H. However, we
note that there can be large scatter in theNH toA V andA V to distance
relations based on which 3D extinction map is used, so this distance
estimate should be considered with caution. In any case, the lack of
significantGaiaparallax measurement suggests the source is at a
distance of at least a few kpc. Additionally, despite the uncertainties
inherent to the calculation, the distance based on extinction agrees
rather well with the distance of 5.3 +1.5
−1.9 kpc inferred from theGaia
parallax (C. A. L. Bailer-Jones et al. 2021). At this distance, after
correcting for extinction following the conversions ofAV to theGaia
bands from R. Canbay et al. (2023), used for later comparison, the
source would have an absoluteGaia Gmagnitude ofM G ≈ −0.9 and
a very blue color (i.e.,Gaia BP−RP≈ −0.9). This suggests that the
optical emission is dominated by the accretion disk, consistent with
the emission lines seen in the optical spectrum. Furthermore, the
absolute magnitude of the IR counterpart to the source is generally
too faint to be consistent with a Be star at∼5 kpc (see e.g., C. Gobat
et al. 2022). Therefore, we favor the CV scenario for the nature of
the source.

6 DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS
Each line of evidence presented from the follow-up X-ray and op-
tical observations contributes to the classification of IGR J20084 as
an IP. First, timing analysis of the X-ray data revealed a character-
istic period atP∼635 s. We interpret this to be the spin period of
the compact object which places IGR J20084 squarely in the typi-
cal range of WD spin periods in IPs (V . F. Suleimanov et al. 2019).
If we instead consider the possibility that the detected period rep-
resents the system’s orbital period, then IGR J20084 would be an
ultra-compact X-ray binary (UCXB). However, an analysis of 16

UCXBs by F. Koliopanos et al. (2021) demonstrated that UCXBs
typically show either no or weak Fe lines (EW∼50−200 eV). The
strong Fe line evident in the X-ray spectrum of IGR J20084 (see
Figure 4) clearly rules out the possibility of a UCXB. The detected
period is therefore most likely the spin period of the compact object.
The X-ray spectral analysis further contributes to the IP classifi-
cation. We confirm the finding of J. A. Tomsick et al. (2021) that
IGR J20084 exhibits a hard X-ray spectrum, characteristic of an IP.
In particular, we apply a thermal Bremsstrahlung model to extract
a plasma temperature ofkT=26.1 +7.3
−4.3 keV and a 3−30 keV flux
of 7.0±0.2×10 −12 erg s −1 cm−2. If we adopt a distance estimate
of 4−6.5 kpc, we find a luminosity of (1.3−3.5)×10 34 erg s−1.
This luminosity is typical ofINTEGRALdetected CVs, but is on the
higher end of the population (see e.g., Figure 6 in A. Lutovinov et al.
2020).
The X-ray analysis on its own does not definitively eliminate the
possibility that IGR J20084 is an HMXB. In some cases, neutron
stars in HMXBs can have spin periods of hundreds of seconds (see
e.g., A. Bodaghee et al. 2006) as well as X-ray spectra with a hard
continuum and strong Fe emission lines (F. Fornasini et al. 2023).
It is the optical spectroscopy and multiwavelength photometry that
rules out the HMXB scenario. IGR J20084 does not have the bright
IR counterpart that would be indicative of an HMXB, and its optical
spectrum appears to be dominated by the accretion disk.
Based on the estimate of the source’s dereddened absoluteGaia
Gband magnitude andGaia BP−RPcolor, IGR J20084 lies mostly
among others with orbital periods longer than 2.6 hours (R. Canbay
et al. 2023). In fact, IGR J20084 shows some similarity to Swift
J0939.7–3224, which is a CV discovered by theSwift-BAT survey
(A. Y . Lien et al. 2025). With a distance of≈3 kpc (C. A. L. Bailer-
Jones et al. 2021), Swift J0939.7–3224 was detected from optical to
IR wavelengths. Follow-up optical spectroscopy of Swift J0939.7–
3224 by J. P. Halpern & J. R. Thorstensen (2015) found an 8.5 hr
orbital period. They additionally found relatively weak Hαemission
(EW≈22 Å) and no clear evidence of the companion star. These
similarities suggest that IGR J20084 may have a comparable orbital
period, which could be measured by future optical radial velocity
measurements.

find new IPs. As exposure time has increased throughout the mis-
sion and fainter persistent sources have been detected, it has become
much more common for the Galactic sources to be IPs even though
HMXBs were common in the early mission. With the mission end-
ing in 2025, it has now reached its maximum sensitivity, and one
would expect that producing a final hard X-ray source catalog along
with follow-up X-ray and optical observations will yield additional
IPs.
Although anINTEGRALIP population study is beyond the scope
of this paper, IGR J20084 is the fifth IGR IP that we have studied in
depth withXMM-NewtonandNuSTAR, leading to X-ray measure-
ments of the WD spin period and mass. The WD spin period mea-
sured for IGR J20084 (P=635.0±0.4 s) is relatively typical of IPs,
and it is only slightly longer than the three slowest spinning IGR IPs
that we have studied, which range from 304.4 s to 576.3 s. Among
our five IGR IPs, IGR J15038–6021 is the outlier with a period of
1678 s (J. A. Tomsick et al. 2023). For WD masses, three of our IGR
IPs are consistent with the Chandrasekhar mass, but IGR J20084’s
mass ofM WD =1.09 +0.12
−0.11 M⊙ is more similar to IGR J18007–4146
with a mass of 1.06 M⊙ (B. M. Coughenour et al. 2022). However, it
should be noted that all five IGR IPs have high masses compared to
the overall IP population (J. Gerber et al. 2024), which is consistent
with their stronger hard X-ray emission.
Finally, while X-ray observations are critical for measuring the
spin and mass, this study also shows the importance of optical
spectroscopy. Given some of the observational similarities between
HMXBs and IPs in the X-ray, it is the optical spectrum that has
shown the presence of emission lines from an accretion disk and
therefore has provided strong evidence in favor of IGR J20084 be-
ing an IP.
