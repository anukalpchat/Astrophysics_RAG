ABSTRACT
The ultraviolet (UV) spectrum of a host star strongly shapes the atmospheric composition and poten-
tial biosignatures of its planets. This relationship may be especially important for the planets orbiting
TRAPPIST-1, an M8V star with substantially different published UV spectral energy distributions
(SEDs). Using a one-dimensional photochemical model, we quantify how these SED uncertainties
affect Archean Earth–like atmospheric analogs on TRAPPIST-1 e with and without biospheres. We
emphasize Earth’s Archean epoch because it represents a planet in transition from primarily abiotic
to biotic controls on atmospheric composition. Different stellar spectra produce order-of-magnitude
variations in the predicted abundances of CH 4, CO, O 2, and O 3, thereby generating photochemical
degeneracies that complicate the interpretation of potential biosignatures. For one TRAPPIST-1 UV
reconstruction, a modeled atmosphere with abiotic deposition velocities and low CH 4 input can sus-
tain simultaneous spectrally discernible CH 4 and O3, yielding a potential false-positive disequilibrium
biosignature. For all SEDs tested, surface deposition consistent with microbially-mediated CO con-
sumption allows substantial O2 and O3 accumulation even without oxygenic photosynthesis, implying
that oxygen-rich atmospheres around ultracool M dwarfs may not uniquely trace oxygenic ecosystems.
Across our models, CO remains a powerful discriminator between abiotic and biotic surface boundary
assumptions. Overall, we show that the abundances of co-occurring CH 4, CO, and O 3 can vary by
orders of magnitude, depending on the assumed stellar spectrum, creating ambiguities in interpreting
atmospheric biosignatures, though observability may be challenging with current capabilities. Reduc-
ing UV spectral uncertainties is therefore essential for assessing surface-to-atmosphere interactions of
temperate exoplanets around ultracool M dwarfs.

The TRAPPIST-1 system (d≈12.5 pc) remains a
major benchmark for assessing the habitability and at-
mospheric evolution of terrestrial planets around ultra-
cool M dwarfs (M. Gillon et al. 2016, 2017). With four
planets (d–g) in or near the habitable zone (E. Ducrot
et al. 2020; E. Agol et al. 2021), it has become a fo-
cal point for astrobiology. Yet the same stellar environ-

Observationally, a coherent picture is emerging for
the inner planets. Pre-JWST transmission spectra for
TRAPPIST-1 b, c, and d were featureless at low res-
olution, excluding cloud-free H 2-rich envelopes (J. de
Wit et al. 2016, 2018). JWST thermal-phase and
eclipse measurements now indicate that b is most con-
sistent with an airless rocky surface, with thick atmo-
spheres strongly disfavored and only a narrow range
of tenuous, low-opacity alternatives not yet fully ex-
cluded (T. P. Greene et al. 2023; M. Gillon et al.
2026). For c, new phase-curve constraints disfavor
both a thick CO 2-dominated atmosphere and most
other dense-atmosphere scenarios, while still permit-

a low-opacity secondary atmosphere, such as aO 2-

small amounts of CO 2 and H 2O (S. Zieba et al. 2023;
M. Gillon et al. 2026). For d, corrected transmis-
sion spectra show no molecular features, tightening
limits on high-mean-molecular-weight atmospheres (C.
Piaulet-Ghorayeb et al. 2025). For potentially habitable
TRAPPIST-1 e, recent measurements rule out H 2-rich
envelopes and disfavor CO 2-dominated cases, though
N2-dominated secondary atmospheres with mixtures of
CH4 and trace CO 2 remain viable when spectral inter-
pretation attempts to control for stellar contamination
(A. Glidden et al. 2025; N. Espinoza et al. 2025).
Photochemical studies of TRAPPIST-1’s habitable-
zone planets have explored a broad range of atmospheric
scenarios, including desiccated O 2-rich and Venus-like
cases, as well as CO 2-N2, CO2-O2, prebiotic, and mod-
ern Earth-like compositions (J. T. O’Malley-James & L.
Kaltenegger 2017; A. P. Lincowski et al. 2018; F. Wun-
derlich et al. 2020; Z. Lin et al. 2021). Another rele-
vant comparison case is the Archean Earth, an anoxic
planetary state spanning from 4.0 to 2.5 Ga. This inter-
val contains some of the earliest, most widely accepted
evidence for life (A. C. Allwood et al. 2006; N. Nof-
fke et al. 2013; B. Cavalazzi et al. 2021; J. M. Wolfe &
G. P. Fournier 2018; M. L. Wong et al. 2025). Compared
to the modern Earth’s atmosphere, the Archean atmo-
sphere likely featured higher CO2, robust CH4 from mi-
crobial methanogenesis, negligible O2 and O3, and com-
parable or lower N 2 concentrations (P. Kharecha et al.
2005; D. C. Catling & K. J. Zahnle 2020; D. Chen &
D. C. Catling 2026). The Archean Earth likely repre-
sents a world in transition from one with primarily abi-
otic controls on atmospheric composition to one where
life began to play a prominent role (C. K. Jones et al.
2025). Archean-like atmospheric compositions provide a
physically motivated counterpoint to modern-Earth-like
assumptions for temperate TRAPPIST-1 worlds and
motivate the absence of an imposed biological O 2 sur-
face flux in this work (M. T. Mak et al. 2024; J. K.
Eager-Nash et al. 2024).
A central uncertainty in such modeling is the star’s
UV spectral energy distribution (SED), which sets key
photolysis rates and atmospheric lifetimes (K. France
et al. 2016; T. Kozakis et al. 2022). Several distinct
TRAPPIST-1 SEDs are now used in the literature. S.
Peacock et al. (2019) computed a series of threePHOENIX
atmosphere models of TRAPPIST-1 calibrated to either
the V. Bourrier et al. (2017) Lyαflux of TRAPPIST-
1 or distance-adjusted GALEX photometry of similar
M8 stars. As part of the Mega-MUSCLES (Measure-
ments of the Ultraviolet Spectral Characteristics of Low-
mass Exoplanetary Systems) Treasury survey, D. J. Wil-
son et al. (2021) obtained HST Cosmic Origins Spec-
trograph (COS) and Space Telescope Imaging Spectro-
graph (STIS) measurements of TRAPPIST-1, resolving
several FUV emission lines and the Mg II h & k doublet
in the NUV. With these data, they released two alter-
native SEDs: the Mega-MUSCLES Adaptive panchro-
matic SED, intended to represent quiescent output, and
the Mega-MUSCLES Semi-empirical Model (SEM) vari-
ant, which replaces low-S/N 110 nm to 420 nm data with
a smoothed polynomial, reconstructed Lyα, and syn-
thetic emission lines. D. J. Wilson et al. (2021) recom-
mended the SEM SED for planetary atmosphere studies
because it replaces the noisiest observed UV regions with
a semi-empirical reconstruction. However, the SEDs dif-
fer substantially across the far- and near-UV, where the
photoabsorption cross sections of CH 4, CO2, H2O, O2,
and O3 are large. In particular, the Adaptive and SEM
SEDs differ in the relative distribution of flux between
the FUV (120.0 nm to 175.0 nm) and NUV (175.0 nm to
312.5 nm), a distinction that can strongly affect oxida-
tive photochemistry (C. E. Harman et al. 2015).
Prior studies already indicate that this sensitivity
matters. One-dimensional photochemical models have
shown that species such as O 3, H 2O, and hydrocar-
bon hazes respond strongly to UV flux differences
between stars (A. Segura et al. 2005; J. L. Gren-
fell et al. 2014; D. J. Teal et al. 2022). Recent
TRAPPIST-1 e modeling has further shown that pre-
oxygenic biospheres can permit surface O 2 accumula-
tion alongside substantial atmospheric CO, reinforc-
ing the need to interpret Archean-analog biosignatures
in the joint context of stellar UV forcing and lower
boundary conditions (J. K. Eager-Nash et al. 2024).

In this study, we quantify how plausible TRAPPIST-1
UV SED choices alone drive order-of-magnitude differ-
ences in predicted atmospheric photochemistry on an
Archean-analog TRAPPIST-1 e with and without life.
Using a one-dimensional photochemical model, we com-
pare steady-state abundances of CH 4, CO, O 2, and O 3
and compute transmission spectra across multiple in-
put SEDs. We show that high-FUV/low-NUV cases
can produce detectable O 3 without life while simulta-

4
neously enhancing atmospheric CO, complicating the
common CH 4+O3 biosignature heuristic and creating
false-positive and false-negative degeneracies for temper-

tify observational discriminants, particularly joint con-
straints on CO, CH4, and O3 spanning the JWST NIR-
Spec and MIRI wavelength ranges. Finally, we demon-
strate that in inhabited worlds, substantial O 2 and O3
concentrations can arise for modeled cases with low CH4
fluxes, without oxygenic photosynthesis, through the
adoption of surface deposition consistent with microbial
CO consumption.
2.METHODS
2.1.Stellar Spectra Modeling, Processing, and Binning
We explore the impact of using five distinct SEDs
as stellar input in our one-dimensional photochemical
model (Figure 1): a median spectrum of the three S.
Peacock et al. 2019PHOENIXmodels, both the Mega-
MUSCLES Adaptive and SEM SEDs from D. J. Wilson
et al. 2021, a “Revised PHOENIX” model described be-
low, and a scaled modern solar reference spectrum based
on high-resolution ATmospheric Laboratory for Appli-
cations and Science (ATLAS-1) composite solar irradi-
ance data (G. Thuillier et al. 2004).
The “Revised PHOENIX” spectrum is an update mo-
tivated by the Mega-MUSCLES observations obtained
after the initial TRAPPIST-1PHOENIXmodels of S. Pea-
cock et al. (2019). The more recent HST data provide
improved empirical constraints on the UV spectrum,
particularly in the FUV and for the Mg II h & k doublet
in the NUV, necessitating an updated model. However,
this version has not yet been fully validated, and we
therefore treat it as an illustrative end-member rather
than a definitive reconstruction of TRAPPIST-1’s UV
output.
We construct a revisedPHOENIXatmosphere model
for TRAPPIST-1 using stellar parameters from E. Agol
et al. (2021) (T eff = 2566 K, logg= 5.24), with
HST/COS and STIS UV observations providing empir-
ical constraints on the chromosphere and transition re-
gion. The model follows the general framework of S.
Peacock et al. (2019), consisting of a photosphere with
parametrized temperature rises in the chromosphere and
transition region defined in column mass space. The
transition region is extended to 2×10 5 K to repro-
duce high-temperature emission lines observed in the
UV. Relative to the S. Peacock et al. (2019) models,
we include an expanded non-LTE treatment (78 versus
63 ionization stages) and updated atomic processes, in-
cluding additional collisional rates with hydrogen and
improvements to the treatment of the HILyman-αline
core.
To identify a representative spectrum, we computed a
grid of 72 models spanning a range of chromospheric and
transition region thicknesses and locations. The best-fit
model was selected viaχ 2 minimization against a set of
well-resolved UV emission lines in the HST data, using
continuum-normalized line fluxes measured over consis-
tent wavelength intervals. The resulting model repro-
duces the FUV pseudocontinuum and matches 7 of 13
diagnostic line fluxes within observational uncertainties,
with most remaining lines within a factor of a few.
In terms of band-integrated UV fluxes, the “Revised
PHOENIX” spectrum is broadly consistent with the
Mega-MUSCLES Adaptive SED in the FUV and NUV,
but with subtle systematic differences. The FUV flux
is in close agreement with the empirical reconstruction,
differing at the∼0.1 dex level. In the NUV, the agree-
ment is similarly good (within∼0.1 dex), although con-
straints are weaker due to the limited signal-to-noise of
the HST/STIS observations and the lack of strong con-
tinuum diagnostics beyond the Mg II h & k doublet.
Figure 1 shows the scaled SEDs, illustrating the UV
structure relevant to the photochemical calculations.
For input intoAtmos, the TRAPPIST-1 SEDs were
subsequently re-binned to the model wavelength grid
with flux-conserving resampling usingSpectRes. Spuri-
ous negative-flux bins were removed within the Mega-
MUSCLES Adaptive data before rebinning was com-
pleted. Across UV wavelengths, the SEDs vary signif-
icantly, with FUV/NUV flux ratios spanning two or-
ders of magnitude across the TRAPPIST-1 SEDs. The
spectra also differ in the relative strengths of individual
emission features. In the rebinned spectra, the Lyαline
at 121.6 nm ranges from being only modestly enhanced
above the neighboring FUV continuum in the Mega-
MUSCLES Adaptive SED to being enhanced by roughly
two orders of magnitude in the Mega-MUSCLES SEM
SED. Similarly, the Mg II h & k region near 280 nm is
prominent in several of the SEDs, but its contrast rela-
tive to the adjacent NUV continuum varies substantially
across the TRAPPIST-1 SEDs.
The photochemical importance of these spectral dif-
ferences depends strongly on wavelength. In Figure 1,
we also show the absorption cross sections of key at-
mospheric species to highlight where differences among
the adopted SEDs overlap with the UV bands respon-
sible for photolysis of CH 4, CO 2, H 2O, O 2, and O 3.
Between 120 nm to 160 nm, the stellar flux overlaps
with large photoabsorption cross sections for CH4, CO2,
H2O, and O 2; differences in this interval therefore af-
fect direct CH 4 destruction, CO2 photolysis to CO and
O, and the production of H-bearing radicals from H 2O.
From roughly 160 nm to 210 nm, CH 4 absorption de-
creases rapidly, but CO 2, H 2O, and O 2 absorption re-
main relevant, so this interval continues to influence the
balance between oxidant production and HOx-mediated
recycling of CO and CH 4. At longer wavelengths, es-
pecially from 200 nm to 350 nm, O 3 is the dominant
absorber among the species shown, making the NUV
continuum and strong features such as Mg II h & k
important for O 3 photolysis. Thus, spectra such as
Mega-MUSCLES SEM, which retain short-wavelength

5
FUV emission while suppressing much of the longer-
wavelength UV continuum, can favor CO 2-driven oxi-
dant production while reducing the UV pathways that
would otherwise enhance radical production and O 3 de-
struction.
2.2.Photochemical Modeling withAtmos
We simulate Archean-analog atmospheres for
TRAPPIST-1 e with the publicly available one-
dimensional photochemical model included within
Atmos. The model descends from the Kasting group’s
one-dimensional photochemical framework (J. F. Kast-
ing et al. 1979; A. A. Pavlov et al. 2001; K. J. Zahnle
et al. 2006). Subsequent updates to the model’s kinetics,
radiative transfer, and wavelength-dependent cross sec-
tions have since been made.Atmoshas been extensively
used within the astrobiology community, including for
simulations of the atmosphere of the Archean Earth
and of Archean-analog exoplanet atmospheres (G. Ar-
ney et al. 2016; G. N. Arney et al. 2017; D. J. Teal et al.
2022; E. T. Wolf et al. 2025). The model integrates the
coupled continuity equations for all atmospheric species
to steady state, balancing chemical and photolytic pro-
duction and loss with vertical transport by eddy and
molecular diffusion. Photolysis rates are computed
from wavelength-dependent absorption cross sections
and quantum yields convolved with the input stellar
spectral energy distributions, which directly link the
stellar UV spectrum to the atmospheric composition.
All modeled atmospheres assume a surface pressure
of 1 bar with a partial pressure of 0.1 bar CO 2, a plane-
tary radius fixed to the measured value of TRAPPIST-
1 e, and a surface gravity consistent with its measured
mass and radius (E. Agol et al. 2021). Following the
methods of R. K. Kopparapu et al. (2013), we set the
surface temperature to 288.4 K and adopt a tempera-
ture–pressure profile consisting of a moist pseudoadia-
bat from the surface up to an isothermal 180 K strato-
sphere. The adopted atmospheric fluxes, surface mixing
ratios, and dry deposition velocities used in the model
are provided in Table 1.
Alongside the previously available modern solar refer-
ence spectrum based on the ATLAS-1 composite solar
irradiance spectrum of G. Thuillier et al. (2004) and
the Peacock+2019 Median SED, we import the three
generated TRAPPIST-1 SEDs (Mega-MUSCLES Adap-
tive, Mega-MUSCLES SEM, and “Revised PHOENIX”)
intoAtmosto determine the extent of the atmospheric
photochemical differences as a function of the differ-
ences in the UV region of these spectral energy distri-
butions, especially as the UV helps to drive the core
CO2-CH4 photochemical-redox network. In the relevant
FUV bands, photolysis of CO2 produces CO and atomic
oxygen. Atomic oxygen recombines to form O 2, and O2
participates in Chapman chemistry to form O 3 (C. E.
Harman et al. 2015). The direct reaction of CO with
O is spin-forbidden and inefficient, so CO tends to ac-
cumulate in the cases with abiotic deposition velocities
unless catalytic cycles or surface sinks return it to CO 2.
The dominant gas-phase removal pathway for CO is by
reaction with OH, yielding CO 2 and H. The abundance
of HO x (OH + HO 2) therefore controls both the re-
cycling of CO to CO 2 and the lifetime of CH 4, which
oxidizes through a short sequence that passes through
formaldehyde and CO before reaching CO2. When HO x
production is strong, CO is efficiently recycled and CH 4
lifetimes are short. When HO x production is weak, CO
lifetimes increase and CH 4 persists.
We incorporate updated cross sections for H 2O and
CO2. Extending H 2O absorption into the near-UV in-
creases HO x production, speeds conversion of CO to
CO2, and shortens CH 4 lifetimes, which avoids artifi-
cial CO buildup that can occur if short-wavelength H2O
absorption is underestimated (S. Ranjan et al. 2020; W.
Broussard et al. 2024). Updated CO2 cross sections have
a small positive effect on the CO 2 photolysis rates and
the partial shielding of H 2O photolysis (W. Broussard
et al. 2025). These updated cross-sections, along with
the molecular cross-sections of CH4, O2, and O3 are also
shown in Figure 1.
To represent contrasting lower boundary conditions,
we prescribe different dry deposition velocities for gases
that life is expected to consume efficiently. Following
P. Kharecha et al. (2005), we adopt two deposition ve-
locity regimes. In the biotic deposition velocity cases,
strong surface sinks for CO, H 2, and O 2 at 1.2×10 −4,
2.4×10 −4, and 1.4×10 −4 cm s −1 are set respectively.
Cases with abiotic deposition velocities use weak or neg-
ligible sinks at 1.0×10 −8, 0, and 0 cm s −1, respectively.
Cases with abiotic deposition velocities are paired with
FCH4 equal to 10 8, 10 9, and 10 10 molecules cm −2 s−1,
spanning methane fluxes consistent with volcanism up
to an ambiguous end member consistent both with a cal-
culated maximum abiotic flux from global serpentiniza-
tion and with a modest-sized methanogenic biosphere
(J. Krissansen-Totton et al. 2018a). Cases with biotic
deposition velocities are paired withF CH4 equal to 109,
1010, and 10 11 molecules cm −2 s−1, representing a flux
range constrained by limited CH 4-producing biospheres
up to a modern-Earth-like CH 4 flux (D. C. Catling &
J. F. Kasting 2017a; J. Krissansen-Totton et al. 2018b;
N. Wogan et al. 2020; M. A. Thompson et al. 2022). The
overlapping fluxes are intentionally included in both the
abiotic and biotic deposition regimes because all but the
highest fluxes are not uniquely diagnostic of either bi-
ology or geology. This overlap allows us to isolate how
biologically enhanced surface uptake of CO, H2, and O2
alters atmospheric chemistry for a fixed CH 4 input.
Lightning is a major natural source of atmospheric
NOx on modern Earth (U. Schumann & H. Huntrieser
2007). Our photochemical model includes a lightning
source of NO, determined by the bulk atmospheric com-
position and normalized to the lightning production in
the atmosphere of modern Earth. NO can catalyze the

2.3.Synthetic Transmission Spectra Generation with
SMART
For each individualAtmosrun, we generate at-
mospheric profiles and then use them to compute
synthetic transmission spectra that include atmo-
spheric refraction but neglect clouds. We use
theSpectral Mapping and Atmospheric Radiative
Transfer(SMART) one-dimensional line-by-line, multi-
stream, multiple-scattering radiative transfer model to
generate the synthetic spectra (K. Stamnes et al. 1988;
V. S. Meadows & D. Crisp 1996; D. Crisp 1997).SMART
uses line lists sourced from the high-resolution trans-
mission molecular absorption database (HITRAN-2020)
(I. E. Gordon et al. 2022) to calculate the opacities of
major atmospheric species using its companion program
LBLABC. The resulting synthetic transmission spectra are
shown in Figures 3 and 4. Each figure includes both the
JWST/NIRSpec (0.6µm to 5.3µm) and MIRI (5.0µm
to 12.0µm) wavelength ranges, and the plotted spectra
are down-binned to match the resolutions of the cor-
responding instrument modes. Although we primarily
focus on the implications for interpreting space-based
infrared transmission spectra with JWST or successor
telescope concepts such as the Origins Space Telescope
(C. Battersby et al. 2018; M. Meixner et al. 2019; D. T.
Leisawitz et al. 2021), these wavelength ranges are also
expected to be observable with ground-based ELT-class
telescopes (K. K. Hardegree-Ullman et al. 2023; M. H.
Currie et al. 2023).
3.RESULTS
3.1.Photochemical Results
For each of the four TRAPPIST-1 SEDs, we run six
Atmosphotochemical simulations: three with abiotic de-
position velocities and three with biotic deposition ve-
locities, each paired with the corresponding prescribed
CH4 surface fluxes. These results are plotted in Fig-
ure 2. One case requires a small exception: for the bi-
otic Mega-MUSCLES Adaptive scenario, the nominal
FCH4 = 1010 molecules cm −2 s−1 run did not converge
inAtmos, so we instead show the nearest converged so-
lution atF CH4 = 1.5×10 10 molecules cm−2 s−1.
Increasing CH4 surface fluxes increases the CH 4 mix-
ing ratios as expected for all SEDs considered. For any
fixed CH 4 surface flux, however, different SEDs pro-
duce order-of-magnitude variations in the resulting CH4
abundance. Across our parameter space explored, the
CH4 mixing ratios span nearly three orders of magni-
tude, set jointly by the input SED, the imposed CH 4
surface flux, and the choice of abiotic versus biotic de-
position velocities.
For most of the modeled cases with low CH 4 fluxes,
CO mixing ratios account for only a small percentage of
the atmosphere. However, as CH 4 fluxes increase, there
is more CH 4 available to be photo-oxidized to CO. Al-
though this CO can be further oxidized to CO 2, this
reaction is rate-limited, as there is not enough OH avail-
able, and thus CO is produced faster than it can be re-
moved (J. F. Kasting et al. 1983; P. Kharecha et al. 2005;
K. Zahnle et al. 2008; S. Ranjan et al. 2022). As a result,
CO can accumulate in the atmospheres modeled under
the abiotic regime (i.e., in the absence of robust surface
sinks), particularly in the “Peacock+2019 Median” and
“Mega-MUSCLES SEM” SEDs. However, the mixing
ratios of CO are kept three to four orders of magnitude
lower in the cases with biotic deposition velocities com-
pared to those with abiotic deposition velocities, since
more CO is removed from the atmosphere as the surface
deposition velocity increases.
O2 and O 3 abundances vary strongly across the
modeled cases, with their significance being strongly
spectrum- and condition-dependent. The clearest abi-
otic oxidant buildup occurs in the low-CH 4-flux Mega-
MUSCLES SEM case, where the surface O2 mixing ratio
reaches 5.3%. The biotic runs, especially for low CH 4
fluxes, can also build up O 2; for example, the “Revised
PHOENIX” biotic case has a surface O 2 mixing ratio
of about 390 ppm. In these oxidized regimes, O 2 ac-
cumulates when O atoms generated by CO 2 photolysis
under FUV irradiation recombine through three-body
reactions such as O + O + M− − →O 2 + M, where M
denotes a background atmospheric molecule that car-
ries away excess energy, while reductant inputs remain
low (C. E. Harman et al. 2015). In the biotic cases, en-
hanced surface uptake of CO further promotes oxidant
buildup by suppressing the dominant CO+OH recombi-
nation pathway, thereby allowing more photochemically
produced oxygen to persist as O 2 and, downstream, O3,
consistent with the mechanism identified by J. K. Eager-
Nash et al. (2024).

hanced HNO 3, indicating more active NO x cycling in
the atmosphere. The ozone-loss step of interest is NO +

3.2.Synthetic Transmission Spectra
Each converged photochemical run was also used as
input toSMARTto generate synthetic cloud-free trans-
mission spectra. Figure 3 shows the cases with abiotic
deposition velocities, while Figure 4 shows the cases
with biotic deposition velocities, each spanning both
the JWST/NIRSpec and MIRI wavelength ranges. We
adopt cloud-free spectra as an idealized baseline to iso-
late the spectral differences between the photochemical
solutions. Low-altitude H 2O clouds could reduce abso-
lute feature amplitudes, especially for lower-atmosphere
absorbers, but would not necessarily obscure the CH 4,
CO, CO 2, and O 3 bands emphasized here, as their ef-
fective transit depths lie above the cloud deck. These
spectra are therefore best interpreted as cloud-free refer-
ence cases rather than cloud-inclusive detectability pre-
dictions (T. J. Fauchez et al. 2019).
Across both figures, increasing the imposed CH 4 sur-
face flux produces systematically stronger CH 4 ab-
sorption features. At fixedF CH4, spectra with larger
FUV/NUV ratios, notably the Mega-MUSCLES SEM
SED, yield the strongest methane features (see Fig-
ure 5). In these cases, the weak NUV flux suppresses
HOx production, extending the atmospheric lifetime of
CH4. Conversely, the other SEDs, with stronger NUV
fluxes, generate more HO x, allowing more rapid oxida-
tion of CH4, decreasing its column abundance and mut-
ing the associated spectral features. This behavior is
most apparent in the broad 3.3µm and 7.7µm bands, al-
though several narrower CH4 features across both wave-
length ranges also vary appreciably.
In the spectra corresponding to cases with abiotic de-
position velocities (Figure 3), CO features become in-
creasingly apparent at high CH 4 surface fluxes. This
effect is most noticeable in the 4.6µm to 4.8µm band
and reflects enhanced atmospheric CO accumulation un-
der weak surface uptake (see Figure 2). A distinct O 3
feature near 9.7µm is also apparent and is most evi-
dent in the low-FCH4 Mega-MUSCLES SEM case, while
the other cases with abiotic deposition velocities show
only weak or absent O 3 features. As a result, for one
TRAPPIST-1 UV reconstruction, an atmosphere can
sustain simultaneous spectral signatures of both CH 4
and O3 without life.
In the spectra generated from the cases with biotic
deposition velocities (Figure 4), CO absorption is sub-
stantially reduced relative to the cases with abiotic de-
position velocities because enhanced surface uptake sup-
presses atmospheric CO. At the same time, several low-
FCH4 biotic cases retain appreciable O 3 absorption near
9.7µm, although this feature weakens substantially as
FCH4 increases and the atmosphere becomes more re-
ducing. These spectra therefore highlight the contrast-
ing observational outcomes of biologically enhanced CO
consumption: muted CO features together with retained
oxidant signatures.
Although organic haze is typically considered to hide
other spectral features, it can also act as a biosignature
when present in high-CO 2 atmospheres, as a high sur-
face flux of CH 4 is necessary to produce these haze lay-
ers. However, since the mixing ratio of CH 4 never rises
above a tenth of the mixing ratio of CO 2, the formation
of haze is limited, and thus significant haze features are
not present within these spectra (M. G. Trainer et al.
2006; G. N. Arney et al. 2017).
4.DISCUSSION
Our results demonstrate that uncertainties in
TRAPPIST-1’s UV SEDs propagate directly into the
chemical context in which biosignatures are interpreted.
For Archean-analog atmospheres around ultracool M
dwarfs, the main ambiguity is not the CH 4-CO2 dise-
quilibrium framework by itself, which is typically con-
sidered alongside low CO, but whether the combined
CH4-CO2-O3 signature can arise abiotically. For some
UV characterizations of TRAPPIST-1, including the
Mega-MUSCLES SEM, abiotic and biotic deposition-
velocity scenarios can produce overlapping O 2 and O 3
abundance regimes. In such cases, the coexistence of
CH4, CO 2, and O 3 is not by itself uniquely diagnostic
of life. Instead, CO provides critical context: abun-
dant CO points to abiotic O 3 accumulation driven by
CO2 photolysis and inefficient surface removal, whereas
muted CO at comparable CH 4 and O 3 levels is more
consistent with efficient biological CO consumption.
Two major pathways capture the accumulation of
O2 and O 3 within these Archean-analog atmospheres.
First, in the cases with abiotic deposition velocities,
the high FUV/NUV ratio associated with the Mega-
MUSCLES SEM SED produces elevated O2 at low CH4
surface fluxes because the enhanced FUV flux drives

4.1.Degeneracies Controlled by the Stellar FUV/NUV
Ratio
Figure 5 summarizes these behaviors using the stellar
FUV/NUV ratio as a convenient proxy for differences
among the input spectra. Although the FUV/NUV ra-
tio is not a complete predictor of the resulting atmo-
spheric state, it represents the dominant contrast among
the adopted SEDs: “Mega-MUSCLES SEM” combines
strong short-wavelength oxidant production with rel-
atively weak longer-wavelength radical and ozone-loss
pathways, producing the most oxidized abiotic solutions
in our model suite. The spread in whisker ranges there-
fore highlights that changes in the assumed TRAPPIST-
1 UV reconstruction can move Archean-analog atmo-
spheres between relatively weakly and strongly oxidized
states. In the cases with biotic deposition velocities, O 2
and O 3 mixing ratios also span many orders of mag-
nitude, but their behavior reflects the combined effects
of stellar UV forcing, imposed CH 4 flux, enhanced CO
uptake, the lower-boundary O2 sink, and NOx-mediated
ozone loss. This behavior is most visually distinct in the
cases with abiotic deposition velocities, where “Mega-
MUSCLES SEM” occupies a high-FUV/NUV, oxidant-
rich regime. Because the abiotic and biotic panels use
CH4 ranges offset by an order of magnitude, the ranges
should be interpreted as regime-specific envelopes rather
than as a one-to-one comparison. A similar figure can
be found in Appendix B that presents the same com-
parison using atmospheric column densities instead of
planetary surface volume mixing ratios.
These trends are consistent with earlier work showing
that high FUV/NUV ratios can drive CO 2-dominated
atmospheres toward more oxidized states by enhancing
CO2 photolysis relative to H2O and reduced gases (S. D.
Domagal-Goldman et al. 2014; J. L. Grenfell et al. 2014;
C. E. Harman et al. 2015; T. Kozakis et al. 2022; F.
Tian et al. 2014). Coupled atmosphere-biogeochemistry
models have likewise found that M dwarf planets can
be easier to oxygenate than their counterparts orbit-
ing Sun-like stars for a given net primary productivity
because their UV environments favor net oxidation (S.
Gebauer et al. 2018). In this context, TRAPPIST-1 is
an end-member: depending on which UV reconstruc-
tion is adopted, the system may occupy either a regime
that favors strong photochemical oxidation and abiotic
O2/O3 buildup or a more weakly oxidizing regime. The
present uncertainty in TRAPPIST-1’s true FUV-NUV
spectrum therefore underpins the plausibility of both
the abiotic false positives and the non-photosynthetic,
oxygen-rich biospheres recovered in our Archean-analog
models.
4.2.Atmospheric Oxygenation
Our biotic runs, which include enhanced CO, H 2,
and O2 deposition velocities to represent CO-, H 2-, and
O2-consuming metabolisms, can produce percent-level
O2 surface mixing ratios together with substantial O 3
columns. This result closely parallels the coupled one-
dimensional atmosphere-ocean-ecosystem simulations of
J. K. Eager-Nash et al. (2024), who showed that CO-
consuming biospheres on TRAPPIST-1 e can oxygenate
an atmosphere by suppressing atmospheric CO and
thereby weakening major oxygen-loss pathways. In our
framework, we recover the same qualitative oxygenation
mechanism but additionally show that the magnitude
of the associated O 3 buildup, and thus the strength of
the resulting O3 spectral signature, depends strongly on
the adopted stellar spectrum. As a result, similar biotic
O2 states can map onto different O3 outcomes across our
SED suite, and in some cases, those biotic oxidant states
overlap with purely abiotic, stellar-UV-driven solutions,
particularly for Mega-MUSCLES SEM.
A key implication is that atmospheric oxygenation
is fundamentally a statement about net redox balance,
rather than oxygenic photosynthesis per se. On Earth,
the rise of O 2 reflects the long-term imbalance between
organic matter production and oxidative recycling, with
burial of reduced carbon providing the net source of at-
mospheric O2 (T. W. Lyons et al. 2014; D. C. Catling &
J. F. Kasting 2017b). Stoichiometrically, both oxygenic
photosynthesis followed by organic carbon burial and
anoxygenic metabolisms that consume CO to produce
organic matter while exporting reduced carbon can, in
principle, drive the atmosphere toward higher O 2. Our
model does not include an explicit time-dependent car-
bon cycle, but J. K. Eager-Nash et al. (2024) already
took an important step in this direction with a cou-
pled atmosphere-ocean-ecosystem framework that in-
cludes redox balance and organic carbon burial. Extend-
ing such coupled models, and connecting them more ex-
plicitly to broader M dwarf atmosphere-biogeochemical
frameworks such as in S. Gebauer et al. (2018), remains
an important next step.
More broadly, the mechanism identified here is not
unique to TRAPPIST-1. Any system in which anoxy-
genic metabolisms efficiently reduce carbon and export
or bury that carbon could, in principle, sustain an O 2-
O3-rich atmosphere without oxygenic photosynthesis.

4.3.Observational Implications
Our synthetic transmission spectra show that these
photochemical differences map directly onto observ-
ables. Across all SEDs, the input stellar spectrum al-
ters the modeled photochemistry and, in turn, the re-
sulting transmission features. IncreasedF CH4 generally
produces larger atmospheric CH4 abundances and there-
fore stronger CH4 absorption bands in both the NIRSpec
(0.6µm to 5.3µm) and MIRI (5µm to 12µm) wavelength
ranges. At fixedF CH4, band depths depend strongly
on the adopted stellar SED, because lower NUV fluxes
suppress HOx production from H2O photolysis, thereby
extending the atmospheric lifetime of CH 4. The same
SED-dependent photochemistry also affects the observ-
ability of CO in the NIRSpec range and O 3 near 9.7µm
in the MIRI range. Strong CO bands co-occurring with
CH4 features indicate slow CO oxidation and are char-
acteristic of some of our lifeless cases, particularly those
that accumulate spectrally discernible O 3. In contrast,
enhanced microbially-mediated surface uptake of CO
suppresses the CO spectral features while permitting O2
and O 3 to build up, yielding spectra in which strong
CH4 and O 3 features coexist despite low CO. Across
our model scenarios, CO therefore retains its value as an
anti-biosignature: lifeless cases show strong CO bands,
which become especially prominent at the highest CH 4
fluxes, whereas biotic cases show muted CO bands at
comparable CH4 levels (though we note that assuming
maximum aqueous biotic deposition rates may be op-
timistic, and ambiguous high-CO cases with life could
arise (e.g., E. W. Schwieterman et al. 2019)).
In practice, separating these scenarios will be chal-
lenging with JWST alone. Detecting O 3 in transmission
around ultracool M dwarfs is difficult even under opti-
mistic noise assumptions, and both stellar contamina-
tion and instrument systematics can further mute small
features. Thus, as previous studies have emphasized,
both abiotic O 2/O3 production pathways and the mod-
est signal amplitudes expected for realistic TRAPPIST-
1 e observations complicate robust O3 detection and in-
terpretation with JWST (V. S. Meadows 2017; V. S.
Meadows et al. 2018, 2023). Our spectra should there-
fore be viewed as optimistic, noise-free upper bounds.
We do not present a formal noise analysis here and
therefore do not quantify retrieval significance on an
instrument-by-instrument basis. Many of the spectral
differences identified in this work would likely require
numerous transits to detect with confidence, especially
for weaker CO and O 3 features. In real data, many
of the atmospheric degeneracies we identify are likely
to be only partially resolved with current capabilities.
We likely require future platforms such as those like the
proposed Origins Space Telescope (C. Battersby et al.
2018; M. Meixner et al. 2019; D. T. Leisawitz et al. 2021)
or yet more advanced instrumentation to better resolve
these spectral differences.
4.4.Model Limitations
Our results come from a one-dimensional, steady-state
photochemical model with prescribed lower-boundary
fluxes and a fixed surface pressure. These assumptions
are appropriate for isolating the effects of stellar spec-
tral uncertainty, but they also introduce important lim-
itations. In particular, this study is designed to test
the sensitivity of Archean-analog TRAPPIST-1 e pho-
tochemistry to different plausible stellar UV spectra,
rather than to provide a fully coupled treatment of cli-
mate, biogeochemistry, interior exchange, and spatial
transport.
These limitations are relevant for tidally locked plan-
ets such as TRAPPIST-1 e, where three-dimensional
circulation, spatially heterogeneous photolysis, and
day-night transport could alter the relationship be-
tween local chemical production and disk-integrated
observables. In addition, our lower-boundary con-
ditions prescribe biological and abiotic surface sinks
rather than calculating them from an explicit ocean-
atmosphere-ecosystem model. Coupled atmosphere-
ocean-ecosystem and atmosphere-biogeochemical mod-
els have already shown that redox balance, biological
boundary conditions, and M dwarf spectral forcing can
strongly modulate atmospheric O2 production, loss, and
net oxygenation (S. Gebauer et al. 2018; J. K. Eager-
Nash et al. 2024). Our results should therefore be inter-
preted as controlled sensitivity experiments that isolate
the consequences of stellar UV uncertainty, rather than
as complete predictions of any single TRAPPIST-1 e
atmospheric state.
5.CONCLUSION
Our results indicate that biosignature interpretation
for Archean-analog atmospheres around TRAPPIST-1 e
depends strongly on the adopted stellar UV SED. Across
otherwise identical planetary boundary conditions, dif-
ferent plausible TRAPPIST-1 UV reconstructions pro-
duce large differences in atmospheric CH4, CO, O2, and
O3 abundances. These differences map directly onto
synthetic transmission spectra. In particular, some UV
prescriptions of TRAPPIST-1 allow CH 4 and O3 to co-
exist in atmospheres without oxygenic photosynthesis,
including a case where O 3 is produced through abiotic

14
CO2 photolysis and inefficient radical-driven loss while
CH4 accumulates from fluxes consistent with volcanic
outgassing. This creates a potential CH 4-O3 false pos-
itive for life if the stellar UV environment is not suffi-
ciently well constrained.
In this regime, CO becomes one of the most important
contextual gases. In our models, abundant CO indi-
cates abiotic oxidant accumulation driven by CO 2 pho-
tolysis and inefficient surface removal, whereas muted
CO at comparable CH4 and O3 levels is more consistent
with efficient biological CO consumption. CO therefore
remains a critical anti-biosignature, when remotely de-
tectable, for distinguishing lifeless high CO atmospheres
from biologically mediated oxidized states (Y. Wang
et al. 2016; E. W. Schwieterman et al. 2016; Y. Watan-
abe & K. Ozaki 2024; N. F. Wogan & D. C. Catling
2020). (However, a more subtle point is that inefficient
biological CO consumption, potentially coupled with di-
rect and indirect biological CO production, could mimic
abiotic CO accumulation within the atmosphere, result-
ing in ambiguous scenarios and potential false negatives
for life (E. W. Schwieterman et al. 2019; V. Molina et al.
2026; Z. Zhan et al. 2022).) More broadly, our results
reinforce that biosignature gases must be interpreted
within the broader context of the atmosphere-surface-
stellar system rather than as isolated pairwise detec-
tions.
A central implication of this work is that stellar UV
characterization is a prerequisite for accurately model-
ing temperate planets orbiting ultracool M dwarf stars.
Better empirical constraints on the FUV and NUV
spectra of TRAPPIST-1 and similar stars are needed
to determine which photochemical regimes are plausi-
ble for observed planets. Future coupled photochemi-
cal, climate, interior-exchange, and atmosphere-ocean-
ecosystem models will also be needed to better connect
stellar UV forcing to potentially observable atmospheric
spectra. Extending this spectral-sensitivity framework
to other nearby habitable-zone M dwarf planets, includ-
ing Teegarden’s Star b and c, LHS 1140 b, and Proxima
Centauri b, would further clarify how stellar-spectrum
uncertainty shapes atmospheric interpretation beyond
the TRAPPIST-1 system. Such work should also as-
sess the impact of stellar variability, including flares,
alongside sensitivity to continuing laboratory updates
to photolysis cross sections and reaction rates (A. Se-
gura et al. 2010; A. Miranda-Rosete et al. 2025). For
Archean-analog planets around the coolest M dwarfs,
the strongest biosignature interpretations will therefore
come not from any single gas or gas pair but from the
combined context of the stellar UV environment and de-
tailed atmospheric characterization, including discern-
ing the presence of atmospheric disequilibria and poten-
tial anti-biosignature gases.

A.NITRIC OXIDE-MEDIATED OZONE LOSS
NOx chemistry is a potentially important control on
oxidant buildup in CO 2-rich atmospheres because NO
can participate in catalytic cycles that promote the re-
combination of CO and free oxygen. Previous work
has shown that lightning-produced NO can suppress the

15
photochemical buildup of O 2 and O 3 in high FUV en-
vironments (C. E. Harman et al. 2018). P. Barth et al.
(2024) suggest that for TRAPPIST-1, substantially en-
hanced lightning activity may be required to eliminate
abiotic ozone features, but they considered only a single
TRAPPIST-1 spectral input constructed as the median
of the three models from S. Peacock et al. (2019).
Figure 6 shows that the NO + O3 loss pathway is gen-
erally a minor contributor to total atmospheric O 3 loss
in most runs with abiotic deposition velocities. Its con-
tribution increases in several of the more oxidized bi-
otic cases, particularly at low and intermediate methane
fluxes. This behavior is consistent with the elevated
HNO3 concentrations seen in Figure 2 and indicates that
NOx chemistry matters most under specific stellar SEDs
and oxidizing states. At the highest methane fluxes,
however, little O 3 remains because strong CH 4 input
suppresses oxidant buildup, limiting the importance of
the NO + O3 sink.
B.FUV/NUV TRENDS IN ATMOSPHERIC
COLUMN DENSITIES
Figure 7 shows the atmospheric column densities of
CH4, CO, O 2, and O 3 as a function of the stellar
FUV/NUV ratio, using the same model suite and plot-
ting convention as Figure 5. This comparison tests
whether the trends inferred from surface volume mix-
ing ratios persist when the full vertical atmospheric col-
umn is considered. The broad behavior is similar to the
surface-abundance comparison: high-FUV/NUV cases
with abiotic deposition velocities produce among the
largest O2 and O3 columns, while cases with biotic depo-
sition velocities show much smaller CO columns because
of enhanced surface uptake. The biotic cases also pro-
duce large O 3 column ranges for several SEDs, under-
scoring that column-integrated oxidant signatures de-
pend on both the stellar spectrum and lower-boundary
redox conditions. The column-depth view is especially
relevant for interpreting the synthetic transmission spec-
tra, since spectral feature strengths depend on the inte-
grated abundance and vertical distribution of absorbers
rather than on surface mixing ratios alone.
