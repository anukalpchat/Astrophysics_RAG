ABSTRACT
We study the persistent black hole X-ray binary Cyg X-1, recently observed by XRISM Resolve and simul-
taneously by NICER and NuSTAR in its hard spectral state. We confirm the result of Draghis et al. that fits of
the Resolve data alone with the simplest available relativistic reflection model,relxill, yield a black hole spin
parameter close to the maximum,a ∗ ≈0.99. However, fitting with an improved, Comptonization-based model,
relxillCp, yields a lowa ∗ =0.0 +0.17. A similarly low range is obtained with another Comptonization-based
model,reflkerrD. Then, fits to the combined data require two Comptonization models but are consistent with
any spin value. We conclude that the spin value of Cyg X-1 is strongly model-dependent. However, low spin
values are consistent with the constraints from gravitational waves. All of the models constrain the inner disk
radius to be≲10 gravitational radii, which is consistent with a recent finding of the weakness of thermal re-
verberation in Cyg X-1. The implied source geometry is that of an outflowing disk corona, which was also
proposed to explain the X-ray polarization observed from this source.
1.INTRODUCTION
The archetypal black hole (BH) X-ray binary (XRB), Cyg
X-1, was discovered as an X-ray source in 1964 (Bowyer
et al. 1965), i.e., more than 60 years ago, and is the best-
studied BH XRB to date. Nevertheless, substantial uncer-
tainties in its parameters persist. In particular, according to
Miller-Jones et al. (2021), the binary inclination of Cyg X-1
isi=27. ◦5+0.8
−0.6, the BH mass isM=21.2±2.2M ⊙, and the dis-
tance to the source isD=2.22 +0.18
−0.17 kpc (which we give here
as the median values with 68% uncertainties). By contrast,
a recent investigation by Ramachandran et al. (2025) using
detailed atmospheric non-LTE models of the donor found a
significantly lower BH mass,M≈(12.7–17.8)M ⊙. When
they used the rotational broadening measurement of Simón-
Díaz et al. (2017), they obtainedi≈34 ◦ andM≈14M ⊙ (see
also figure 7 in Ramachandran et al. 2025).
Another uncertainty concerns the value of the BH spin pa-
rameter,a ∗. Cyg X-1 has been measured to have very high
spin in its soft spectral state (see Done et al. 2007 for a
spectral state definition) using the X-ray continuum method
(McClintock et al. 2014), e.g.,a ∗ >0.9985 (Zhao et al.
2021; Miller-Jones et al. 2021), 0.99964+0.00003
−0.00007 (Steiner et al.
2024). Recently, Draghis et al. (2025b), hereafter D25, used
the X-ray reflection method (Bambi et al. 2021 and refer-
ences therein) to analyze a XRISM (Tashiro et al. 2021) ob-
servation of Cyg X-1 in the hard spectral state, accompanied

by a NuSTAR observation (Harrison et al. 2013). They also
obtained a high spin,a ∗ ≈0.98.
However, Cyg X-1 is a high-mass X-ray binary (HMXB)
with an OB supergiant donor (Walborn 1973). As such, it
belongs to the class of potential progenitors of BH merg-
ers (Ramachandran et al. 2025). The spins of merging BHs
measured by gravitational waves are generally low; the mod-
eled distribution of individual spins peaks ata ∗ ≈0.01–0.23,
and∼90% havea ∗ ≲0.57 (The LIGO Scientific Collabo-
ration et al. 2025). Furthermore, when comparing merger
results with XRBs, only the spins of the first-born BHs are
of interest. They are plausibly identified with the slower-
spinning premerger BHs, while the faster-spinning ones un-
dergo tidal spin-up (Qin et al. 2018; Mandel & Fragos 2020;
Ma & Fuller 2023; Olejak & Belczy´nski 2021). Some higher
spins measured in mergers appear to originate from dynami-
cal interactions (Tong et al. 2025; Antonini et al. 2025), with
accretion appearing ineffective at spinning up BHs (van Son
et al. 2020). The consensus from those studies is that na-
tal BH spins are low. This finding aligns with stellar models
that predict the core remains coupled to the outer envelope
during expansion (upon leaving the main sequence), i.e., an-
gular momentum transport is efficient (Spruit 2002; Fuller &
Ma 2019; Belczy ´nski et al. 2020). Those models yield natal
spins ofa ∗ ∼0.01–0.1.
For Cyg X-1, evolutionary calculations indicate that only
hyper-Eddington conservative accretion, at ˙M∼10 −2M⊙/yr,
could spin up its BH toa ∗ >0.98 (Qin et al. 2022). Those
authors invoked the production of neutrino pairs in the ac-
cretion flow to remove excess angular momentum, thereby

enabling the required mass accretion rate onto the BH. How-
ever, the density of such a flow remains orders of magnitude
below that required for neutrino pair production, as shown
by Zdziarski et al. (2024, 2026). On the other hand, accre-
tion rates onto the BH appear to be approximately limited to
the Eddington rate, with most of the mass transferred from
the donor forming strong winds, e.g., Fragile et al. (2025).
The same tension applies to two other BH HMXBs with
measured spins, LMC X-1 and M33 X-7, whose spins were
also found to be high using the continuum method:a ∗ =
0.92+0.05
−0.07 (Gou et al. 2009) anda ∗ =0.84±0.05 (Liu et al.
2008), respectively. It remains unclear how to reconcile the
gravitational wave results with those from BH HMXBs, a
problem reviewed by Zdziarski et al. (2026).
An important caveat for the continuum method is that the
theoretical models of accretion disks used in it (Novikov
& Thorne 1973; Abramowicz et al. 1988; S˛ adowski 2009)
predict that disks are unstable in the radiation-pressure-
dominated regime (Lightman & Eardley 1974; Shakura &
Sunyaev 1976). This approximately corresponds to the range
of Eddington ratios observed in the soft states of BH XRBs.
However, observations show that the soft state is stable in
X-rays (Gierli ´nski & Done 2004). Disks can be stabilized
by large-scale magnetic fields, but this substantially alters
the structure of the accretion flows, particularly by increas-
ing dissipation in the upper layers of the disk (see Zdziarski
et al. 2026 and references therein). This effect has been phe-
nomenologically modeled by adding a warm corona on top of
the disk (Belczy´nski et al. 2024; Zdziarski et al. 2024). This
modeling has resulted in fits to the Cyg X-1 spectra with very
low spin values,a ∗ ≈0. Additionally, the disk’s inner edge
can move downstream of the innermost stable circular orbit
(ISCO), which would also reduce the fitteda ∗.
There are also caveats regarding the other main method of
spin measurement, which is based on X-ray reflection. For
this method to be applicable, the disk must extend down to
the ISCO, or very close to it. The space-time metric becomes
weakly dependent on spin at≳10R g, so if the disk is sub-
stantially truncated, spin cannot be measured. The method
has been applied mostly in the hard spectral state, for which
there are strong arguments for truncation (see, e.g., Done
et al. 2007). While studies by Duro et al. (2011), Fabian et al.
(2012), and D25 show disks extending close to the ISCO of
a maximally rotating BH, those by Makishima et al. (2008),
Yamada et al. (2013), and Basak et al. (2017) show substan-
tial disk truncation in Cyg X-1. A related important issue
concerns the shape of the incident spectrum, which likely
consists of multiple spectral components and thus strongly
affects the measurements (see, e.g., Zdziarski et al. (2021)).
Here, we revisit the Cyg X-1 data set associated with the
XRISM observation. We spectrally fit the XRISM spectrum,
as well as the NICER (Gendreau et al. 2016) and NuSTAR
(Harrison et al. 2013) spectra from observations conducted
during the XRISM observation.

We have calculated the fractional variability, rms(E), as a
function of photon energy over the frequency range 0.01–
1 Hz using theStingraypackage (Huppenkothen et al.
2019a,b). For the NuSTAR data, we have used the co-
spectrum computed with theHENDRICSpackage (Bachetti
et al. 2015). However, we have found that the NuSTAR data
show significantly lower variability than NICER and Resolve
in the overlapping 3–10 keV energy range. This appears to be
due to dead time in the NuSTAR data, which artificially low-
ers the rms (even when the co-spectrum is used; Bachetti &
Huppenkothen 2018; M. Bachetti, private communication).
On the other hand, the NICER and Resolve rms values show
only minor differences in the Fe K line energy band, likely
arising from their different instrumental responses10. Alter-
natively, the discrepancy may reflect intrinsic source non-
stationarity combined with the XRISM observation’s longer
temporal coverage.
The results, excluding NuSTAR, are shown in Figure 3.
We see the rms increase up to about 1.2 keV , then decrease.
Similar results for another NICER observation are shown
by Basak et al. (2025). They calculate the rms separately
for four frequency bands and show that the hump around
1.2 keV and the decline of the rms above it are most pro-
nounced at frequencies<0.5 Hz. This behavior is consistent
with the findings of Wilkinson & Uttley (2009) for two other
BH XRBs, which they interpreted as long-term variability of
the coronal emission driven by disk variability (see also Ut-
tley et al. 2011). In that process, variable disk seed photons
enter the corona, modulating its cooling and thus the spectral
slope (see, e.g., figure 14 in Zdziarski et al. 2002). However,
the decline of the rms at energies below∼1 keV indicates the
presence of a more stable disk component, which is difficult
to explain in this scenario. For comparison, we show the rms
spectrum of Cyg X-1 in the soft spectral state (Zdziarski et al.
2024). Curiously, there is a similar hump at≈1 keV . Those
authors proposed that it may be caused by photon transmis-
sion through the stellar wind.

account for the absorption using thetbfeomodel inxspec
and assume the elemental abundances of the absorber from
Wilms et al. (2000). In prior work, the column density to-
ward Cyg X-1 was found to beN H ≈(6±2)×10 21 cm−2
based on reddening toward the donor (Balucinska-Church
et al. 1995), and≈4.65–4.85×10 21 cm−2 based on model-

3.2.Broad-band spectra
We now perform spectral fitting of the joint spectrum, in-
cluding NICER, NuSTAR, and XRISM Resolve, over the
0.5–79 keV range. In our approach, we treat blackbody pho-

tion in the corona. This limits our choice of models. Al-
thoughrelxillCpincludes Compton upscattering of black-
body photons, its temperature is fixed at 50 eV (García et al.
2018), which is not applicable to our spectra. We there-
fore use only thereflkerrDmodel, which allows the seed
blackbody temperature to vary freely. We also use its op-
tion in which the seed photons have a disk blackbody spec-
trum (with a maximum temperature ofT bb) from thediskbb
model (Mitsuda et al. 1984).
The soft X-rays emitted by Cyg X-1 are absorbed by both
the ISM and the donor’s stellar wind. The latter was taken
into account by Tomsick et al. (2014) using a table model
based on thexstarionization model Kallman & Bautista
(2001). We tested that model. However, we found that in-
cluding it has virtually no effect on the derived parameters,
and the fit converges at the lowest column density allowed by
this model. Thus, we decided to model absorption only with
tbfeo. However, we allow the O and Fe abundances in that
model to differ from unity.

5.DISCUSSION AND SUMMARY
Our analysis has significantly extended the important work
of D25. While their study covered many details of the Fe
K emission from Cyg X-1, particularly the narrow Fe Kα
features measured for the first time by XRISM Resolve, it
used only a single model for the relativistically broadened X-
ray reflection spectrum,relxill(Dauser et al. 2010; García
et al. 2013). This deviated from the previous practice of those

9
authors (Draghis et al. 2023, 2024, 2025a), who considered
and tested a range of available relativistic reflection mod-
els, particularly the improved, physically motivated model
relxillCp(García et al. 2018).
In our study, we have found a strong dependence of the fit-
ted spin parameter on the model. While therelxillmodel
(García et al. 2013) fitted to the Resolve data alone yields
a spin parameter close to the maximal value,relxillCp
yieldsa ∗ =0 +0.17. Similarly, fitting with another physi-
cally motivated reflection model,reflkerrD(Nied´ zwiecki
et al. 2019), yieldsa ∗ =0.18 +0.10
−0.18. An argument for the
low spin is that the high disk inclination obtained with the
relxill,≳60 ◦, is inconsistent with both the binary in-
clination and most previous spectral-fitting results for Cyg
X-1. By contrast, fitting withrelxillCpandreflkerrD
yieldsi=33 +1
−2
◦ andi=36 +1
−4
◦, respectively, i.e., inclination
ranges compatible with the binary inclination of Ramachan-
dran et al. (2025). As a possible explanation of the high disk
inclination, a precession of the BH spin axis was invoked by
D25. Such precession would then govern the disk inclination
and, in principle, could alter it relative to that reported in pre-
vious studies. However, the actual period of that de Sitter,
spin-orbit, precession is several thousand years, see equation
(14) of Zdziarski et al. (2023).
We then studied the broadband spectra from NICER (0.5–
10 keV), XRISM Resolve (2.6–12 keV), and NuSTAR (9–79
keV), with the Resolve spectrum chosen to dominate the Fe
K range. We found that models consisting of a single plasma
cloud that Compton-upscatters photons from the disk and re-
flection cannot properly account for the observed spectrum.
Instead, models including two Comptonizing regions, their
reflections, and an accretion disk provide a relatively good
description of the broadband data. The geometry can then
be similar to that found to describe the hard state of MAXI
J1820+070 (see figure 4 of Zdziarski et al. 2021). The harder
component is closer to the BH, as evidenced by hard X-ray
lags (Kotov et al. 2001; Basak et al. 2025), but it can also
illuminate outer disk regions.
However, the spin parameter in this model is uncon-
strained,a ∗ =0.18 +0.82
−0.18. By contrast, including the full en-
ergy range of the NuSTAR detector (3–79 keV) in the broad-
band fit yields a low spin,a∗ =0.10 +0.19
−0.10. Thus, the fitted spin
depends on both the model and the data set. Nevertheless, our
results are consistent with low spin and, therefore, with the
spin measurements from gravitational waves (see Section 1).
A notable result from our reflection spectroscopy is that the
disk in Cyg X-1 appears at most weakly truncated, with the
inner disk radiusR in ≲10R g. Thus, the disk either extends
to the ISCO of a slowly rotating BH or is weakly truncated.
By contrast, the normalization of the disk component in our
broadband modeling implies that its inner radius is several
Rg or more. Taken together, these results indicate that the
disk extends down to∼10R g, consistent with the ISCO of
a slowly rotating BH but not with that of a fast-rotating one.
Thus, ifa ∗ ∼1 in Cyg X-1, its disk has to be truncated. The
disk’s proximity to the BH is also consistent with the weak
soft X-ray lags in Cyg X-1, as reported by Basak et al. (2025).
The amplitude of the lag is≈1 ms (see figure 10 in Basak
et al. 2025). If due to reverberation, this corresponds, at face
value, to the light travel time over≈(10–15)Rg, depending on
the BH mass.
At the same time, the corona in the hard state of Cyg X-1
extends horizontally, as indicated by the X-ray polarization
angle aligned with the jet of this source (Krawczynski et al.
2022). Thus, the source geometry is that of a slab corona.
However, static coronae exhibit soft,Γ>2, X-ray spec-
tra due to feedback between the disk and corona (Haardt &
Maraschi 1991; Poutanen et al. 2018), whereas we observe
a hard spectrum withΓ∼1.7 (see Table 2). This discrep-
ancy can be resolved if the corona is outflowing at a mildly
relativistic velocity (Beloborodov 1999). Our results are con-
sistent with the outflowing corona model of Poutanen et al.
(2023) in the variant where the underlying disk extends near
the ISCO. The reflection fractions in our models are signifi-
cantly less than unity due to relativistic beaming away from
the disk. Additionally, our finding that the inner parts of the
disk are only weakly irradiated,∝R −q1 withq 1 ≈0 +0.8 (see
Table 3), is consistent with a mildly relativistic outflow of
the corona, which then reduces the irradiation of regions be-
low the corona’s edges. In addition, the model of Poutanen
et al. (2023) explains the high degree of linear polarization in
X-rays, 4.0%±0.2%, measured in the hard state of Cyg X-1
(Krawczynski et al. 2022).
On the other hand, Cyg X-1 is the only source found so far
in which the soft X-ray (thermal) reverberation is very weak
(Basak et al. 2025). Those authors contrast this source with
the transient XRB MAXI J1820+070, which exhibits pro-
nounced soft X-ray lags and has a geometry consistent with
disk truncation (De Marco et al. 2021; Dziełak et al. 2021;
Zdziarski et al. 2021). Soft X-ray lags were also observed in
GX 339–4 (De Marco et al. 2015, 2017) and H1743–322 (De
Marco & Ponti 2016).
To explain this difference, Basak et al. (2025) noted that
the relatively low luminosity of Cyg X-1 places it on the
lower branch of the hardness-luminosity diagram (soft-to-
hard transition for transients), whereas most evidence for
disk truncation lies on the upper branch. However, reverber-
ation lags on the lower branch of GX 339–4 were, on aver-
age, longer than those on the upper (hard-to-soft) branch (De
Marco et al. 2017). We propose that the specific geometry of
Cyg X-1 may be related to its accretion mode from the stellar
wind. This may continuously replenish the inner parts of the
accretion flow, thereby preventing truncation.
We also note that the low disk temperature we obtained,
0.15±0.01 keV , implies that disk emission in the 0.5–1 keV
range (used to measure the soft lags by Basak et al. 2025) ac-
counts for only≈1/3 of the total absorbed flux in that range
(using the model with a disk and tworeflkerrDcompo-
nents, which yields nearly the same fraction whether in pho-
tons or energy). Therefore, the intrinsic lag is significantly
diluted; see figure 20 in Uttley et al. (2014).
