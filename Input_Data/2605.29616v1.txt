ABSTRACT
We present a comprehensive study of an infrared (IR) flare in the star-forming galaxy SDSS
J010320.39+140152.5, which is selected from the sample of mid-IR (MIR) outbursts in nearby galaxies
(MIRONG). Its MIR luminosity rose rapidly to a peak of∼5.4×10 43 erg s −1, maintained in the high state
for about a year, and decreased continuously afterward. No optical variability was detected throughout the IR
flare. Near-IR follow-up observations around the peak pinpointed the flare’s location to spatially coincide with
the galactic nucleus, with a 3σupper limit of the offset of≲100 pc. The IR spectral energy distribution (SED) of
the flare is consistent with thermal emission of dust with temperatures of∼900 K. Using a dust radiative transfer
model, we inferred a peak UV luminosity of∼(4−10)×10 44 erg s−1 and a total energy of∼(0.9−2)×10 52 ergs
released. We ruled out the possibility of a supernova, and prefer that the IR flare originated from an obscured
tidal disruption event (TDE) rather than a changing-look active galactic nucleus (AGN). This flare stands as one
of the most compelling cases to date for the emerging class of dust-obscured TDEs in recent years. They are
missed by optical surveys, partly accounting for the observed bias in TDE host galaxies, and represent a crucial,
yet often overlooked, component for a complete understanding of the TDE population.
Keywords:black hole physics — galaxies: nuclei — infrared: galaxies
1.INTRODUCTION
Tidal disruption events (TDEs) occur when stars come too
close to supermassive black holes (SMBHs) and are tidally
disrupted Hills (1975). About half of the stellar debris falls
back to the central SMBH, producing a flare that peaks at
the UV to soft X-ray band (Rees 1988; Phinney 1989). The
first TDE was detected in the X-ray band (Bade et al. 1996)
with theROSATtelescope, while optical time-domain sur-
veys have dominated the discovery of TDEs (van Velzen et
al. 2020, 2021; Gezari 2021) in recent years. The total num-
ber of TDEs has exceeded∼200.
Statistically, optical TDEs display a puzzling distribution
across different types of host galaxies: they are overrepre-
sented in post-starburst galaxies (Arcavi et al. 2014; French
et al. 2016, 2020), which experience a violent starburst
episode approximately 109 yrs previously (Dressler & Gunn
1983), but are almost absent in normal star-forming galax-
ies. Scenarios that may lead to the rate enhancement have
been proposed (see the review in French et al. 2020), such
as SMBH binaries (Chen et al. 2009; Coughlin et al. 2019),
central stellar over-density (French et al. 2020; Stone et al.
2016), and velocity anisotropy (Stone et al. 2018). How-
ever, these explanations cannot address why TDEs are rare
in galaxies with current intense star formation.
Star-forming galaxies are long known to have a large
amount of dust, which may obscure the TDE occurring in
their nuclei. Fortunately, the nuclear dust can reprocess the
UV radiation it absorbs into IR reradiation, hence forming
an echo. Such IR echoes have been observed Jiang et al.
(2016); van Velzen et al. (2016); Dou et al. (2016) by the
IR time-domain survey Wide-field Infrared Survey Explorer
(WISE; Wright et al. 2010; Mainzer et al. 2014). IR echo pro-
vides a new probe to detect obscured TDEs, and the first case
is Arp 299-B AT1 (Mattila et al. 2018), whose TDE nature
was confirmed with the help of a transient radio jet. Subse-

quent studies have identified dozens of TDE candidates in
star-forming and starburst galaxies using IR surveys (e.g.,
Kool et al. 2020; Jiang et al. 2021a; Reynolds et al. 2022;
Masterson et al. 2024). Unfortunately, the nature of the vast
majority of these TDE candidates still lacks clear validation.
Most IR flares lack optical counterparts (Jiang et al. 2021a;
Masterson et al. 2024). Thus, the TDE rate in star-forming
galaxies would be greatly underestimated if the majority of
IR flares are indeed TDEs. This suggests that the rare op-
tical detection of TDEs in star-forming galaxies could be a
selection bias due to dust obscuration. However, to prove
this point, it is necessary to verify the TDE nature of these IR
flares. Moreover, a tool that can infer the UV peak luminos-
ity based on the IR data is required to compare the luminosity
functions of optical TDEs and IR TDEs.
In this paper, we present the multi-band analysis of an
IR TDE candidate in a nearby spiral star-forming galaxy
SDSS J010320.39+140152.5 (RA = 01:03:20.39, DEC =
+14:01:52.5, hereafter J0103+1401) atz= 0.04181. The tran-
sient was first reported as an IR transient in the sample of
MIR Outbursts in Nearby Galaxies (MIRONG; Jiang et al.
2021a), and was not alerted by any optical survey. We de-
scribe the public data and our follow-up observations in Sec-
tion 2, present the analysis of multi-bands photometric and
spectroscopic data in Section 3, and describe a dust echo
model in Section 4, and discuss the nature of the IR transient
and the implications in Section 5, and finally summarize in
Section 6. Throughout this paper, we assumed aΛCDM cos-
mology withΩ M = 0.3,Ω Λ = 0.7, andH 0 = 70 km s−1 Mpc−1.
2.DATA AND OBSERV ATIONS
2.1.MIR discovery
J0103+1401 was discovered using data from the all-sky
surveyWISEand its successor Near-Earth Object Wide-field
Infrared Survey Explorer (NEOWISE). The originalWISE
survey has mapped the full sky every∼180 days, working
in 4 bands (W1, W2, W3, W4) centered at 3.4, 4.6, 12, 22
µm since Jan 2010 until its hydrogen cryostat was exhausted.
The survey was extended by an additional 4 months as NEO-
WISE Post-Cryogenic Mission operated only in W1 and W2
bands. Following a 33-month hibernation period, theWISE
instrument recommenced survey operation in Dec 2013, and
this post-hibernation mission is referred to as NEOWISE Re-
action. TheWISEand NEOWISE surveys visit the object
about half a year in W1 and W2 with∼10−20 exposures
per band per visit.
We adopted unWISE coadds (Lang 2014; Meisner et al.
2018) that stack all single exposures during a specific visit
to improve the signal-to-noise ratio (S/N). The unWISE data
up to 2024 has been released, with a total of 25 visits, which
we referred to as epoch 1 to 25, respectively. We measured
the photometry of the whole galaxy from an isophotal K S
fiducial aperture (r_k20f e) obtained in the 2MASS all-sky
source catalog (the same aperture will be applied in other
bands when carrying out the host galaxy fluxes with no fur-
ther explanation). We show the MIR light curves in the W1
and W2 bands in Figure 1. The galaxy remained invariant
until an abrupt rise in 2017.
2.2.Archival optical light curve data
We collected archived optical light curve data of
J0103+1401 as follows.
The ongoing All-Sky Automated Survey for Supernovae
(ASASSN; Shappee et al. 2014) has been observing the
galaxy since 2013. We obtained image subtraction pho-
tometry (reference flux added) in theVandgbands from
ASASSN Sky Patrol (Kochanek et al. 2017), and binned the
light curves monthly with a few to 20 data points per bin for
a deeper sensitivity. As shown in Figure 1, no significant
variation was detected around 2017, when the IR transient
occurred.
To check for possible optical flares over a longer period of
time, we collected data from the Catalina Real-time Transient
Survey (CRTS; Drake et al. 2009), the Zwicky Transient Fa-
cility (ZTF; Masci et al. 2018), and the Asteroid Terrestrial-
impact Last Alert System (ATLAS; Tonry et al. 2018). The
CRTS data from 2005 to 2014 are public, and the ongoing
ZTF and ATLAS surveys provide data starting in∼2018. We
obtained the CRTSV-band light curve from CRTS data re-
lease 3. We downloaded theg- andr-band science images of
ZTF from the Infrared Science Archive (IRSA), and ATLAS
c−ando−band science images from NASA Planetary Data
System (PDS), and measured the aperture flux. We show the
CRTS, ZTF, and ATLAS light curves in Figure 1, which are
also binned monthly. The galaxy showed no significant opti-
cal variability in the past∼20 years.
2.3.Follow-up NIR imaging observations
We triggered follow-up NIR observations of J0103+1401
on Jul 16 (epoch 1) and Nov 22, 2018 (epoch 2) in theJ-,H-,
andK-band with the Wide-field InfraRed Camera (WIRCam)
on the Canada France Hawai’i Telescope (CFHT). For each
epoch, we took three exposures per band, with an exposure
time of a single exposure of 30, 15, and 11 seconds for the
three bands, respectively. We computed astrometric solutions
using a combination ofSExtractor(Bertin & Arnouts
1996) andSCAMP(Bertin 2006). We then resampled and
combined the images to improve the S/N for each band in
each epoch usingSWarp(Bertin et al. 2002).
To remove the host galaxy contribution and obtain the flux
of the flare, we collected NIR images taken in August 2007,
before the IR flare occurred, from the UKIRT Infrared Deep
Sky Survey (UKIDSS; Lawrence et al. 2007) as reference im-
ages. We downloaded the images from the WFCAM Science
Archive.

2.4.Spectroscopic Observations
We obtained a historical optical spectrum of the galaxy ob-
served by Slone Digital Sky Survey (SDSS), which was taken
on Sept 24, 2000, before the IR flare occurred.
After the IR flare occurred, we took two new optical spec-
tra with the Double Spectrograph (DBSP) mounted on Hale
5m (P200) telescope at Palomar Observatory on Dec 16,
2017 and Sept 2, 2021. The first was taken around the peak
of the MIR flare, and the second was taken during the fad-
ing stage, with an exposure time of 900 and 1600 seconds,
respectively. For both spectra, we adopted a D55 dichroic,
a blue grating of 600 lines per mm blazed at 3780 Å, and
a red grating of 316 lines per mm blazed at 7150 Å, result-
ing in a continuous wavelength coverage of 3100−10500 Å.
We used slit widths of 1. ′′5 and 2. ′′0, respectively, based
on the weather conditions. The spectral resolutions are
7.84/10.46 Å in the red channel and 4.04/5.32 Å in the blue
channel. We reduced the DBSP data according to the stan-
dard reduction procedures for a long-slit spectrum. The flux
calibration was made using a standard star’s spectrum taken
on the same night.
2.5.X-ray Observations
To check for any X-ray emission associated with the MIR
flare, we triggered twoSwi f t(Burrows et al. 2005) observa-
tions on Jun 5, 2019 and Dec 16, 2021 with net exposure
times of 1666 and 4031 seconds, respectively. We repro-
cessed the XRT event files in Photon Counting mode with
the taskxrtpipeline. We extracted source photons from
a circle centered at the optical nucleus with a radius of 36 ′′
to avoid covering a companion galaxy, and extracted back-
ground photons from a source-free annulus region with inner
and outer radii of 40 and 60 pixels, respectively.
Neither observation yielded a significant detection (≲1σ).
We stacked two observations, and obtained a 3σupper limit
of the net count rate in the 0.3−10 keV band of 1.7×10−3 s−1.
Assuming a power-law spectrum with a photon index ofΓ= 2
with a galactic absorption of N H = 3.55×10 20 cm−2 (HI4PI
Collaboration et al. 2016), we estimated withPIMMSa 3σ
upper limit for the unabsorbed flux at 0.3−10 keV to be 6×
10−14 erg s −1 cm−2, corresponding to a luminosity of 2.4×
1041 erg s−1.

3.DATA ANALYSIS
3.1.Occurrence time and position of the flare
With the MIR light curves obtained from photometry of the
entire galaxy (Figure 1), it is clear that epoch 11 and the sub-
sequent epochs were all in the flare state, while it is not clear
whether epoch 10 is in the flare state or not because of the
contamination from the host galaxy. To obtain a more accu-
rate flux of the flare, we made image subtraction for epoch 10
and later epochs, using the median stack of images at epochs
from 1 to 9 as the reference images. The image subtraction
was made usingHOTPANTS(Becker 2015). We present ex-
amples of image subtraction results in Figure 2, where a point
source is clearly shown on the difference image.
We then performed point spread function (PSF) photome-
try on the host-subtracted images. In addition to the statisti-
cal error of the photometry, we also evaluated the systematic
error caused by the galaxy background in the image subtrac-
tion process. Taking epoch 10 as an example, we performed
PSF photometry on difference images of 10−1, 10−2, ...,
and 10−9, and adopted the standard deviation of the 9 fluxes
as the systematic error.
The flare was significantly detected at epoch 10 (≳10σ),
and thus we adopted the time of epoch 10, MJD = 57748.7, as
the occurrence time of the flare. In addition, the photomet-
ric results were used for the subsequent SED and variation
analysis of the flare.
We also made image subtraction on CFHT images in
NIR using UKIDSS images as references. During this
process, thekernelmatching the effective PSFs (ePSFs)
of CFHT and UKIDSS (ePSF ukidss = ePSF cfht ⊗kernel)
could not be accurately calculated using the default algo-
rithm ofHOTPANTS, possibly because the overlap regions
of UKIDSS and CFHT images are small and the number of
stars for matching the ePSF is not sufficient. Thus, we manu-
ally built ePSFs from nearby stars without restricting the use
of overlapping stars, and calculated thekernel, both using
algorithms inPhotutils(Bradley et al. 2025). The flare
was detected significantly in theKandHbands with>25σ,
while only barely detected in theJband with a fewσ.
We measured the positions of the flare and the nucleus of
the host galaxy by fitting the difference and reference images
with a 2-dimensional Gaussian, respectively, and then calcu-
lated the offset between them. For NIR bands, we adopted
the positions and offset measured from theKband with the
best S/N. The uncertainty is dominated by the systematic er-
ror of the astrometric calibration, and we estimated it from
the offsets of nearby stars between UKIDSS and CFHT im-
ages after correcting for the stars’ proper motions (Gaia Col-
laboration et al. 2018). The position of the flare spatially
coincides with the galaxy nucleus because we measured an
offset of∼0. ′′041±0. ′′038, corresponding to a projected
distance of 37±34 pc in physical. The flare’s position mea-
sured in the MIR W2 band also agrees with the NIR result,
with an offset of∼0. ′′12±0. ′′08.

The SED that matches blackbody curves, the high lumi-
nosity of several 1043 erg s−1, and the slow variation rule out
the synchrotron radiation of a relativistic jet. Since the black-
body temperature is low with∼700−1000 K, we considered
dust thermal emission as the origin of the IR flare. The en-
ergy budget of several 1051 erg requires the dust to be heated
by an energetic transient source, with possible natures being
SN, TDE or changing-look AGN (CLAGN). We will further
discuss the possible natures in Section 5.
3.3.Optical spectra
We show the pre-flare SDSS spectrum and the two post-
flare DBSP spectra (observed at phases of 355 and 1711
days) in Figure 4. The three spectra resemble each other:
they are all dominated by starlight with strong narrow emis-
sion lines (NEL) detected. We detected no new emission
lines in the post-flare spectra.
We fitted the three spectra with models consisting of a stel-
lar component and NEL components usingPPXFusing a
Maximum Penalized Likelihood approach (Cappellari et al.
2004). The model fit the data well for all three spectra with
no additional power-law component required. We present
starlight-subtracted spectra in the [N II]+Hαregion and the
best-fitting NEL models in Figure 4. No broad emission line
component was required.
We classified the host galaxy of J0103+1401 using the
NEL ratios following Kewley et al. (2001, 2006) and Kauff-
mann et al. (2003). As can be seen in Figure 5, the NEL ratios
on the pre-flare spectrum fall into the star-forming regime,
while those on the two post-flare spectra fall into the com-
posite regime due to slightly higher [N II]/Hαratios. This
difference might be caused by the aperture variation of the
spectroscopic observations, or by the enhancement of [N II]
emission line caused by the IR flare. In either case, the NELs,

4.DUST ECHO MODEL
According to the analysis in Section 3.2, the IR flare in
J0103+1401 originates from dust heated by a transient event
with a total energy of>5×10 51 erg. There are two pos-
sible ways for dust to be heated. One is to be heated by the
UV/optical radiation of the transient event, known as IR echo
(Dwek 1983; Graham et al. 1983; Miller et al. 2010; Jiang et
al. 2016; van Velzen et al. 2016). The other is to be heated by
the kinetic energy of SN ejecta/TDE outflow released from
interaction with circum-stellar/inter-stellar media (e.g., Fox
et al. 2011).
We estimated the scale of IR emitting dust (rdust) using two
methods. First,r dust must be no less than the blackbody ra-
dius of 0.05–0.1 pc from the SED analysis. Second, for the
case of IR echo, the primary UV radiation that heats the dust
must be more luminous than IR reradiation, and thus we have
LUV >L IR ∼6×10 43 erg s−1. Assuming a sublimation tem-
perature of 1500 K and a typical dust grain radius of 0.1µm,
theL UV corresponds to a sublimation radius of≳0.1 pc us-
ing the relation of Namekata & Umemura (2016), andr dust
must be even larger. Both methods yield consistent results
thatr dust ≳0.1 pc. If the dust is heated by radiation, the IR
reradiation would be delayed byr dust/c≳0.3 year, consis-
tent with the observed rise time in the MIR band. However,
if heated by ejecta/outflow with a velocity of∼10 4 km s−1,
the delay time would be≳10 years. Such a long time delay
makes it difficult to explain the observed sharp rise and rapid
decay. Therefore, we considered dust echo as the origin of
the IR flare hereafter.

To infer the properties of the primary UV radiation, we fit-
ted the observed IR light curves with the dust radiative trans-
fer model of Lu et al. (2016). The model was designed to
simulate the IR echo of TDEs. However, since the model
can self-consistently calculate the reduction in dust grain ra-
dius caused by sublimation through thermal equilibrium, it
is also applicable to the study of IR echo from other high-
energy transient events, such as SN and changing-look AGN
(CLAGN). The model calculates the IR reradiation of dust
in a spherical shell heated by a transient UV source. The UV
source has a luminosity ofL0, starting att0 and lasting fortflare
(tTDE in Lu et al. 2016). The dust is a mixture of 47% graphite
and 53% silicate, the typical value for the Milky Way’s dust.
The dust grains with an initial radius ofa 0 are uniformly dis-
tributed in the shell with a number density ofn d. The initial
inner radius of the shell isr in and the outer radius isr out. We
fitted the data using a Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC)
approach. We assumed that all parameters have log-uniform
prior probability distributions and thatr out >r in. We added
a factor to represent the systematic error, whose amplitude is
proportional to the model flux. We only fitted the data for the
first 5 years because the later IR emission comes from outer
dust, where the assumption of uniformly distributed dust may
deviate significantly from the actual situation.
As shown in Figure 8, the model fits well with the data,
since it requires a systematic error of only 10% of the model
flux. This systematic error may be due to the simplicity of our
model assumptions. For example, the UV light curve is over-
simplified, the real dust may not be uniformly distributed, or

5.DISCUSSIONS
5.1.Nature of the IR flare
5.1.1.SN?
Although the host galaxy of J0103+1401 has a high star
formation rate of∼5−6 M ⊙ yr−1, predicting a high core-
collapse SN rate, the IR flare is unlikely to be caused by a
supernova for the following reasons.
First, the position of the flare spatially coincides with the
galaxy nucleus, with an offset of<100 pc, while most of
SNe in spiral galaxies were found off-center.
Second, the IR energy of≳5×10 51 exceeds the total ra-
diant energy of the vast majority of SNe of∼10 51 erg. Only
some of super-luminous SNe, a rare type of bright core-
collapse SNe, can release so much radiant energy, such as
SN 2016aps withE tot ∼5×10 51 erg (Nicholl et al. 2020).
However, the host galaxies of super-luminous SNe typically
have small masses of≲10 9.5 M⊙ (Frohmaier et al. 2021)
and low metallicities (Perley et al. 2016), inconsistent with
J0103+1401.
Third, the peak W2 luminosityL W2 of∼4×10 43 erg s−1
far exceeds the maximum MIR luminosity of all known SNe.
Based on a sample study of hundreds of Spitzer SNe (Szalai
et al. 2019), a check of all SNe IIn exploded during 2009
and 2016 withWISEdata (Jiang et al. 2019), and a sample
study of 11 super-luminous SNe before 2018 withWISEdata
(Sun et al. 2022), the most MIR-luminous SNe haveL W2 of
∼(1−2)×10 42 erg s−1, an order of magnitude lower than that
of J0103+1401.
5.1.2.CLAGN?
We found no evidence of AGN in J0103+1401. The optical
spectrum shows neither broad emission lines nor AGN-like
narrow emission lines (Section 3.3). The pre-flare MIR color
of W1−W2 = 0.18 is consistent with inactive galaxies and is
much bluer than AGNs, which have W1−W2>0.8 (Stern et
al. 2012; Yan et al. 2013). In addition, an AGN component is
not required to fit the SED of the host galaxy (Section 3.4).
Although CLAGNs can produce MIR flares, the flares’
amplitudes are generally small, with the majority showing
∆W2<1 mag and a small fraction showing∆W2≈1.5
mag (Lyu et al. 2022), where∆W2 is maximum magnitude
variation in the W2 band. Meanwhile, the variation am-
plitude of J0103+1401 reaches∆W2 = 1.9 mag, exceeding
all known CLAGNs in the sample of Lyu et al. (2022). In
addition, the MIR flares caused by CLAGNs generally rise
slowly with rise times of 1−5 years (Yao et al. 2025), while
J0103+1401 rose in less than half a year. Therefore, the IR
flare in J0103+1401 is unlikely to be caused by CLAGN.
5.1.3.Obscured TDE?
The IR flare in J0103+1401 rose in several months and
remained near the peak for about one year, and these time
scales are similar to MIR-bright optical TDEs, such as AT-
LAS17jrp (Wang et al. 2022b), ASASSN-18ap (Wang et
al. 2024), AT 2020nov (Earl et al. 2025), and AT 2022upj
(Newsome et al. 2024), and are also similar to those of
WTP14adbjsh discovered in the IR band (Panagiotou et
al. 2023). Its W2-band luminosity of 4×10 43 erg s −1 is
slightly higher than MIR bright optical TDEs (LW2 ∼1−2×
1043 erg s −1), while is similar to IR TDE Arp 299-B AT1
(LW2 ∼4×10 43 erg s−1; Mattila et al. 2018). It shows black-
body spectra in 1−5µm with temperature decreased over
time, withT BB ∼900 K near the peak and∼700 K in the
dropping phase, and the spectra and temperature variation is
similar to Arp 299-B AT1 (Mattila et al. 2018). In short,
the IR flare in J0103+1401 shows many similarities to the IR
flares caused by TDEs.
We have inferred the physical parameters of the primary
UV flare based on the dust echo model in Section 4. The peak
luminosity of∼(4−10)×10 44 erg s−1 and the total energy of
∼(0.9−2)×10 52 erg are all within the parameter ranges of
known optical TDEs (Lu et al. 2018; van Velzen et al. 2021;
Yao et al. 2023), although in the high end of the samples,
possibly because of the selection effect of the MIR sample.
The mass of the central SMBH was estimated to be∼1×
107 M⊙ using the host galaxy properties, not exceeding 10 8
M⊙ and ensuring that the TDE can occur outside the event

5.2.Obscured TDEs in star-forming galaxies
Most TDEs’ host galaxies are post-starburst (e.g., French
et al. 2016, 2020; Law-Smith et al. 2017; Hammerstein
et al. 2021) and few are star-forming like J0103+1401.
We collected TDEs occurring in star-forming galax-
ies with SFR>1 in the literature, including SDSS
J095209.56+214313.3 (SDSS J0952+2143 for short, Ko-
mossa et al. 2008, 2009; Palaversa et al. 2016), SDSS
J074820.67+471214.3 (SDSS J0748+4712, Wang et al.
2011), SDSS J015957.64+003310.5 (SDSS J0159+0033,
Merloni et al. 2015), IRAS F01004-2237 (Tadhunter et al.
2017; Dou et al. 2017; Sun et al. 2024), and ASASSN-18ap
(Wang et al. 2024). We list their basic information in Ta-
ble 4. We found that 4 out of the 5 clearly show luminous IR
echoes, while whether SDSS J0159+0033 has an IR echo is
unclear due to a lack of IR data. The high detection rate indi-
cates high dust coverage factors in the nuclei of star-forming
galaxies. This is quite different from TDEs in normal galax-
ies, which show a weak IR echo with a dust covering factor
(defined as the ratio of peak dust luminosity and UV/optical
luminosity) of only∼1% (Jiang et al. 2021b).
The IR echo reflects the rich dust at the pc scale in star-
forming galaxies. Combining with the rich dust on a larger
scale, which has long been known, the fraction of TDEs be-
ing obscured in star-forming galaxies should be much higher
than that in other galaxies. IR-selected samples may di-
rectly test this high fraction. As Wang et al. (2022b) pointed
out, among the 28 MIR flares in star-forming galaxies in the
MIRONG sample, only one (ATLAS17jrp) was detected by
optical surveys. In addition, the 5 MIR flares in dusty star-
burst galaxies in the sample of Reynolds et al. (2022) are
all obscured. The low fraction of optical detection supports
the idea that most TDEs in star-forming galaxies are ob-
scured and missed by optical or X-ray surveys, as the case
of J0103+1401.
In addition, among the four nearby TDEs detected within
50 Mpc, including IGR J12580+0134 (14.5 Mpc; Nikołajuk
& Walter 2013), WTP14adbjsh (42 Mpc; Panagiotou et al.
2023), Arp 299-B AT1 (44.8 Mpc; Mattila et al. 2018), and
AT2023clx (47.8 Mpc; Zhu et al. 2023), two (WTP14adbjsh
and Arp 299-B AT1) are obscured TDEs in star-forming
galaxies. This provides another piece of evidence that a sig-
nificant fraction of TDEs are obscured.
Using 28 MIR flares in star-forming galaxies in the
MIRONG sample and the total number of the parent sam-
ple, we estimated an event rate of∼3×10 −5 gal−1 yr−1 fol-
lowing Jiang et al. (2021a). On one hand, although we have
ruled out flares in star-forming galaxies with broad emission
lines, it is still possible that CLAGN contributes, leading to
an overestimation of the rate. On the other hand, for TDEs in
star-forming galaxies, perhaps only those with high UV lumi-
nosities, like J0103+1401 withL UV ∼(4−10)×10 44 erg s−1,
can cause MIR variability amplitudes exceeding the thresh-
old of 0.5 magnitude of Jiang et al. (2021a) due to the high
MIR background from the host galaxy, leading to an un-
derestimation of the rate. Despite these uncertainties, the
rate is comparable with the observed rate of optical TDEs
(∼3×10 −5 gal−1 yr−1, Yao et al. 2023) and X-ray TDEs
(∼1×10 −5 gal−1 yr−1, Sazonov et al. 2021; Grotova et al.
2025), and agrees with the estimation of IR-selected TDE
rate∼2×10 −5 gal−1 yr−1 in the IR-luminous galaxy sample
of Masterson et al. 2024. This suggests that the actual TDE
rate in star-forming galaxies may not be lower than the aver-
age of other galaxies if obscured cases are taken into account.
5.3.Pc-scale dust
Using the dust radiative transfer model, we inferred a total
mass of the echoing dust to be∼10−140 M ⊙ within a 3
pc scale. This mass is 2−3 orders of magnitude higher than
logM d =−0.81±0.05 M ⊙ obtained by Jiang et al. (2021a) by
fitting the near-peak SED using an optically-thin dust model.
The reason is that the dust is optically thick at 3−5µm, and
that the peak IR emission only comes from a small amount of
dust near the paraboloid with the same time delay since the
UV illumination does not last long. Assuming a typical gas-

to-dust ratio of 100, the total gas mass in the nucleus reaches
∼10 3 −10 4 M⊙ within 3 pc.
The dust covering factor at the sub-pc scale of obscured
TDEs in J0103+1401 and analogs can be one to two orders
of magnitude higher than that in normal optical TDEs. The
huge difference implies that the sub-pc-scale environment
has undergone a drastic evolution from the star-forming to
the passive/post-starburst stage. The transfer may be trig-
gered by intense feedback from star formation coupled with
possible nuclear activity, which are both regulated by gas
supplies. Galaxy merging may also play a role by redistribut-
ing gas and dust. Therefore, further efforts of TDE search-
ing by IR echoes can also enable a deeper understanding of
the sub-pc environments of SMBHs, which are otherwise ex-
tremely challenging to probe.
6.SUMMARY
We present the detailed analysis of multi-band data of the
IR flare J0103+1401, which was first reported by Jiang et
al. (2021a) as a candidate obscured TDE in a star-forming
galaxy. We measured an SFR of∼5−6 M ⊙ yr−1 in the
host galaxy based on the SED from UV to MIR and the ra-
dio spectrum, and estimated the mass of central SMBH to be
MBH ≈10 7 M⊙ using the host galaxy’s properties.
The IR flare occurred in 2017, peaked in a year, and grad-
ually faded over several years. It was only detected in the
MIR and NIR bands, while no counterpart was detected in
the optical, X-ray and radio bands. The flare’s NIR position
coincides with the galaxy center, limiting the positional offset
to within 100 pc (3σupper limit). Its 1−5µm SED is consis-
tent with a blackbody curve, whose temperature was∼900
K initially and declined to∼700 K at the late time. The peak
blackbody luminosity is∼6×10 43 erg s−1 and the total IR
energy is∼5×10 51 erg by integrating theL BB curve. The IR
multi-band light curves can be explained by IR echo, i. e., the
IR reradiation from dust heated by a primary UV flare. We
inferred the properties of the primary UV flare using the ra-
diative transfer model of Lu et al. (2016), and obtained a UV
luminosity of∼(4−10)×10 44 erg s −1, a time duration of
∼200 days, and a total UV energy of∼(0.9−2)×10 52 erg.
This total energy exceeds the value estimated by integrating
theL BB curve by a factor of 2−4, because the model predicts
that the actual IR SED is a graybody spectrum, which mim-
ics a blackbody spectrum at 1−5µm and exceeds the black-
body’s prediction at longer wavelengths. The James Webb
Space Telescope (JWST; Gardner et al. 2006) working in a
wide wavelength range of 0.6 to 29µm may provide a power-
ful way to explore the complete IR SED of SDSSJ0103+1401
and analogs and the characteristics of dust in the future.
We considered three possible origins of the primary UV
flare: an SN, a CLAGN or a TDE. We ruled out the possibil-
ity of SN because its position coincides with the center of the
galaxy, its total energy exceeds the vast majority of known
SNe, and its MIR luminosity exceeds all known SNe by one
order of magnitude. We disfavored the CLAGN explanation
because of that no AGN signal was seen before the flare, that
the amplitude of the IR flare exceeds the majority of known
CLAGNs, and that the rise time is shorter than most known
CLAGNs. TDE is the best explanation because the luminos-
ity, time scale, temperature variation and total energy of the
IR flare are consistent with those of known IR bright TDEs.
Since no optical flare was detected, the TDE must be ob-
scured by dust withA V ≳2−5 magnitudes.
This study of J0103+1401 presents an example of how to
identify obscured TDE through IR echo and infer the proper-
ties of primary UV radiation. In the future, similar analyses
for more IR flares are required to obtain an unbiased UV lu-
minosity function of TDEs in star-forming galaxies. In this
way, we can ultimately determine whether the star-forming
galaxy has a lower TDE rate than post-starburst galaxies,
leading to an advanced understanding of the TDE-producing
mechanism.
