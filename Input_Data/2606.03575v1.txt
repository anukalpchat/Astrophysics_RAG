1 Introduction
A recent milestone in astrochemistry is the identification of individual gas-phase polycyclic
aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) in the cold, dense cloud TMC-1. Using the Green Bank
Telescope (GBT), McGuire et al. reported the detection of 1- and 2-cyanonaphthalene.1
Shortly afterward, the pure bicyclic aromatic indene was detected by Cernicharo et al.2 with
the highly sensitive receivers of the Yebes 40 m radio telescope, and this detection was soon
confirmed at the GBT.3 Since then, additional PAHs have been identified, including cyano-
acenaphthylene C12H7CN (1- and 5- isomers),4 phenalene C13H10,5 cyano-pyrene C16H9CN
(1-, 2- and 4- isomers)6,7 and cyano-coronene C24H11CN, the largest PAH identified so far.8
Most PAHs detected to date are cyano-substituted species, whose large dipole moments
significantly enhance their radio detectability.
The formation of cyano-PAHs under the cold conditions of dark molecular clouds is
thought to result from reactions between PAHs and CN radicals; for benzene and toluene,
this reaction has been shown to be barrierless.9–11This connection makes it possible to infer
the abundances of the unsubstituted parent PAHs from the observed abundances of their
cyano derivatives.7,8 The inferred values indicate that PAHs must be produced efficiently in
cold clouds.12 Although the underlying processes remain uncertain, current evidence favors
a bottom-up gas-phase chemistry.4,5,13 Moreover, consistent with the pioneering work of
2

Herbst and Klemperer on dense-cloud chemistry,14 these findings further highlight the key
role of ion–molecule reactions in building molecular complexity.15
PAHs – including methylated and, more broadly, alkylated PAHs – have been found to
be abundant in laboratory analyses of primitive Solar System matter, particularly in car-
bonaceous chondrite meteorites16–20and, more recently, in samples from the asteroid Ryugu
returned by the Hayabusa-2 mission.21,22 Zeichner et al.23 investigated the13C isotopic com-
position of several small PAHs in Ryugu material and concluded that these molecules were
formed through distinct chemical processes: some at the very low temperatures characteris-
tic of molecular clouds (∼10 K), and others at much higher temperatures (>1000 K) that
may prevail in circumstellar environments or within the parent body.
Methylated PAHs may contribute to the 3.4µm emission band,24 which appears as a
satellite feature of the 3.3µm aromatic infrared band (AIB) – both of which are key targets
of James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) observations in the Milky Way and in external
galaxies.25–27The 3.4µm band is known to be particularly sensitive to UV irradiation,24,28
consistent with its carriers containing aliphatic C–H bonds, either in alkyl side groups or in
the form of superhydrogenated PAHs with additional H atoms.29,30
Previously, we investigated the properties of the C11H +
9 ions – the H-loss fragments of
methyl-naphthalene cations – and highlighted the relevance of these ions in environments
exposed to mild UV irradiation due to their photophysical properties.31 Three long-lived iso-
mers were identified: 1- and 2-naphthylmethylium (1- and 2-NpCH+
2 ) and benzyltropylium
(BzTr+). Their reactions with the parent neutral lead to two products of unknown structure,
C12H +
11 and C22H +
19, the latter corresponding to an adduct. It was further shown that BzTr+
is unreactive, whereas the other two isomeric populations – very likely 1- and 2-NpCH+
2 –
display reaction rate constants differing by an order of magnitude.
This article investigates the reactivity of C11H +
9 ions with several isomers of dimethyl-
naphthalene (diMeNp), namely 2,3-, 1,4-, and 1,5-diMeNp. Using the multiplex capabilities
of PIRENEA, a setup dedicated to laboratory astrophysics, we studied these reactions under
3

isolated conditions relevant to radiative association. Moreover, the reaction products were
exposed to UV–visible light to gain further insight into their structures and to identify frag-
ments with enhanced photostability. We show that this experimental approach, supported
by DFT calculations, not only improves our understanding of the chemistry of these species
– including isomer-specific effects – but also reveals new chemical pathways for the formation
of cosmic PAHs under cold and mild-UV irradiation conditions.

2 Methodologies
2.1 Experimental Methods
The experiments were performed using the PIRENEA setup,32 operated under the config-
uration described in detail elsewhere.31 The apparatus consists of a Fourier Transform Ion
Cyclotron Resonance Mass Spectrometer (FTICR-MS) maintained under ultra-high vacuum
conditions (∼10 −9 mbar at room temperature), enabling ion trapping under isolated con-
ditions and for long time scales. PIRENEA can also be run at cryogenic temperatures but
4

this capability was not used in this study due to the sticky nature of the injected molecules.
When required, specific ions in the ICR cell (e.g.,12C isotopologs) were isolated by selectively
exciting the cyclotron motion of unwanted ions, thereby ejecting them.
C11H +
9 cations were produced from 2,3-, 1,4-, and 1,5-diMeNp (Figure 1), all of which
possess sufficient vapor pressure at room temperature to be used in our experiments. The
neutral precursors were ionized using the fourth harmonic (266 nm, 4.66 eV) of a Nd:YAG
Surelite I laser (Continuum), producing ions that were trapped in the ICR cell. The pure
12C parent ions were then isolated, and the production of12C11H +
9 was optimized through
photoprocessing of the trapped parent ions using a high-pressure Xe arc lamp equipped with
a 610 nm color filter (CF). Under these conditions, the parent ions dissociate at low energies,
and the resulting fragment ions do not absorb further. Consequently, the12C11H +
9 fragments
are expected to have low internal energy and thermalize with the background during ion
production, which occurs over 20s. Typical mass spectra illustrating the different steps in
the case of 1,4-diMeNp are shown in Figure S1.
Reactions between 12C11H +
9 ions and their neutral precursors were conducted at room
temperature and a typical pressure of∼10−8 mbar. The reaction time was adjusted between
30 and 120s to ensure complete conversion of the reactive ion population. Sufficient time
(up to 200s) was allowed to recover a low background pressure (below a few 10−9 mbar)
before recording the mass spectrum.
Photodissociation kinetics were measured for12C11H +
9 and its reaction cationic products
using broadband visible irradiation from a Xe arc lamp. A 400 nm CF filter was used, with
or without a neutral-density filter (NDF) providing 37% transmission.
Finally, our most challenging experiment imvolved recording a multiple-photon dissocia-
tion (MPD) spectrum of one the reactivity product, C13H +
13. After the reaction and sufficient
pumping time, the product of interest was isolated and irradiated using a mid-band tunable
optical parametric oscillator (OPO) laser (Panther EX, Continuum), operating at 10 Hz with
5 ns pulse duration. The dissociation yield was recorded as a function of OPO wavelength
5

using a fixed irradiation time of 10 s (100 laser pulses) and a pulse energy of 5.5 mJ. This
multi-stage mass spectrometry (MSn) procedure required 3 min per recorded wavelength.
For all experiments, the recorded intensities were normalized to the summed ion intensity
to account for variations in absolute ion intensities across different measurements.
2.2 Computational Methods
Following the methodology proposed by Simon et al.33, we performed molecular dynamics
(MD) simulations to investigate the dissociation of the three diMeNp cations studied here.
In this approach, molecular dissociation is modeled in the electronic ground state, with the
internal energy represented as vibrational excitation. This regime is consistent with the
building of internal energy in the cations by absorption of multiple photons from the Xe
lamp.34 However, the energies need to be sufficiently high for dissociation to occur within
the nanosecond timescale accessible to the simulation. This approach has been recently
applied to rationalize the dissociation dynamics of both MeNp radical cations (C11H +
10 ) and
their C11H +
9 fragments.31
Briefly, the MD simulations were carried out in the Born–Oppenheimer approximation;
the electronic structure is computed on-the-fly using the self-consistent charge density func-
tional based tight-binding (SCC-DFTB) method,35 employing the recently revisedC–H pa-
rameter set.36 A Fermi temperature of 1500K was applied to prevent oscillations in the
self-consistent DFTB procedure, which frequently occur in dissociated or near-dissociated
systems. This also ensured continuity of energies and gradients during level crossing.33 This
explicit treatment of the electronic structure enables the description of chemical evolution
processes such as isomerization and dissociation. For simplicity, this approach will hereafter
be referred to as MD/DFTB.
Each simulation was initiated from the ground-state optimized geometry with random-
ized initial velocities, propagated in the microcanonical (NVE) ensemble. Energies and
gradients were computed every 0.2 fs. All MD/DFTB simulations were performed using the
6

deMonNano code.37 In the present work, a total of 180 simulations were run for the three
diMeNp cations at internal energies of 15.0 and 16.5 eV, for simulation durations of 2 ns and
1 ns, respectively. This strategy enables insight into reaction kinetics, branching ratios, and
underlying mechanisms and thus contributes to the rationalization of experimental results.
In addition to MD/DFTB simulations, DFT local optimisations and all atoms harmonic
frequency calculations were performed at the B3LYP38/6-31G(d,p) level of theory to support
and interpret experimental results. Unless it is explicitly mentioned, all reported molecular
structures were obtained at this level of theory and all reported energy values include zero-
point energy corrections. In specific cases, wavefunction (CCSD(T)/cc-pvtz) single point
energy calculations were performed. Vertical excitation energies were computed using the
Time-Dependent (TD) DFT method with the PBE0 functional39 in conjunction with the cc-
pvtz basis set. This level of theory has been shown to yield satisfactory excitation energies
for C11H +
9 isomers (see Table S3 in the Supplementary material of Lozano et al.31). All
these calculations were achieved with the Gaussian16 suite of programs.40

3.1 C 11H +
9 as a common photofragment
3.1.1 Experimental Insights
Our first key experimental finding is that photodissociation of the diMeNp cations yields
C11H +
9 atm/z141 as the dominant fragment, regardless of the methyl substitution pattern.
A similar behavior is observed for the MeNp cations, for which C11H +
9 is produced as the
exclusive photofragment.31,41 In all cases, the C11H +
9 cation further dissociates by losing
C2H2, producing C9H +
7 cations, which were previously assigned to indenylium when 1-MeNp
was used as the precursor.31 Unlike the photodissociation of MeNp cations, a very minor H-
loss channel was detected for diMeNp when irradiated at 266 nm (see Figure S1), accounting
for at most∼3% of the total dissociation yield.
Figure 2 shows the photofragmentation kinetics under irradiation with the Xe lamp of
the C11H +
9 species produced from all diMeNp investigated. All curves, regardless of the
precursor, display an initial fast decay followed by a slower one, quantified by a biexponential
decay.
Such behavior was observed in our previous work on C11H +
9 ions produced from 1-
MeNp.31 The distinct decay components were attributed to the coexistence of several long-
lived isomers exhibiting distinct absorption features in the visible range.31,42 One could ex-
pect the presence of the same isomers for the C11H +
9 species produced from diMeNp, given
the structural similarities with MeNp. This is further corroborated by the MD/DFTB simu-
lations, as discussed in Section 3.1.2. The slower decay component could then be attributed
to the dissociation of BzTr+, as this isomer has the lowest absorption cross section in the
visible range.31,42 Conversely, the faster decay likely arises from both NpCH+
2 isomers, given
their dominant photoabsorption in this spectral region.31,42 However, the scenario may be
further complicated by potential interconversion between isomers under photon irradiation.31

4 Discussion
The results presented in Section 3 establish C11H +
9 as a key intermediate in the photodis-
sociation and subsequent reactivity of methylated naphthalenes. Its prominence as a com-
mon photofragment of both diMeNp and MeNp, together with its ability to react with
these species, leads to the formation of two primary reaction products, C12H +
11 and C13H +
13.
Photophysical characterization of these two products further reveals distinct fragmentation
pathways.
In the following sections, we examine how our experimental findings data support the
formation of acenaphthene derivatives and can be used to propose chemical scenarios that
could account for the production of these species.
17

4.1 Evidence for acenaphthene derivatives
Among the candidate structures for the C12H +
11 products, our results point to protonated
acenaphthene, [ACN+H+] (Figure 10). This assignment is supported by dissociation via
2H/H2 loss, consistent with the results reported by Szczepanski et al.44, who showed that
[ACN+H+] dissociates primarily through the ejection of two mass units, attributed to H2
loss from the pentagonal ring.

4.2 Formation of acenaphthene derivatives: building chemical sce-
narios
We outline in this section plausible key steps involved in the reactions between C11H +
9 and
methylated naphthalenes. While the proposed chemical scenarios are supported by our DFT
calculations, reaching a definitive conclusion would require more extensive computational
investigation of the reaction pathways. Further details on the calculations performed in this
study are provided in Section S7.
We first consider the formation of [ACN+H+] with 1-NpCH+
2 + 1-MeNp as reactants.
The underlying hypothesis is that [ACN+H+] formation involves C-C coupling between the
methylene carbon of the molecular cation and the methyl carbon of the neutral precursor.
This coupling requires electronic unsaturation in the -CH3 group, which can be achieved by
H migration, either within the same molecule or between molecular units, from the methyl
group to the closest carbon atom of the PAH skeleton.

5 Implications
The chemistry discussed in this work is centered on the reactivity of the C11H +
9 ion, a
common fragment produced during the dissociative ionization (or sequential ionization and
dissociation) of methyl- and dimethyl-naphthalene. Species in which one hydrogen atom of
a PAH is replaced by a methylene cation (CH+
2 ) form a family of carbocations that are
expected to be highly reactive.52 In addition, they are resonance-stabilized through charge
delocalization over the aromaticπ-system. Their study is of interest not only for fundamental
gas-phasechemistry, butalsoforunderstandingprocessesinastrochemicalenvironmentssuch
as cold interstellar clouds and Titan’s ionosphere.
Early studies on the benzylium/tropylium (C7H +
7 ) system showed that benzylium reacts
with toluene to form C8H +
9 ,53,54 via a proposed mechanism involving CH+
2 transfer from
the ion to the neutral molecule without H scrambling (see Scheme II in Shen & Dunbar53).
This mechanism alone is insufficient to explain all our experimental results. Instead, we
propose an alternative mechanism based on the formation of a long-lived complex (adduct),
which can facilitate conformer exploration, thereby promoting H migration, C–C coupling,
23

and, in most cases, the formation of a pentagonal cycle. Our calculations also suggest that
the geometry of the (2-NpCH+
2 )(1-MeNp) complex is less favorable for C-C coupling. A
more detailed exploration of the ion-molecule potential energy surface (PES) would allow to
conclude on the attribution of the C22H +
19 species to this complex.
Two isomers of cyanoacenaphthylene (1- and 5-C12H7CN) were recently detected in TMC-
1.4 Interestingly, the derived column density of this species is 1.7 times higher than that of
the smaller cyanonaphthalene isomers (1- and 2-C10H7CN).1 Phenalene (C13H10) has also
been detected in TMC-1.5 The authors of that study proposed that phenalene is most likely
formed through the reaction of acenaphthylene (ACYN) with CH+
3 , producing C13H +
11 via
radiative association, followed by dissociative recombination to yield C13H10. However, no
pathway was proposed for the formation of ACYN itself, highlighting missing reactions in
current chemical networks that could enable efficient PAH growth in cold dark clouds.4
Thechemistrypresentedhererevealsanefficientroutetoacenaphthenederivativesthrough
ion–molecule reactions, which are expected to proceed efficiently at low temperatures. Al-
though the experiments were conducted at 300 K rather than under cryogenic conditions,
the isolation conditions ensure that only radiatively stabilized processes occur, mirroring
those relevant in astrophysical environments. The key step involves the reactivity of C11H +
9 ,
a typical fragment arising from dissociative ionization of methyl- and dimethyl-naphthalene
and its complexation with these neutral species favoring CC coupling. Photoprocessing of
the two reactivity products, C12H +
11 and C13H +
13 leads to the formation of the acenaphthylene
radical cation, ACYN+. In astrophysical environments, additional energy input could be
supplied by mild UV photons or electron recombination.
Jochimsetal. reportedacriticalenergyof7.8eVforthesurvivalofACYN +inastrophys-
ical environments, compared to only 3.9 eV for methyl-naphthalene cations.29 More recent
experiments using electrostatic storage rings have enabled measurements of improved astro-
physical relevance by probing the competition between radiative cooling and dissociation of
isolated ions over long timescales.55,56 Using the cryogenic DESIREE setup, it was recently
24

determinedthatACYN +remainsstableuptovibrationalenergiesofapproximately7.6eV, 57
a value comparable to that of the anthracene cation (C14H +
10)55 and significantly higher than
the 5.8 eV derived for naphthalene cations.58 ACYN + was identified as the main fragment
ion produced in the dissociative ionization of C14H10(anthracene and phenanthrene),59 which
indicates that it is also the principal fragment of C14H +
10 dissociation. However, any environ-
ment capable of photodissociating C14H +
10 would also lead to the dissociation of ACYN+,
making it unlikely that this pathway accounts for the cyanoacenaphthylene observed in
TMC-1.
The chemistry explored in our study may also influence hydrocarbon chemistry in dense
and FUV-irradiated regions, such as the Orion Bar. These photodissociation regions (PDRs)
contain dense and warm gas (Tgas = 300–600 K) and exhibit active chemistry of small
hydrocarbon radicals, such as C2H and CH2.60 Models predict abundances of these small
hydrocarbons at their peak of a few 10−7 relative to H. Assuming that 10% of carbon is
locked in PAHs with a C/H ratio of 1.4×10−4, the PAH abundance would be 1.4×10−6 if
all PAHs are similar in size to naphthalene, or 2.8×10−7 for PAHs with an average size of
50 C atoms. Under these conditions, highly reactive radicals could readily react with PAHs,
potentially enabling the type of chemistry explored in this study.
C11H +
9 is among the abundant large cations observed byin situmass spectrometry in
Titan’s ionosphere61,62 and could therefore play a key role in PAH chemistry there.63 Un-
derstanding this chemistry is important, as it contributes to the formation of tholins, which
are responsible for Titan’s atmospheric hazes.64 Westlake et al.65 showed that Cassini mis-
sion data on large organic ions are consistent with ion–molecule reactions, highlighting the
formation of C6H +
7 as a precursor of benzene. They also emphasized the need to investigate
ionic chemistry for larger species, particularly those containing 8–13 carbon atoms.65,66 Our
results on the reactivity of C11H +
9 are directly relevant to Titan’s ionosphere, indicating
an active photochemistry that promotes the growth of PAHs, including species containing
pentagonal rings.

6 Conclusion
Using the multiplex capabilities of PIRENEA, we studied the reactivity of C11H +
9 with
methylatednaphthalenesunderisolatedconditionsrelevanttogas-phasechemistryinspace. 67
TheC 11H +
9 ionsatm/z141wereconsistentlyidentifiedaskeyfragmentsproducedinthepho-
todissociationofMeNpanddiMeNpcations. Theseionscomprisethreelong-livedC 11H +
9 iso-
mers – 1-NpCH+
2 , 2-NpCH+
2 , and BzTr+– previously identified from 1-MeNp and 2-MeNp.31
The benzylium-like isomers of C11H +
9 react with neutral MeNp and diMeNp to form two
primary products atm/z155 andm/z169, with relative abundances that depend on the
precursor. The corresponding reaction products, C12H +
11 and C13H +
13, exhibit distinct pho-
todissociation pathways: C12H +
11 predominantly undergoes sequential hydrogen loss, whereas
C13H +
13 follows two competing channels, yielding either C12H +
10 or regenerating C11H +
9 . In
both cases, C12H +
8 emerges as a stable terminal photofragment upon irradiation with visible
light from the Xe lamp.
We propose a chemical scenario involving long-lived ion-molecule complexes, where con-
former exploration can facilitate C–C coupling and the formation of a pentagonal cycle.
While the proposed reaction pathways require further investigation to fully validate each
step, our experimental results, supported by DFT calculations, demonstrate that this chem-
istry offers a viable mechanism for the formation of acenaphthene derivatives in TMC-1, for
which no formation pathway has yet been identified.4
Our study highlights the central role of C11H +
9 as both a common photofragment and
a reactive intermediate that drives the formation of acenaphthene derivatives and related
species. Thisdoesnot, however, ruleoutalternativegrowthpathwaysinvolvingthereactivity
ofnaphthalenewithsmallhydrocarbonradicalsandtheirions, whichwouldrequirededicated
investigations. We are entering an exciting era in which the carbon chemistry underlying
chemical complexity in star- and planet-forming regions can be characterized in ever greater
detail using powerful astronomical instruments.60 The discovery of PAHs in cold clouds with
radio telescopes, together with the recent detection of CH+
3 by JWST,68 poses new challenges
26

for chemical modeling and opens perspectives for laboratory astrophysics in the coming
years. Theseexperimentsmustneverthelesscontendwiththeunusualconditionsprevailingin
space, namely extremely low temperatures and pressures. In particular, identifying radiative
association channels experimentally is far from straightforward,69–73and this is precisely
where ICR setups such as the one used here can provide definitive answers.

S7 Computational details and additional results for Sec-
tion 4.2 "Formation of acenaphtene derivatives: build-
ing chemical scenarii"
Computational details.The relative enthalpies at 0K and 298K of the products and
possible RIs represented in Figure??, relative to the reactants, were computed at the
B3LYP-GD3/aug-cc-pvtz//B3LYP-GD3/6-31G(d,p) level of theory within the Gaussian16
package. Except in specific cases explained hereafter, only local B3LYP-GD3/6-31G(d,p)
optimizations were performed. For the calculations performed in this section, dispersion in-
teractions were taken into account taken into account through the empirical term introduced
by Grimme and collaborators1 due to hypothesis of the formation of the initial molecular
complex RI 1 and RI 1’. In the case of the ion-molecule complexes RI 1 and RI 1’, the
geometries were obtained through a three-step procedure : (i) the potential energy surface
(PES) was explored using a parallel tempering Monte-Carlo algorithm (PTMC) with the
electronic structure computed at the SCC-DFTB level - using the hamiltonian described in
Rapacioli et al.2, constraining the positive charge to be on the 1-NapCH2 monomer. The
PTMC simulations were run for 1,000,000 steps with temperatures ranging from 40K to
S12

400K ; exchanges between all temperatures were allowed and the moveclass was restricted
to intermolecular motions. A repulsive wall (sphere of 13 Å radius centered on the center of
mass of the system) was added to prevent from evaporation. (ii) The 100 most stable iso-
mers were retrieved and locally optimized at the same charge constrained SCC-DFTB level
with all atoms optimization. (iii) The most stable isomers were then optimized at the DFT
(B3LYP-GD3/6-31G(d,p)) level. The derived geometries for RI 1 and RI 1’ are reported in
Figure??. For RI 2 and RI 2’, the geometries were first locally optimized at the SCC-DFTB
level (with no charge constraint) and subsequently optimized at the DFT level.
