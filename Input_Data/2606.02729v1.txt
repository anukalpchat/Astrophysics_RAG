Abstract
We compute the (operator-valued) commutator of a massless scalar fieldϕcoupled to
gravity. We work in perturbations around Minkowski space, in transverse-traceless gauge at
the lowest order inG N. The commutator is composed of different operators, including terms
with Dirac delta derivatives supported on the lightcone. These are responsible for “bending”
the Minkowski lightcone when evaluated on a classical/coherent state of gravitons, which
allows to recover standard microcausality in the fixed-background limit. On more general
gravitational states, metric fluctuations induce an uncertainty in the causal structure. We
compute this effect on a thermal state of gravitons at temperatureTby evaluating the
probability that [ϕ(t, ⃗ x), ϕ(0)]̸= 0. We find that the probability is Gaussian in⃗ x2, centered
on the lightcone and with time-growing variance
Var(⃗ x2) = 16G N T t3
3 .
This result is obtained by subtracting a universal vacuum contribution, which is log-divergent
in the UV and subleading in the large-time limit. As a source of finite size can effectively
serve as a regulator in this case, the lightcone spread in the vacuum appears to be source-
dependent.
1 Introduction
Commutators of local observables are a direct probe of causality, because they determine
whether measurements made at separate events can affect each other. When fields are quantized
on a classical background spacetime, such commutators vanish at spacelike separation—this is
the principle ofmicrocausality[1, 2]. In this limit, the analytic properties of the commutator,
or equivalently of the Green functions, encode detailed information about the causal structure
of the theory [3–8]. Things get more subtle when gravity is taken to be dynamical. Strictly
speaking, local field operators are no longer gauge invariant and therefore are not observables.
Challenging the principle of microcausality in this context can be misleading—this is why one
typically resorts to verifying subluminal propagation directly at spacetime asymptotics while
ignoring what happens in the bulk (e.g. [9–13] and related references).
On the other hand—of course—onecan stillmake “localized” measurements in the bulk of
a fluctuating spacetime. It is just that some coordinate fixing prescription must be attached
to the corresponding operators to specify, in a gauge invariant manner, the position at which
the measurement is performed. This results in mildly non-local observables. For example, in
asymptotically AdS, one can define a location in the bulk by sending in a geodesic from a point
of the boundary. The resulting operators are dressed by a gravitational Wilson line. 1

These dressed operators are physically meaningful, especially if they represent the observables
available to a physical observer. Their commutators are a direct probe of the causal relations
experienced by those observers. It is a fair interesting question to ask how such relations are
affected in generic gravitational states. One expects that the quantum fluctuations of the metric
can make the lightcone structure uncertain and the causal relations probabilistic [20–22].
It is the purpose of the present paper to quantify this phenomenon in the concrete setup of a
thermal state of gravitons (see also the results in [23], on which we comment in Sec. 7.1 below).
To this end, we calculate in perturbation theory the commutator of a scalar field minimally
coupled to gravity. We show that its support becomes operator-valued, leading to a nonzero
probability that the commutator has support away from the classical lightcone. In a thermal
state, and to lowest order in the gravitational coupling, this probability is Gaussian, centered
on the classical lightcone, with a variance that grows in time.

2 Choice of frame
Before diving into the computations, a remark is in order to better understand what is going
on. In the presence of dynamical gravity, different coordinate fixings,i.e.different dressing
procedures, effectively correspond to different definitions of an event. This phenomenon can be
summarized with the sloganrelativity of the event[24] (see also [25–27]). A straightforward
consequence is that, in some situations, the commutator of a massless field can have support
on a sharp lightcone surface in some coordinates and exhibit uncertainty in a different set of
coordinates. In the simplest possible terms, this can be shown with a mini-superspace example.
The metric element can be written in conformal timeτ, or in proper timet, as
ds2 =a 2(τ)(−dτ 2 +d⃗ x2) =−dt 2 +a 2(t)d⃗ x2 .(1)
When usingτ, light rays are obtained by integratingd|⃗ x|=dτ. No matter how crazily the
scale factorafluctuates, these coordinates always exhibit a sharp classical lightcone. In the
t-coordinate, light rays are instead obtained by integratingd|⃗ x|=dt/a(t)—fluctuations ofa
now do induce an uncertainty of the lightcone. Of course, on a classical spacetime, switching
fromτtotis mere relabeling. But whenais dynamical, switching totchanges the nature of
causal relations from sharp to probabilistic.
While all coordinate choices are equivalent in principle, physical measurements correspond
to a particular type of dressing, in which positions are tied to observers worldlines and time is
identified with the proper time measured along them. As free-falling observers in the universe,
we maintain continuous causal contact with nearby galaxies/observers. When we detect, for
instance, a gamma-ray burst, we record the event at a definite value of our own proper time—
the only notion of time directly accessible from our geodesic. The source event itself, such as the
collapse of the progenitor star, is attached to another distant geodesic and triggered by the local
physics there,i.e., by a definite proper time along that worldline. For real observers, decoding
causal relations in any other frame than their own is possible in principle, but very difficult in
practice. In the example above, accessing theτcoordinate would require complete knowledge
of the (quantum) state of the scale factora.

correlation functions is smooth and finite. More generally, for Hadamard states, the singular
structure is universal and coincides locally with that of the Minkowski vacuum, so that it drops
out when isolating the state-dependent contribution. 4

6 The vacuum contribution
In the previous analysis we employed the purely thermal propagator (28). Restoring the vac-
uum contribution leads to a divergent variance, reflecting the universal short-distance structure
of Hadamard states. From the point of view of the generating functional, this divergence arises
from ultraviolet modes evaluated on the current (24), whose support has co-dimension three,
namely a null line segment. In this section we examine this vacuum contribution and possible
regularizations of it. We will also attempt to discuss its physical meaning, while remaining
essentially agnostic about it.

Finally, and perhaps more importantly, the result is regulator dependent—ultimately, as
argued above, source-dependent. Taken at face value, (37) seems to imply that an extremely
localized source can produce a larger spread of the lightcone. This is somewhat reminiscent of
standard quantum mechanics, where the more localized a particle is initially, the more rapidly it
spreads. However, we are not used to this phenomenon in field theory where, on the opposite, the
lightcone structure is typically sharp, in the absence of gravity. Perhaps, the source-dependent
result (37) is spurious and should be subtracted from the total computation, much like the
vacuum contribution to the entropy in quantum field theory should be removed if one is interested
in the thermal entropy of a region of space.
7 Discussion
A thermal state of gravitons is not precisely an everyday occurrence, but it provides a partic-
ularly simple and well-controlled setting (see however the discussion in Sec. 7.2 below), entirely
within the domain of the effective field theory of gravity. It will be interesting to generalize
our computations to states different than the thermal state, and to frames different than the
free-falling one, such as aRindler-like frame where⃗ x= constant represent the worldlines of ob-
servers with constant acceleration. We conclude with a list of comments on our results, ordered
roughly from the more technical to the more speculative.
7.1 Comparison with the results of L. H. Ford
While finalizing our draft, we became aware of the important works by Laurence H. Ford
and collaborators on fluctuations of causality on general gravitational states [23, 39–42]. In
particular, in [23] without explicitly calculating the commutator in perturbation theory, Ford
correctly assumes that the retarded propagator is delta-like supported on the modified lightcone

7.2 Self-consistency of the setup
One might correctly object that a thermal state of gravitons is incompatible with Minkowski
spacetime once backreaction is taken into account. Indeed, a thermal energy density of∼T 4
produces a curvature radius ofH −1 ∼1/(κT 2). Therefore, strictly speaking, our calculation
should be trusted only up to times shorter than that. Roughly, this time scale is also the
age of a Universe dominated by radiation at that temperature. In this Universe, however,
the temperature evolves in time, so, again, our calculations can be trusted only for a limited
time-span (see below, however, for possible implications for black hole physics). It is tempting
to speculate, however, that the broader message of our result (31) is a secular linear growth
in time of⟨O(x) 2⟩β, with a coefficient proportional to the temperature, and that this could
be essentially extrapolated to more realistic scenarios, e.g., when the temperature is smoothly
changing in time. It will be interesting to verify this conjecture in a concrete setup.
7.3 The spacetime geometry of a bath of thermal gravitons
According to the “particle physicist’s viewpoint” on Einstein gravity, famously pioneered
by Feynman [43] and later by Weinberg among others, general relativity is the theory of a
massless spin-2 particle in Minkowski. The lightcone spread discussed in this paper is most
directly interpreted inside this scheme, as due to the scattering of a massless particle off a bath
of thermal gravitons. In many respects, however, therelativists’viewpoint can prove more
efficient, by condensing all the effects of gravity into the geometry of spacetime. So what is the
spacetime geometry of a thermal state of gravitons?
With vanishing expectation value for the graviton field,⟨h ij(x)⟩β = 0, the average metric
tensor is everywhere flat.6 One could therefore argue that such a state is indistinguishable from
Minkowski spacetime. This intuition, however, only holds at short distances. Our results show
a growing uncertainty of causality at large separations, which the average metric alone cannot
account for. In this sense, the classical spacetime description of general relativity gradually
ceases to apply. One might be tempted to associate any breakdown of classical spacetime with
a sharp, localized phenomenon, like in the famously debated firewall scenario in black-hole
evaporation [44]. Our results point to a different possibility. Roughly speaking, the classical
spacetime picture appears to fail here in the same way that flat spacetime fails in general
relativity itself: not abruptly, but at sufficiently large mutual separations [36,45].
