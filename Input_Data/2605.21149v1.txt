ABSTRACT
Context.Optimal power spectrum estimation on the largest angular scales of the cosmic microwave background relies on the Quadratic
Maximum Likelihood (QML) estimator. Existing public implementations, however, each address only a subset of the problem and
none combines power spectrum estimation with a self-consistent pixel-space likelihood within a single framework.
Aims.We presentCosmoForge, a public Python framework that unifies QML power spectrum estimation and pixel-based Gaussian
likelihood evaluation for spin-0 and spin-2 fields on the sphere, with general (non-diagonal) noise covariances.
Methods.The framework is split into three installable packages:CosmoCore(infrastructure),QUBE(Fisher and QML estimation), and
PICSLike(pixel-space likelihood). A common interface exposes two interchangeable computation bases — a harmonic basis built
on the Sherman–Morrison–Woodbury identity and a direct pixel-space basis — selecting whichever is cheaper for the configuration at
hand. Exact algorithmic optimisations reduce the Fisher cost toO(ℓ 4
max) for arbitrary noise covariances, withNumbaJIT compilation
of the hot kernels and MPI parallelisation of the likelihood scan.
Results.CosmoForgereproduces the Planck low-ℓFortran reference implementation across both the QML and pixel-space likelihood
pipelines, consistently with double-precision arithmetic. Native multipole binning and three output normalisations (deconvolved,
decorrelated, window-convolved) are exposed through a single code path, and the same covariance infrastructure powers both QML
estimation and likelihood evaluation.
Conclusions.CosmoForgeoffers a general-purpose, modular, and validated tool for the optimal analysis of large-scale data on the
sphere. It is publicly available, pip-installable, and extensible to non-CMB observables.

1. Introduction
The angular power spectrum of the Cosmic Microwave
Background (CMB) is a cornerstone observable of modern
Cosmology. Its measurement has driven precision constraints
on the cosmological model, from the first detections of acous-
tic oscillations to the tight bounds on the tensor-to-scalar
ratiorfrom current experiments (Planck Collaboration
2020; BICEP/Keck Collaboration 2021; Tristram et al.
2022). Next-generation experiments such as LiteBIRD
(LiteBIRD Collaboration 2023) and the Simons Observa-
tory (Ade et al. 2019) will target large angular scales with
unprecedented sensitivity, where the primordial gravitational
wave signal in B-mode polarization is expected to peak and
where the optical depth to reionizationτmust be determined.
At these large angular scales, the limited number of available
modes and the effects of partial sky coverage make optimal sta-
tistical methods essential. The Quadratic Maximum Likelihood
(QML) estimator, introduced by Tegmark (1997) and extended
to polarization by Tegmark & de Oliveira-Costa (2001), pro-
vides minimum-variance, unbiased power spectrum estimates
by construction. Unlike pseudo-C ℓ methods (Hivon et al. 2002;
Alonso et al. 2019), which are computationally cheap but sub-
⋆ Corresponding author:giacomo.galloni@unife.it
optimal on large scales, the QML approach accounts exactly for
the mask-induced mode coupling and noise anisotropy through
the full pixel-space covariance matrix. This optimality comes
at a computational cost that scales asO(N 3
pix) in the naive im-
plementation, set by the inversion of the pixel-space covariance
and by the dense matrix products in the Fisher-matrix trace,
limiting its applicability to low-resolution maps (N side ≲64).
However, the large-scale science case — primordial B-modes,
reionization optical depth, parity violation, isotropy anomalies
— precisely requires this regime, making QML the method of
choice for the most sensitive measurements planned in the com-
ing decade. The QML estimator’s optimality is not, however,
restricted to the large-scale regime: at intermediate and small
scales, where pseudo-C ℓ methods are usually adopted for their
cheap cost, QML can still deliver tighter error bars when the
noise covariance is non-trivial or the mask geometry induces sig-
nificant mode coupling that pseudo-C ℓ approximations handle
only asymptotically, opening room for deeper scientific targets
where computational resources allow.
The pixel-space likelihood offers a complementary ap-
proach: rather than estimating the power spectrum as an in-
termediate step, it evaluates the full Gaussian log-likelihood

Over the past two decades, several QML implementa-
tions have been developed, each tailored to specific analy-
sis needs: pixel-space polarization estimation (Gruppuso et al.
2009), the unified low-ℓlikelihood and QML formalism
of Gjerløw et al. (2015), which compared pixel, spherical-
harmonic, and signal-to-noise eigenbasis representations on the
same footing, cross-spectrum estimation for noise bias removal
(Vanneste et al. 2018), optimised harmonic-space formulations
(Bilbao-Ahedo et al. 2021), and scaling to large numbers of cor-
related fields for galaxy surveys (Kvasiuk et al. 2025). These
codes have enabled important scientific results, but they indi-
vidually address different subsets of the problem. Some are re-
stricted to specific computation bases (pixel or harmonic, but
not both), others assume diagonal noise covariance — excluding
the correlated noise patterns common in real CMB experiments,
both space- and ground-based, and ubiquitous when residual sys-
tematics or component-separation outputs are propagated to the
analysis covariance — and none integrates a pixel-space likeli-
hood module within the same framework. The situation echoes
that of the pseudo-C ℓ landscape before the development of Na-
Master (Alonso et al. 2019), where well-validated codes such as
MASTER (Hivon et al. 2002), PolSpice (Chon et al. 2004), Xpol
(Tristram et al. 2005), and Xpure (Grain et al. 2009) each han-
dled specific cases well, but the community lacked a single pub-
lic framework consolidating those capabilities into a maintained,
validated tool.
In this paper, we presentCosmoForge, a unified Python
framework for QML power spectrum estimation and pixel-based
likelihood analysis of fields on the sphere. While designed
with the CMB in mind, the framework applies to any signal
characterised by an angular power spectrum (e.g. galaxy sur-
veys, 21 cm intensity mapping, gravitational-wave backgrounds)
and to any combination of them.CosmoForgesupports the
joint analysis of scalar (spin-0, denotedS) and tensor (spin-
2, decomposed into gradientGand curlCcomponents) fields
with all auto- and cross-spectra (S S,GG,CC,S G,S C,GC; the
CMB analogues areTT,EE,BB,T E,T B,EB) in arbitrary multi-
field configurations, with automatic detection of field groups
that decouple in both signal and noise for fast block-diagonal
covariance handling. The same algorithm is exposed through
two interchangeable computation bases — a harmonic ba-
sis built on the Sherman–Morrison–Woodbury (SMW) identity
(Planck Collaboration 2016a) and a direct pixel-space basis with
Cholesky inversion — behind a common abstract interface that
auto-selects the cheaper path. Native multipole binning is pro-
vided through a single code path that recovers per-multipole
estimation as its∆ℓ=1 special case, with three output nor-
malisations available without recomputation: deconvolved ˆCℓ =
F−1q, decorrelatedF −1/2qwith unit covariance, and window-
convolved estimates for direct comparison with theory. The same
covariance infrastructure powers a pixel-space Gaussian likeli-
hood over cosmological parameter grids, with MPI parallelism
on the independent stages (Fisher trace pairs, independent sim-
ulations, parameter-grid points) and Numba JIT compilation
of the hot kernels (Legendre polynomials, Wignerd-matrices,
signal-matrix assembly), bringing the harmonic-basis Fisher to
an exactO(ℓ 4
max) scaling for arbitrary noise covariances.
CosmoForgeis organised as three interconnected, inde-
pendently pip-installable packages:CosmoCore(computational
foundation, including the computation-basis abstraction with its
harmonic and pixel implementations and the SMW machin-
ery),QUBE(Quadratic maximum likelihood UnBiased Estima-
tor: Fisher matrix and QML estimation), andPICSLike(Pixel-
based Inference with Correlated-Skies Likelihood: pixel-space
Gaussian likelihood over parameter grids). The full pipeline
has been validated against the Planck low-ℓFortran refer-
ence of Pagano et al. (2020) and thesimalllikelihood of
Planck Collaboration (2020) to machine-precision tolerances
(Sect. 4.1). Forthcoming work will present specific features and
scientific case studies.
The paper is organised as follows. Section 2 reviews the
QML formalism, the harmonic-space reformulation based on the
Sherman–Morrison–Woodbury identity, native multipole bin-
ning, the three available output normalisations, and the pixel-
space likelihood. Section 3 describes the package architec-
ture and its principal classes. Sections 4–5 present the vali-
dation against the Planck low-ℓreference, internal consistency
and Monte Carlo tests, an end-to-end pixel-based inference
demonstration on the tensor-to-scalar ratio, and the empirical
performance characterisation. Section 6 summarises the work
and outlines forthcoming developments. Appendices A–E col-
lect the spin-2 extension and the spin-weighted harmonic con-
ventions, the matrix-determinant-lemma derivation of the log-
determinant, the algorithmic optimisations of the harmonic-
space implementation, the comparison ofCosmoForgewith ex-
isting public QML codes, and the configuration interface to-
gether with the Y AML reference.
2. Formalism
In this section, we present the mathematical framework underly-
ing the QML estimator and its implementation inCosmoForge.
We begin with the signal model and covariance structure
(Sect. 2.1), then present the QML estimator in pixel space
(Sect. 2.2). Section 2.3 introduces the harmonic-space refor-
mulation based on the Sherman–Morrison–Woodbury identity,
which provides an exact and computationally efficient alternative
to direct pixel-space evaluation. For clarity, we present the for-
malism for spin-0 (scalar) fields throughout Sects. 2.1–2.3; the
generalisation to spin-2 (tensor) fields is given in Appendix A.
Sections 2.4–2.6 describe multipole binning, the three normali-
sation modes, and the pixel-space likelihood, respectively.

2.3. Harmonic-space formulation
An algebraically equivalent but computationally more efficient
formulation of the QML estimator can be obtained by factor-
ing the signal covariance through the spherical harmonic basis.
The harmonic factorisationS=V ⊤ΛVthat underpins this re-
formulation goes back to Tegmark (1997), where it was intro-
duced as a computational device for the pixel-space Fisher ma-
trix; Gjerløw et al. (2015) placed it within a unified low-ℓlike-
lihood framework that treats the spherical-harmonic, pixel, and
signal-to-noise eigenbasis representations on a common footing.
Its combination with the Sherman–Morrison–Woodbury (SMW)
identity (Woodbury 1950; Golub & Van Loan 2013) for efficient
covariance inversion was introduced in the context of the Planck
low-ℓlikelihood (Planck Collaboration 2016a).CosmoForge
builds on this lineage, extending the SMW-accelerated harmonic
basis from likelihood evaluation to QML power spectrum esti-
mation and binding the two to a common pixel-space alternative
through a single computation-basis interface (Sect. 3.2).

3. Code architecture and implementation
CosmoForgeis implemented in Python and organised as three
interconnected packages with a layered architecture:CosmoCore
provides the computational foundation,QUBEimplements the
QML estimation pipeline, andPICSLikeevaluates the pixel-
space likelihood. Each package is independently installable but
shares a common infrastructure throughCosmoCore. In this sec-
tion we describe the design principles, the main classes and their
responsibilities, and the computational strategies employed.
3.1. Package structure
The three packages are:
–CosmoCore— Core computational library. Provides field
management (spin-0 and spin-2), spherical harmonic and
pixel-space operations, matrix algebra, the harmonic oper-
atorVand SMW machinery, binning, beam handling, and
I/O utilities. All numerically intensive operations (Legendre
polynomial evaluation, Wignerd-matrix computation, signal
matrix construction) are accelerated with Numba JIT compi-
lation (Lam et al. 2015) to achieve performance comparable
to compiled languages.
–QUBE(Quadratic maximum likelihood UnBiased Estima-
tor) — QML analysis engine. Implements theFisher
andSpectraclasses for Fisher matrix computation and
power spectrum estimation, including all three normalisa-
tion modes (Sect. 2.5), noise bias computation, and cross-
spectrum support.
–PICSLike(Pixel-based Inference with Correlated-Skies
Likelihood) — Pixel-space likelihood engine. Implements
thePICSLikeclass for evaluating the Gaussian likelihood
(Eq. 30) over parameter grids, with automated marginalisa-
tion and confidence interval extraction.
BothQUBEandPICSLikeinherit from theCoreabstract
base class defined inCosmoCore, which provides the common
setup pipeline: field initialisation, pixel geometry computation,
noise covariance loading, power spectrum and beam configura-
tion, and — when the harmonic-space formulation is used —
construction of theVoperator and SMW quantities. This shared
inheritance ensures that the same covariance infrastructure is
used consistently across QML estimation and likelihood eval-
uation. A schematic overview of the package structure is shown
in Fig. 1.

3.3. QUBE
TheQUBEpackage implements the QML estimation pipeline
through two main classes.
TheFisherclass computes the Fisher information matrix
(Eq. 4 or 21) and optionally caches the binned derivative ma-
trices for reuse by theSpectraclass. It supports both single-
spectrum (e.g., a scalar-only analysis) and multi-spectrum (e.g.,
joint scalar and spin-2) configurations. In the multi-spectrum
case, the Fisher matrix has dimension (n s ×N bins)2, with blocks
coupling different spectra and multipole bins. The computation
is parallelised across MPI processes by distributing the Fisher
matrix element pairs.
TheSpectraclass computes the QML power spectrum es-
timatesq ℓ (Eq. 6 or 24), the noise biasb ℓ (Eq. 7), and re-
turns the final estimates in any of the three normalisation modes
(Sect. 2.5). It can accept a precomputedFisherinstance to
avoid redundant computation, and inherits the cached deriva-
tives and basis configuration. Multiple simulation maps are pro-
cessed together at every rank, vectorised across the simulation
index throughnumpybroadcasting; the MPI parallelism on this
stage distributes the (spectrum, bin) pairs of the QML evaluation
across ranks rather than the simulations themselves. The cross-
correlation mode (Eq. 11) is supported for pairs of independent
data sets with uncorrelated noise.
3.4. PICSLike
ThePICSLikepackage evaluates the pixel-space Gaussian like-
lihood (Eq. 30) across a user-specified grid of cosmological pa-
rameters.
TheParameterGridclass manages the Cartesian product of
parameter ranges and the associated theoretical power spectra,
which are loaded from precomputed files at each grid point. The
ℓ-switching mode (Sect. 2.3.4), shared with the QML pipeline
through the commonCoresetup, lets the user vary the power
spectrum only over a target multipole range while holding higher
multipoles fixed at a fiducial model. This reduces the multipole
range that must be tabulated in each grid-point spectra file and
the per-point computational cost; the file count is unchanged.
At each grid point, the signal covarianceS(θ) is rebuilt from
the theoreticalC ℓ(θ), the total covarianceC(θ)=S(θ)+Nis
formed, and the log-likelihood is evaluated. When the harmonic
basis is available, the SMW identity provides both the quadratic
formd ⊤C−1dand the log-determinant ln|C|at a cost ofO(n 3
modes)
per grid point. Grid points are distributed across MPI processes
in a round-robin fashion. Multiple simulation maps are evaluated
together at every rank, sharing the per-point covariance inverse
and producing per-simulation likelihood curves at no extra in-
version cost.
TheLikelihoodResultclass stores theχ 2 and log-
likelihood values across the parameter grid, and provides meth-
ods for best-fit extraction, marginalised likelihoods (by summing
over unwanted parameter dimensions), and confidence interval
computation (by finding the smallest interval containing the de-
sired probability mass). Results are serialisable for later analysis.
The configuration interface that drives everyCosmoForge
analysis (Y AML schema, default templates, and a minimal
Python usage example) is documented in Appendix E.

5.1. Numba JIT acceleration
The two performance-critical Python kernels inCosmoCoreare

The compilation overhead is paid exactly once per process:

6. Conclusions and outlook
We have presentedCosmoForge, a Python framework that inte-
grates QML power spectrum estimation and pixel-space Gaus-
sian likelihood evaluation for spin-0 and spin-2 fields with gen-
eral (non-diagonal) noise covariances, exposing a harmonic-
space SMW path and a direct pixel-space path through a single
abstract computation-basis interface (Sect. 3.2). The implemen-
tation is validated end-to-end against the Planck low-ℓFortran
reference of Pagano et al. (2020) and thesimalllikelihood of
Planck Collaboration (2020) and exercised on 10 4 Monte Carlo
polarisation realisations across five sky fractions (Sect. 4.1).
Within the QML code landscape (Appendix D),CosmoForge
is the only public framework that simultaneously exposes three
normalisation modes, both computation bases through a single
abstract interface, an integrated pixel-space likelihood, and a
per-component multipole floor, all in Python.
Several extensions of the released framework are at vari-
ous stages of development and will be presented in dedicated
follow-up papers. Iterative QML (Bilbao-Ahedo et al. 2021) can
be driven on top ofCosmoForgeby re-entering the pipeline with
the previous-iteration estimates in place of the fiducial, leverag-
ing the modular separation between fiducial ingestion and basis
assembly; an end-to-end pixel-based-inference characterisation,
including application to real CMB data and integration with cos-
mological samplers, is the subject of a forthcoming companion
paper based on the demonstration in Sect. 4.3; and GPU accel-
eration of the pixel-space hot loops viaCuPyorJAXis localised
to the basis kernels by the abstract interface and is the obvious
next step where the dense linear algebra dominates. The frame-
work targets the regime of upcoming low-ℓCMB analyses —
LiteBIRD (LiteBIRD Collaboration 2023) and the Simons Ob-
servatory (Ade et al. 2019) — where correlated noise, partial
sky, and a primordialB-mode target make optimal estimators es-
sential, and extends to non-CMB spherical observables (galaxy
clustering, 21 cm intensity mapping, stochastic GWB searches)
and their cross-correlations through the multi-field design.
CosmoForgereplaces the historical proliferation of single-
purpose QML codes with a single, modular, validated, and
openly-developed framework, lowering the barrier to entry for
optimal large-scale analyses and providing a reliable foundation
on which future algorithmic and scientific work can build.

C.4. Implementation notes
The pixel-space noise covariance is loaded via tilednumpyfancy
indexing (unmasked-pixel index split into∼64 MiB tiles), so
that the active-mask submatrix is assembled inO(⌈N pix/Ntile⌉2)
tile iterations without a full-size intermediate; signal-covariance
assembly and spin-2Vconstruction areNumba @njitkernels
paired with direct LAPACK bindings, leaving no critical path
in the Python interpreter. The sparse derivatives{E ℓ}built dur-
ing the Fisher computation are cached and reused byQUBEat
the power-spectrum stage at no extra setup cost. On multi-rank
nodes the largest precomputed objects (N,S, the SMW factors)
are placed in MPI shared-memory windows, so that per-node
footprint is set by the object sizes rather than by the rank count.
Numerically, the (2ℓ+1)/(4π) prefactor is absorbed into the
spherical-harmonic basis functions (eliminating a class of post-
hoc normalisation errors atℓ=2), Wigner-dmatrix elements use
the Jacobi-derived three-term recurrence (Appendix A), Fisher
decorrelation regularises eigenvalues below 10 −12 of the maxi-
mum, and log-determinants flow through the matrix-determinant
lemma (Appendix B) on LAPACK Cholesky factors so that no
large determinant is ever computed directly.

Appendix D: Comparison with existing codes
The QML estimator has been implemented several times over
the past two decades, each implementation tailored to the sci-
ence case at hand and to the computing resources available at the
time. The role of this section is to positionCosmoForgewithin
that landscape — not to claim superior performance against any
specific competitor. The correctness ofCosmoForgeis estab-
lished independently in Sect. 4.1 through element-by-element
validation against the Planck low-ℓFortran reference implemen-
tation. What follows is a feature-scope comparison in the spirit
of Alonso et al. (2019), who introducedNaMasteras a unified
pseudo-Cℓ tool succeeding a generation of single-purpose codes.
The QML landscape today is at a similar stage: each public
code addresses a useful subset of the problem, and the gap that
CosmoForgeseeks to fill is not a missing feature but the uni-
fication of features across a single, modern, extensible Python
framework.
D.1. The QML code landscape
We focus on the QML implementations that are publicly avail-
able, documented, and used in the recent CMB literature. We
deliberately exclude internal or unpublished codes, and we do
not attempt a historical survey; Bilbao-Ahedo et al. (2021) and
Vanneste et al. (2018) provide complementary discussions of the
algorithmic lineage.
The Planck low-ℓreference.The Fortran code used as the ref-
erence in Sect. 4.1, distributed athttps://baltig.infn.it/
cosmology_ferrara/lowell-likelihood-analysisand
used in Pagano et al. (2020) and thesimalllow-ℓlikelihood of
Planck Collaboration (2020), comprises three modules: template
fitting for polarisation, QML power spectrum estimation sup-
porting both auto- and cross-spectra, and a pixel-based Gaussian
likelihood evaluated on a parameter grid. It supports general
(non-diagonal) noise covariances and the fullT,Q,Ujoint
analysis. Its main limitation is the implementation language —
Fortran with custom build chains — and the absence of native
multipole binning; both were appropriate for the production
context but represent a barrier to entry today.
ECLIPSE (Bilbao-Ahedo et al. 2021).A Fortran imple-
mentation distributed athttps://github.com/CosmoTool/
ECLIPSE, designed around the observation that the QML algebra
simplifies dramatically when the noise is spatially uncorrelated.

both pixel- and harmonic-space implementations of the algebra,
and provides a binned version of the estimator that recovers a
regular Fisher matrix when small sky fractions would otherwise
render the standard estimator singular. The code is parallelised
for high-performance computing centres (144 cores in the pub-

— an assumption that allows several traces and matrix-vector

related noise covariances that arise generically once residual sys-
tematics, component-separation outputs, or cross-frequency cor-

space- and ground-based experiments.

D.3. Positioning
A direct head-to-head runtime comparison with ECLIPSE in its
diagonal-noise regime — the only configuration in which a like-
for-like measurement is possible without porting one of the two
codes outside its design assumptions — requires running both
codes at matchingN side,ℓ max and mask, on the same hardware,
and is deferred to a dedicated companion note where the con-
figuration can be controlled in full. The purpose of the present
section is feature scope rather than runtime benchmarking.
CosmoForgefills, for QML analyses, the same gap that
NaMaster(Alonso et al. 2019) filled for pseudo-C ℓ analyses: a
single public, validated, modular Python framework that does
the standard analysis correctly out of the box, can be ex-
tended to non-standard configurations without forking the code-
base, and complements existing single-purpose implementations
within one community-maintained codebase. Distribution chan-
nels (PyPI package, GitHub repository, online documentation)
are listed in the conclusions (Sect. 6).
