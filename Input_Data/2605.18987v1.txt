ABSTRACT
We investigate the role of ejective and preventive feedback in∼10 10 −10 11 M⊙ dwarf halos using
cosmological zoom-in simulations. These simulations use adaptive mesh refinement to capture high-
specific-energy outflows, together with an implementation of discrete supernovae (SNe). We show that
episodic, SNe-driven shock heating sustains the circumgalactic medium (CGM) at∼T vir. This process
also increases the ratio t cool/tff >10 in the outer CGM and intergalactic medium (IGM), placing the
gas in a radiatively stable regime. Hot outflows (≳10 5 K) dominate the energy budget, and their high
specific energy allows them to traverse the CGM, escape the halo, and heat the IGM. In contrast,
warm outflows (≲10 5 K) dominate the mass budget and are largely recycled back into the interstellar
medium (ISM), where they fuel future star formation. We identify a gradual transition at∼5 Gyr
that marks a shift in the balance between ejective and preventive feedback. At early times (<5 Gyr),
although the CGM cooling rate dominates for a larger fraction of time, the infrequent yet powerful
SNe energy injection into the CGM is able to quickly dominate the cumulative energy balance. These
outflows and their high specific energy are able to ‘sweep’ up mass in the CGM and IGM. At late times
(>5 Gyr), the CGM baryon fraction is only∼0.1, leading to a transition toward a preventive feedback
mode in which SNe maintain t cool/tff >10 and prevent∼75% of the expected baryon accretion rate.

1.INTRODUCTION
Howdid the gas in and around galaxies get there?
This simple question has become central to one of the
most important (yet elusive) topics in galaxy formation
and evolution –– feedback. In order to form a galaxy, the
rate of radiative cooling (t cool) for some fraction of the
baryon content must be less than the age of the Universe
(Spitzer 1956). Yet, early models of galaxy formation re-
vealed that, without feedback, gas cools too efficiently,
leading to excessive star formation – an issue known as
the “overcooling” problem (Dekel & Silk 1986; White &
Frenk 1991; Katz et al. 1996). Feedback, which encom-
passes processes such as stellar winds, supernova (SNe),
radiation, and active galactic nucleus (AGN) outflows,
plays a critical role in regulating star formation. A di-
rect consequence of feedback is that gas does not always
trace dark matter. Instead, the difference between the

observed gas fraction within the CGM (f CGM) reflects
the extent to which feedback has redistributed the gas
(see review articles from Crain & van de Voort 2023;
Wright et al. 2024).
The advent of the Cosmic Origins Spectrograph
(COS) aboard the Hubble Space Telescope (HST) and
large-scale galaxy surveys has allowed astronomers to
study the circumgalactic medium (CGM) and its con-
nection to the interstellar medium (ISM) and intergalac-
tic medium (IGM) in unprecedented detail (Chen et al.
1998; Tumlinson et al. 2011, 2013; Werk et al. 2014).
However, the sightline approach of HST/COS has made
it difficult to directly link these observations to feedback-
driven processes. For example, absorption lines probe
individual lines of sight which may not trace the gas
phase carrying most of the energy from feedback. To
help, astronomers have turned to recent advances in
computation, enabling the detailed study of gas at vari-
ous scales: from the interaction between the cold and hot
phase at the parsec level (Gronke & Oh 2020; McCourt
et al. 2018; Abruzzo et al. 2024) to cosmological simu-

1.2.Low Mass Halos
Given the observed lowη M and clear evidence for the
necessity for energy-loaded, high-specific energy winds,
the idea of SNe-driven preventive feedback regulating
star-formation in the dwarf galaxy regime is promis-
ing. In the halo mass range below∼10 11.5 M⊙, the
role of SNe feedback can be isolated from AGN feed-
back (although this is an open question, see Koud-
mani et al. 2019; Barai & de Gouveia Dal Pino 2019;
Sharma et al. 2020; Arjona-G´ alvez et al. 2024) and sta-
ble virial shock-heating of the halo (Birnboim & Dekel
2003; Dekel & Birnboim 2006). In addition, low mass
halos (∼10 10 −10 11 M⊙) have a virial temperature of
Tvir ∼10 5 K and are therefore uniquely positioned at
the peak of the cooling curve (Mo et al. 2010).
In considering whether SNe-driven preventive feed-
back can regulate star formation, two distinct but re-
lated questions arise. The first concerns the thermal
state of the CGM: in the traditional view of a dwarf
galaxy, we might naively expect SNe heating of the
halo to cool rapidly (White & Frenk 1991), driving
TCGM ≪T vir. The second concerns the radiative stabil-
ity of the CGM: even if SNe wind-induced shock heating
sustains T CGM ∼T vir, the CGM may still be radia-
tively unstable with tcool/tff ≲10 (McCourt et al. 2012;
Sharma et al. 2012; Voit et al. 2017), allowing continued
gas accretion and thus requiring an unrealistically high
ηM to keep the galaxy on the SHMR.
The notion of SNe wind-induced shock-heating the
CGM has been discussed in recent literature (Voit et al.
2015; Fielding et al. 2017; Pandya et al. 2023; Carr
et al. 2023). For example, in an idealized setup of
a M h ∼10 11 M⊙ halo, Fielding et al. (2017) demon-
strated that energy-loaded winds produced a shock-
heated CGM and mass-loaded winds produced a cool,
clumpy CGM. Voit et al. (2015) argue that low mass
halo CGMs should be similar to the behavior observed
and simulated at the galaxy cluster scale, where the
heating and cooling of gas surrounding the central black
hole regulates star-formation (Li et al. 2015; Donahue &
Voit 2022). In recent work, the time evolution of tcool/tff
was measured in Mh ∼10 12 M⊙ halos in FIRE-2 as part
of a process described as ‘outside-in virialization’ (Stern
et al. 2019, 2020, 2021). In FIRE-2, Stern et al. (2021)
found that SNe feedback is (eventually) able to increase
the inner CGM temperature to∼T vir and tcool/tff ∼10,
which is interestingly correlated with the transition from
bursty to steady star-formation and disk-formation.
Lastly, feedback can be traced in gas observables,
such as the kinetic and thermal Sunyaev-Zel’dovich ef-
fect (SZ; Moser et al. 2022), X-ray surface brightness
(XSB; Lau et al. 2025), fast-radio bursts (FRBs; Con-
nor et al. 2025), and HST/COS absorption. However,
the SZ effect and XSB are currently used to trace the
ionized gas in massive halos (M h ≳10 12 M⊙) and there
has been little work exploring the role of the electron
dispersion measure contribution from the low mass halo
population. HST/COS absorption, on the other hand,
could be used to help distinguish between ejective and
preventive feedback, where the temperature and baryon
content of the CGM will manifest itself in absorption
(although there will likely be some degeneracy). For ex-
ample, neutral hydrogen (Hi) is ubiquitously detected
to large radii and low metal ions (e.g., Cii, Ciii, Civ,
Siii, Siiii, Siiv) do not extend beyond 0.5 R vir (Bor-
doloi et al. 2014, 2018; Zheng et al. 2019, 2020, 2024).
In addition, Mishra et al. (2024) detected highly ion-
izedOviextending out to 1−2 R vir in a low-redshift,
star-forming dwarf galaxy sample. However, the origin
ofOviis unclear, but could be used to better under-
stand and constrain the underlying SNe-driven feedback
in∼10 10 −10 11 M⊙ halos.
There has been considerable progress simulating
Mh ∼10 10 −10 11 M⊙ halos in a cosmological context.
A number of zoom-in suites have characterized the star
formation histories, structural properties, and scaling re-
lations of field dwarfs in this mass range (Munshi et al.
2021; Tremmel et al. 2017; Shen et al. 2014; Applebaum
et al. 2021; Tomaru et al. 2025). Others have focused
more directly on the CGM, including ion column den-

4
sity distributions (Mina et al. 2021; Baumschlager et al.
2025) and the thermal structure of the halo gas (Tung
& Chen 2025; Cook et al. 2025). However, compara-
tively little attention has been paid to the time evolution
of the CGM thermal structure in this mass regime —
in particular, whether SNe-driven outflows can sustain
TCGM ∼T vir, drive t cool/tff ≳10 in the outer CGM,
and prevent gas accretion into the halo in the first place.
We use this paper to quantify several outstanding ques-
tions regarding feedback and the flow of energy in the
low-mass halo regime. In particular,
1. Can the CGM and nearby IGM be sustained at
∼T vir through SNe wind-induced shocks?
2. What is the specific energy of the outflow and more
broadly, what are the energy outflow and inflow
rates at the ISM boundary and CGM boundary?
3. Can these outflows entrain mass in the CGM (and
beyond) and prevent future gas accretion?
4. Can feedback drive the dwarf CGM to a radia-
tively stable regime, where t cool/tff ≳10, similar
to what is observed in more massive systems?
5. How can this model be tested through observa-
tions? For example, the column density profile of
highly ionizedOvi.
In this paper, we explore these five key questions us-
ing cosmological zoom-in simulations using the adaptive
mesh refinement codeenzo(Bryan et al. 2014) and a
discrete SNe feedback model where high-specific energy
winds penetrate a high-resolution CGM (Forbes et al.
2016; Goldbaum et al. 2015, 2016).
The paper is organized as follows. We first briefly
introduce the simulation setup and feedback model in
Section 2. In Section 3, we introduce the dwarf galaxy
sample and present our main results. We then dis-
cuss these findings in the context of other work in Sec-
tion 4. Finally, we conclude in Section 5 and discuss
the future work needed to build upon our findings.
Throughout this paper, we adopt the cosmological pa-
rameters presented in Planck Collaboration et al. (2020),
where H 0 = 67.4 km/s/Mpc, Ω Λ = 0.685, Ω m = 0.315,
and Ω b = 0.049. This corresponds to a cosmic baryon
fraction of f b ∼0.16.
2.METHODOLOGY
In order to understand the role of preventive feedback
in low-mass M h ∼10 10 −10 11 M⊙ halos, we are inter-
ested in a high resolution CGM where the SNe-driven
high specific energy winds are able to propagate. We
choose to use the structured adaptive mesh-refinement
(AMR) codeenzo(Bryan et al. 2014).enzois a Carte-
sian code designed to model astrophysical fluid flow, and
can be used in any dimension and scale. The collisionless
matter (e.g., dark matter and stars) is treated as a dis-
crete particle on the Cartesian grid that interacts with
the surrounding baryon content via gravity. AMR refers
to the use of an adaptive hierarchy of grids that can vary
spatial resolution based on the user-defined threshold;
therefore,enzocan achieve a large spatial dynamical
range. In the context of our simulation suite, we aim
to model both the small-scale star-formation and SNe
and large-scale cosmological gas flow. Inenzo, the fluid
equations are solved using a modified Godunov third-
order accurate piecewise parabolic method (Bryan et al.
1995). In addition, compared to a purely Lagrangian
method using a fixed particle mass,enzo, although in
general cells are refined based on baryon and dark mat-
ter overdensities, low mass cells with high specific energy
are permitted. Therefore,enzomay be more suitable
to better resolve both the high specific energy flows we
expect from SNe-driven feedback (see also the discussion
in Smith et al. 2024b).
enzoalso enables the implementation of heating and
cooling in our simulation suite using the standardized
chemistry and cooling library,grackle(Bryan et al.
2014; Smith et al. 2017).gracklecontains the heating
and cooling rates for primordial species, metal species,
and UV background photo-heating and photo-ionization
rates. These are calculated for the temperature and den-
sity range found in our simulation. We use a six-species
model: H, H +, He, He +, He ++, e − and model other
ionization species (e.g.,Ovi) using a photo-ionization
model (§3.9). We also use a time-dependent meta-
galactic radiation field with self-shielding (Haardt &
Madau 2012), beginning at z = 9 and ending at z = 0.

musicconstructs a bounding convex hull that encloses
only the ‘must-refine’ particles. These are a specialized
class of dark matter particles that force additional mesh
refinement in their local surroundings (specifically, the
eight nearest cells), up to a prescribed level beyond the
base zoom-in refinement. We set the minimum refine-
ment level of the must-refine particles equal to the high-
est level of the initial zoom-in refinement. Compared
to the traditional approach of refining a fixed Eulerian
zoom-in volume, the must-refine particle method offers
two key advantages. First, it prevents artificial collapse
and excessive refinement of structures outside the pri-
mary halo of interest. Second, it allows the refinement
region to follow the target halo as it moves through the
cosmological volume. This is particularly important for
low-mass halos, which can drift out of (and later re-
enter) a fixed zoom-in region due to large-scale velocity
flows.
Cells are refined based on both baryonic and dark
matter overdensity criteria. To ensure adequate reso-
lution in the CGM – which is significantly lower density
than the ISM – we increase the number of initial re-
finement levels to 3. For the 20 Mpc h −1 cosmological
box, the root grid has a resolution of 256 3 (1283 for
the 10 Mpc h −1 box). Therefore, in the 3 initial level
region, the initial spatial resolution is∼15 kpc. How-
ever, beyond this initial refinement configuration, gas
cells are allowed to refine up to 8 levels. This yields a
maximum spatial resolution of∼450 pc. We repeat one
zoom-in simulation with 4 initial levels of refinement,
corresponding to a CGM spatial resolution a factor of
∼2 smaller than our fiducial sample. Throughout this
paper, we refer to the high resolution simulation run as
M8-h (§3).

3.2.Present-Day Properties
The present-day galaxy properties are presented in Ta-
ble 1, where each dwarf is identified using the letter M
(for ‘Messere’ instead of ‘Messier’ object) followed by a
number corresponding to Mvir (ordered from most mas-
sive to least massive). In Table 1, we include M vir, Rvir,
MISM, M CGM, and M ∗. We define the outer bound-
ary of the ISM using 0.1 R vir(t), which is the definition
often adopted in other simulation work (e.g., Pandya
et al. 2020, 2023). In addition, we define the CGM as
the region contained between 0.1 R vir(t) and 1 R vir(t).
In particular, we use the time-dependent halo radius,
Rvir(t), which is calculated in our implementation of
rockstar(Behroozi et al. 2013a) andconsistent-
trees(Behroozi et al. 2013b), which is based on the
definition presented in Bryan & Norman (1998). The
merger tree enables us to smoothly track the time evolu-

It is important to note that the range of stellar mass
in our dwarf sample is high compared to the observed
SHMR. We show a direct comparison to Behroozi et al.

This discrepancy is likely due to the inability of the
feedback model to effectively heat the ISM gas at high
redshift (i.e. due to overcooling), resulting in too much
early star-formation. We put this into context using the
regulator model presented in Voit et al. (2024a,b). In
their model, Voit et al. (2024a) find that low mass halos
self-regulate through the expansion of their atmosphere
(i.e., CGM), where the SNe energy is able to couple
with the CGM gas. In agreement with the conclusion
from Carr et al. (2023), Voit et al. (2024a) find that the
SHMR is not sensitive to the mass loading of the outflow
and instead driven by the SNe energy, or energy loading
factor.

3.3.1.SNe-Induced Shock Heating in M8-h
To demonstrate an example of SNe-induced shock
heating, we show our high-resolution dwarf galaxy (M8-
h) at z = 0.28 in Figure 3. The left panel shows an edge-
on temperature slice (usingyt; Turk et al. 2011) extend-
ing out to∼3 R vir. We define the edge-on orientation
using the angular momentum vector of gas in the inner
halo (r/Rvir <0.25). For reference, the virial tempera-
ture of M8-h at z = 0.28 is T vir ∼10 4.9 K (Equation 8).
The upper-right panel shows the halo gas pressure out
to∼1 R vir. Instead of plotting the physical pressure,
we normalize the pressure slice by the volume-weighted
three-dimensional radial pressure profile in order to bet-
ter visualize the pressure contrast across the shock. The
lower-right panel shows the gas radial velocity relative to
the galaxy center. The radial velocity is calculated after
correcting for the bulk motion of the ISM (r/Rvir <0.1).
In M8-h, the ISM (r/Rvir <0.1) is cold, with the mass-
weighted temperature below 104 K (dark blue). Outside
the ISM, hot gas vents along the minor axis of the star-
forming disk into the surrounding CGM. This hot gas
is driven out of the ISM by SNe feedback and produces
multiple shock fronts at small radii. The shocks are most
clearly visible at larger radii; for example, a previous
episode of star formation and subsequent SNe produced
the shock at∼1 R vir, where the CGM interior to the
shock has been heated to∼T vir and above (dark red).
Although the shock has likely cooled since first breaking
through the star-forming disk and reaching its current
radial extent, it remains well above T vir (≳10 6 K).
The same shock front at∼1 R vir can be seen in the
upper-right panel in Figure 3, where a pressure en-
hancement appears along the shock edge, surrounded
by the lower-pressure CGM and IGM. The lower-right
panel further shows that the radial velocity at the shock
front is directed away from the galaxy center (50 to
100 km s−1). Interestingly, the inner CGM (≲0.3 R vir)
is dominated by net inflow (−10 to−50 km s −1), likely
fueling future star formation as it reaches the ISM.
3.3.2.Volume-Weighted Temperature Evolution
We selected this snapshot (z = 0.28) specifically to
highlight SNe-driven shock propagation on both small
scales (near the star-forming disk) and large scales (near
the halo boundary). More generally, we expect the CGM
properties of our sample to evolve over time, since each
galaxy undergoes a unique merger history and resides
in a different environment. To understand this evolu-
tion and to better place M8-h at this particular snap-
shot (Figure 3) in the context of our full galaxy sample,
we include the time evolution of the gas temperature
in Figure 4. In particular, we calculate the volume-
weighted temperature of the ISM (r <0.1 R vir), CGM
(0.1 Rvir < r <1 R vir), and IGM (1 R vir < r <2 R vir).
We note that this isnotthe mass-weighted temperature
and therefore the ISM temperature will be much higher
than typical dense, star-forming gas in the ISM. How-
ever, the volume-weighted temperature is better able
to capture the hot SNe-driven outflows from the ISM,
which is the main focus of this work.
In the three upper-left panels in Figure 4, we show
the median fiducial sample temperature evolution in
the ISM, CGM, and IGM. In the CGM, we de-
fine three different mean temperature regions, aver-
aged over different regions of the halo: (1) T CGM,1.0
(0.1−1 R vir), (2) T CGM,0.5 (0.1−0.5 R vir), and (3)
TCGM,0.25 (0.1−0.25 R vir). The median profiles are use-
ful for understanding the typical temperature behavior;
however, in order to see the temporal behavior of the
temperature in a single halo, we also show, in the upper-
right panels of Figure 4, the temperature evolution of the
M8-h halo as the solid blue lines and its T vir(t) as the
dashed black lines.
During the Figure 3 outflow, the M8-h ISM tempera-
ture in Figure 4 shows two peaks just before∼10 Gyr,
the second reaching∼10 7 K, before declining rapidly.
This SNe event then propagates outward into the CGM
and with a time delay of∼0.6 Gyr, corresponding to a
shock velocity of∼98 km s −1 (assuming Rvir ∼60 kpc),
consistent with the radial velocities shown in Figure 3.
The IGM temperature peaks precisely at the vertical
line, reflecting the coincidence of its inner boundary with
the outer shock at∼1 R vir.
We find that the ISM temperature in M8-h is sus-
tained near∼T vir(t). We note again that this is
the volume-weighted temperature, which is dominated
by volume-filling gas near the outer ISM boundary
(∼0.1 R vir). The mass-weighted temperature is below
∼10 4 K, since most of the mass is in the cold phase.
The interpretation of the temperature being sustained
at∼T vir is less straightforward in the CGM. Although
the CGM temperature reaches∼10 6 K during strong
SNe bursts (Figure 3 and Figure 4), the CGM gradually
cools (adiabatically and radiatively) just below T vir(t).
This is most clear during the time period t = [7,10] Gyr
in M8-h, where the volume-weighted CGM temperature
falls to∼3×10 4 K. This apparent cooling is, however, a
consequence of the strong (negative) radial temperature
dependence at late times (a result which is seen gener-

At early times in the fiducial sample (red; lower-left),
the temperature profile increases from the center until

3.4.Flow of Energy
We have thus far demonstrated that the CGM of our
low-mass halo sample is sustained at∼T vir(t) through
SNe-induced shock heating, despite being at the peak of
the cooling curve. In§1, we suggested that, compared
to a Lagrangian approach with fixed mass resolution,
Eulerian-based codes (e.g.,enzo) may better capture
the propagation of high-specific-energy winds from the
ISM into the CGM and beyond (see Smith et al. 2024b,
for more discussion). In this section, we quantify both
the specific energy of the outflows in the ISM boundary
and more generally, the flow of energy between the ISM,
CGM, and IGM.
3.4.1.Specific Energy of Flows
To quantify the specific energy of our SNe-induced
outflows, we first separate the gas by temperature. We
follow Li & Bryan (2020), where we define warm gas as
T≤3×10 5 K and hot gas as T>3×10 5 K. This warm
gas definition will trace gas at T vir and below. In com-
parison, the hot gas definition will only include gas well
above Tvir, which will be the result of SNe-driven feed-
back. We additionally require the gas to be outflowing
(vr >0 km s−1), where v r is the bulk-velocity–corrected
radial velocity relative to the halo center of mass. Since
we are interested here in gas flowing out of the ISM, we
further restrict our selection to gas at the ISM boundary,
defined as a shell between 0.1−0.2 R vir.
The specific energy is calculated from the sum of the
kinetic and thermal energy components of gas cells that
satisfy these criteria, separated by temperature. The
specific energy (Equation 3) can be interpreted as the
ratio of the energy outflow rate to the mass outflow rate,
or equivalently the ratio of the energy and mass loading
factors (since E SN/m∗ is constant in our simulation).
We calculate the median specific energy for both the
warm and hot components at each simulation output.
The results are shown in Figure 5. The solid red line
represents the median specific energy of the hot com-
ponent, while the shaded red region is bound by the
minimum and maximum value across the sample. The
solid blue line and shaded blue region show the same
quantities for the warm component.
Following Li & Bryan (2020), we compare the wind
specific energy to the gravitational potential of the dark
matter halo to determine whether the outflow can es-
cape. This comparison uses the scaling relation

3.5.Flow of Mass
We next track the flow of mass in our sample. As
discussed in§1, the flow of energy and mass are comple-
mentary. For example, high resolution tall-box simula-
tions find that the hot phase carries only a small fraction
of the total mass but most of the energy (§3.4). In ad-
dition, the flow of mass measured as different radii can
indicate how much mass is ‘swept’ up in outflows or how
much gas is prevented from entering the halo.
3.5.1.Mass Flow Rate
To measure the mass flow rates, we use the same def-
inition as the energy flow rate (Equation 10), but with

Figure 8 (with a format analogous to Figure 6).
In general, the behavior of the mass flow evolution is
similar to the energy flow (Figure 6). In the upper-left
panel, we see large variations in the CGM to ISM mass
flow, with ˙Mout,ISM ∼0.1−1 M ⊙ yr−1 for t≲5 Gyr be-

after∼5 Gyr. In comparison, ˙Min,ISM remains fairly
steady, with a slow decline, and is nearly always higher
than ˙Mout,ISM (although again we note that the com-

A similar behavior is seen in ˙Mout,CGM and ˙Min,CGM
in the upper right-panel. For reference, we add
the expected baryon accretion rate ( ˙Min,b(t)) based
on the mean and median dark matter accretion rate
( ˙Min,DM(t)) of our fiducial sample. Since we track the
dark matter halo mass, we approximate the time deriva-
tive as the difference between neighboring simulation
output and ignore any timestep where the halo mass
is lowered. We then multiply ˙Min,DM(t) by fb ∼0.16.
We find that, in the early Universe, the median
˙Min,CGM and ˙Min,b(t) trace each other quite closely.

is prevention in action. We calculatef prev, the frac-

We next show the M8-h mass flow evolution in the

3.5.2.Mass Entrainment
Turning to a measure of the amount of mass entrained
as the outflows sweep over the CGM, we show in Fig-
ure 9 the ratio of ˙Mout,CGM to ˙Mout,ISM for each halo
in the fiducial sample and M8-h. In order to show the
overall trend in these noisy data, we report the median
and 16th/84th percentile ˙MCGM,out/ ˙MISM,out, smoothed
with a Gaussian filter of widthσ= 10 timesteps
(∼400 Myr). We find that, before∼5 Gyr, for the typ-
ical halo in our sample, ˙MCGM,out/ ˙MISM,out >1, sug-
gesting that a large fraction of the mass in the CGM
is entrained by the outflows. However, after∼5 Gyr,
˙MCGM,out/ ˙MISM,out is more consistent with∼1. This is
directly related to the CGM mass and baryon fraction,
discussed in§3.7.

3.7.Baryon Fraction
We now examine the CGM baryon fraction (fCGM) in
our dwarf sample. Since the stellar mass in the CGM is
negligible,f CGM is merely the CGM gas fraction. Be-
cause the gravitational potentials of these low-mass ha-
los are shallow, the CGM baryon fraction can serve as a
measure of feedback efficiency. For example, weak feed-
back (i.e., slow winds, fixedη M) may produce a baryon-
rich CGM, where gas is recycled and unable to leave the
halo. In contrast, strong feedback (i.e., fast winds, fixed
ηM) may remove gas from the CGM, leaving behind a
baryon-depleted halo. However, a similarly gas-depleted
CGM can also arise in a preventive feedback scenario,
where gas is prevented from accreting onto the halo in
the first place. We define the CGM baryon fraction as
the total gas mass between 0.1–1 R vir divided by the
halo mass, M vir, and normalize this value by the cos-
mic baryon fraction (f b). In the absence of feedback,
we therefore expectf CGM ∼1, where the gas traces the
dark matter.

4.DISCUSSION
Taken together, our results provide a coherent pic-
ture of energy and mass transport in dwarf galaxy ha-
los, where SNe-driven feedback drivesf CGM down and
brings the CGM into a radiatively stable regime. In
this section, we first connect our results and discuss the
physical picture that emerges and how it is related to
the transition in a variety of properties around∼5 Gyr
(§4.1). We then discuss our results in the context of
other work, including a comparison to the outflow spe-
cific energy, temperature evolution, ‘inner CGM viri-
alization’, mass entrainment, and the baryon extent
(§4.2), before discussing potential caveats of our sim-
ulation suite (§4.3).

The thermal and structural state of the CGM reflects
this evolution. In the early Universe, frequent SNe
bursts heat the outer CGM while the inner CGM re-
mains relatively cool, and the entire halo sits in a ra-
diatively unstable regime (t cool/tff ≲10). However, as
outflows progressively remove gas and lower the CGM
density, the cooling time increases relative to the free-
fall time, pushing the halo toward radiative stability. By
∼3–7 Gyr, this manifests as the bulk of the CGM being
‘lifted’ above the precipitation threshold (tcool/tff ≳10),
with only the innermost regions (r/Rvir ≲0.40) remain-
ing susceptible to cooling.
Simultaneously, as halo growth slows after∼5 Gyr –
with∼90% of the present-day mass already in place
– the frequency of merger-driven SNe bursts decreases,
and ˙Eout,ISM begins to exceed ˙Ecool,CGM for a larger frac-
tion of time. The inner CGM is sustained near T vir,
baryon accretion from the IGM is suppressed by∼75%,
andf CGM has nearly plateaued – the CGM is left in a
hot, diffuse, and radiatively stable configuration that is
in equilibrium at late times.
4.2.Comparison to Previous Work
With this physical picture in mind, we now place our
findings in the context of other work. We begin by com-
paring the temperature evolution of the CGM to other
simulations (§4.2.1), followed by a comparison of energy
flow (§4.2.3,§4.2.4), and mass flow (§4.2.5). We then
discuss the radiative stability of our halos in the context
of ‘inner CGM virialization’ (§4.2.6), and conclude with
a discussion of the closure radius (§4.2.7) and compari-
son with recent observational work (§4.2.8).
4.2.1.Temperature
In§3.3, we demonstrated that the CGM is contin-
uously heated to T vir by episodic SNe-induced shock-
heating of the halo. A similar behavior is seen in the
IGM (1−2 R vir), although the gas only temporarily ex-
ceeds Tvir before cooling on a∼Gyr timescale.

24
Fielding et al. (2017) report the mass-weighted tem-
perature time evolution of their idealized Mh ∼10 11 M⊙
halo for both a low and high mass-loading (ηM) feedback
model, corresponding to high and low energy per unit
mass, respectively. Although their halo is more massive
than our sample, Fielding et al. (2017) find that the low
ηM model (characterized by low mass-loading and high
wind velocity) is able to similarly sustain a larger frac-
tion of the halo at 10 5 −10 6 K compared to their high
ηM model (high mass-loading and low wind velocity).
Fielding et al. (2017) also report the radial tempera-
ture profile of their Mh ∼10 11 M⊙ halo (see their Figure
7). In the highη M case, the CGM is kept well below
Tvir (∼1.2×10 5 K) and reaches a peak temperature
at∼0.25 R vir. However, in the lowη M case, a better
match to the high specific energy of our outflows, the
temperature profile is flatter and is sustained near T vir
between 0.1−0.5 R vir. We find a qualitatively similar
result in our work. In the volume-weighted tempera-
ture profile of our sample (Figure 4), the temperature
peaks at∼0.25 R vir at z∼2 (below T vir), and the peak
gradually migrates inward to∼0.1 R vir by z∼0 (above
Tvir). At z∼0, our temperature profile exceeds T vir out
to∼1 R vir, somewhat higher than Fielding et al. (2017),
but note that Fielding et al. (2017) averaged their tem-
perature over∼6 Gyr, whereas we report the median
over∼0.5 Gyr bins.
The picture is quite different in the FIRE-2 simula-
tion suite. In Stern et al. (2021), the radial temperature
profile of∼10 10 −10 11 M⊙ halos peaks at∼0.5 R vir at
z = 0, with the peak volume-weighted temperature just
below T vir and the rest of the CGM remaining below
Tvir (see their Figure 3). This is similar to the highη M
halo in Fielding et al. (2017). Interestingly, the time evo-
lution of T/Tvir in our work more closely resembles the
Milky Way-mass sample (Mh ∼10 12 M⊙) in Stern et al.
(2021) than their low-mass sample. For example, in the
FIRE-2 m12i halo, the radial volume-weighted temper-
ature peak occurs at∼0.5 R vir below Tvir at z∼1 and
migrates inward to∼0.1 R vir above Tvir by z∼0 (see
their Figure 2). 3 This inward migration of the temper-
ature peak and heating of the inner CGM above T vir is
consistent with what we find in our lower mass galaxy
sample (Figure 4).

and EAGLE. Wright et al. (2024) report the me-
dian CGM temperature profile in the halo mass range
log10(M200c/M⊙)∈[10.75,11.75], which overlaps with
the upper end of our sample (Table 1). At z∼2, both

4.2.3.Energy Flow Rate
We now turn to the energy flow rate. In§3.4.2,
we demonstrated that although infrequent, SNe-induced
outflows are able to drive ˙Eout,ISM to 1041 −10 42 erg s−1,
with the largest and most frequent outbursts occurring
at high redshift, likely due to an elevated merger rate.
Turning to comparisons with other work, Pandya et al.
(2021, 2023) compute ˙Eout,ISM and ˙Eout,CGM in FIRE-2,
finding that, for their M h ∼10 10 M⊙ sample, ˙Eout,ISM
and ˙Eout,CGM drop off very quickly (see Figure 11 in
Pandya et al. 2023). In comparison, their Mh ∼10 11 M⊙
sample is broadly consistent with our work in both the
typical ˙E value and its bursty, redshift-dependent be-
havior. Oren et al. (2026) perform a similar calcula-
tion in TNG100, computing ˙Eout,ISM and ˙Eout,CGM as
a function of M 200c (see their Figure 13); their median
values are consistent with what we find though we re-
frain from a more direct comparison since their results
aren’t smoothed on a similar timescale.
In their regulator model, Carr et al. (2023) report
the ˙E time evolution for both a M h = 1010 M⊙ and
Mh = 1011 M⊙ halo, where ˙Ewind, ˙Eout, ˙Ein, and ˙Ecool
are equivalent to our ˙Eout,ISM, ˙Eout,CGM, ˙Ein,CGM, and
˙Ecool,CGM, respectively. Our results are significantly
higher than their Mh = 1010 M⊙ halo, so we restrict the
comparison to their M h = 1011 M⊙ halo. There, our
˙Eout,ISM, ˙Eout,CGM, and ˙Ein,CGM fall below their ˙Ewind,
˙Eout, and ˙Ein, respectively, suggesting that our outflows
carry lower specific energy than assumed in the Carr
et al. (2023) model and therefore propagate less effi-
ciently (see also§4.2.5).

in the first∼5 Gyr, the cumulative E out,ISM exceeds
Ecool,CGM, highlighting the importance of infrequent yet

their M h ∼10 10 −10 11 M⊙ halos is higher (and bursts

halo, but ˙Ecool,CGM > ˙Eout,ISM at all model outputs.

4.2.5.Mass Flow Rate
In§3.5, we reported the mass flow rate and found that
there is a significant decrease in ˙Min and ˙Mout (in both
the ISM and CGM boundary) at∼5 Gyr. We now com-
pare these findings to other literature. As discussed in
Wright et al. (2024), mass flow rates provide a more
direct basis for comparison across the literature than
mass-loading factors, which depend on the star forma-
tion rate and therefore vary with halo mass. Wright
et al. (2024) present a like-for-like comparison of inflow
and outflow rates normalized by M 200c ×Ω b/Ωm as a
function of radius, down to M200c ∼10 10.5 M⊙. For con-
sistency, we adopt their M 200c ∼10 10.8 M⊙ result and
convert to M ⊙ yr−1 (Figure 8). We find that our ˙Min
and ˙Mout results more closely resemble EAGLE than
IllustrisTNG (in both the ISM and CGM boundary).
In terms of the mass outflow rate, our median
˙Mout,ISM ∼0.1 M ⊙ yr−1 is similar to EAGLE and sig-
nificantly lower than IllustrisTNG. However, unlike EA-
GLE, we do not find ˙Mout,CGM > ˙Mout,ISM at z∼0.
Wright et al. (2024) interpret this regime in EAGLE
as evidence for swept-up material in outflows (see also
Mitchell et al. 2020b), whereas IllustrisTNG is charac-
terized by strong, efficient recycling within the CGM.
In our simulation suite, ˙Mout,CGM > ˙Mout,ISM only at
early times (≲5 Gyr; Figure 9), and their ratio is ap-
proximately unity afterwards. This suggests that en-
trainment is most efficient at early times in our work,
consistent with the gradual decline in M CGM that we
measure (Figure 11). In addition, since so much material
has been entrained in the outflows at early times, there
simply isn’t much CGM material left to sweep up after
the first∼5 Gyr. This will naturally result in a lower
˙M compared to a gas-rich CGM (e.g., IllustrisTNG).
In terms of the mass inflow rate at the halo bound-
ary, Wright et al. (2024) find that at z∼0 IllustrisTNG
prevents∼50% of accretion (f prev ∼0.5), whereas EA-
GLE prevents∼90% (f prev ∼0.1). Oren et al. (2026)
find a similar result in their TNG100 analysis at the
halo-boundary (see their Figure 8). In general, EA-
GLE shows strong suppression of the inflow rate at both
CGM and ISM boundaries, while IllustrisTNG exhibits
a higher inflow rate, especially at the ISM boundary
where there is heavy recycling. In our work, we com-
pute an analogousf prev and find substantial scatter (al-
though near unity) at early times, followed by conver-
gence tof prev ∼0.25 after∼5 Gyr, corresponding to
∼75% suppression. This is consistent with EAGLE. In
FIRE-2, Pandya et al. (2020) measure ˙Min,gas/ ˙Min,DM
at the halo boundary and also findf prev ∼0.1 (see their
Figure 14).
In their regulator model, Carr et al. (2023) re-
port the time evolution of ˙M for their M h = 1011 M⊙
halo, where ˙Mwind, ˙Mout, and ˙Min correspond to
our ˙Mout,ISM, ˙Mout,CGM, and ˙Min,CGM, respectively
(see their Figure 7). At the ISM boundary, our
˙Mout,ISM is significantly higher than Carr et al. (2023)

at early times (∼0.1−1 M ⊙ yr−1 at≲5 Gyr) before set-

4.2.6.Radiative Stability
In the upper two panels in Figure 10, we demonstrated
that tcool/tff exceeding∼10 in the outer CGM occurs
around∼3−7 Gyr. Stern et al. (2021) identified a
related process in their FIRE-2 M h ∼10 12 −10 13 M⊙
sample, which they term ‘inner CGM virialization’. In
their framework, the outer halo (∼0.5 R vir) is already
heated to∼T vir, followed by a relatively brief phase
during which the inner CGM (∼0.1 R vir) is also raised
to∼T vir. During this transition, t cool grows to ex-
ceed tff (tcool/tff ∼2−10), a large fraction of the inner
CGM becomes subsonic, and the pressure distribution
becomes more spherically symmetric. This transition is
also associated with a shift from bursty to steady star-
formation and the emergence of a rotation-dominated
disk. In their FIRE-2 sample, m12i undergoes this
transition at z∼0.3, m12b at z∼0.7, and m13A1 at
z∼3.5. 4 We find that our results better resemble these
halos than the 1010 −10 11 M⊙ halos in FIRE-2, in both
the temperature and t cool/tff evolution.
Stern et al. (2021) conclude that no ‘inner CGM
virialization’ occurs in halos with M h ≲10 12 M⊙ (see
their Figure 17). In their z = 0 sample, halos with
Mh ∼5×10 10 M⊙ have tcool/tff ∼0.2−0.7 at 0.1 R vir,
whereas we find t cool/tff ∼4 at the same radius
(Figure 10). At z∼2, Stern et al. (2021) report
tcool/tff ∼0.04−0.1, while we find t cool/tff ≳1 since at
least this time. Stern et al. (2021) suggest that their low
tcool/tff values may be the result of the FIRE-2 CGM
gas mass being too high. For example, El-Badry et al.
(2018a) find a CGM mass exceeding the combined cold
gas and stellar mass by factors of several, whereas in
our sample the CGM mass is comparable to or smaller
than the ISM gas mass (Table 1). Similarly, Hafen et al.
(2019) reportf CGM ∼0.15−0.30 at z = 0.25, compared
to∼0.01−0.15 in our simulations (Figure 11).
Stern et al. (2021) further note that if ‘inner CGM
virialization’ is linked to disk formation, its occurrence

4.2.8.Absorption Line Comparison
We calculate the column density profiles of ions in Fig-
ure 13 and find general agreement with the archival and
new detections reported in Zheng et al. (2024). In addi-
tion, we find general agreement with the EAGLE predic-
tions presented in Zheng et al. (2024), where there is de-

beyond 2 Rvir. There are a few possible reasons for this
difference. First, we do not account for the radiation
from the stars themselves, which will likely only affect
Oviclose to the disk. Second, the Mishra et al. (2024)
sample is star-forming out to z∼0.7, whereas our sam-
ple does not have this selection bias (and is at an overall
lower star formation rate by z∼0.7). It is also possible
that the highOvicovering fraction at large radii arise
from the cooling of the SNe-induced shock propagation
in our work that isn’t captured in the z∼0 median pro-
file for each halo in Figure 13. In addition, we aren’t
able to resolve theOviat the interface of clouds and the
surrounding medium (radiative turbulent mixing layers;
Fielding & Bryan 2022). We also assume ionization equi-
librium in thecloudyoutput we use fromtrident.
In a recent analysis, Piacitelli et al. (2025) found that
their 64 isolated dwarfs from the Marvel-ous Dwarfs
(Munshi et al. 2021) and Marvelous Massive Dwarfs

4.3.Caveats of our Simulation Suite
There are several important caveats to note about
our zoom-in simulation suite. The most significant
is that, as discussed in§3.2, our stellar masses are
too high compared to the observed SHMR (Moster
et al. 2010; Behroozi et al. 2019). We find that
M∗/Mh ∼10 −2 (z∼0) atz∼0, roughly an order
of magnitude above the Behroozi et al. (2019) median
for a M h ∼5×10 10 M⊙ halo. This excess stellar mass
is formed at early times, pointing to inefficient ISM
feedback at high redshift and the well-known overcool-
ing problem (Wise et al. 2012; Stinson et al. 2013).
Although M8-h includes one additional level of ini-
tial CGM refinement, the ISM resolution remains fixed
across all runs, and we find no significant improvement
in M∗/Mh at higher CGM resolution. This suggests the
overcooling originates at ISM rather than CGM scales,
and future work should explore higher ISM resolution
alongside improved early feedback prescriptions such as
radiation pressure or early stellar winds.
While the high stellar mass implies a higher integrated
SNe energy budget, the lower amount that flows into
the CGM (after ISM cooling) is commensurate with the
expected energy input of a correctly placed stellar com-
ponent. Therefore, we argue that the key qualitative re-
sults – shock heating of the CGM to∼T vir, hot-phase
energy dominance, and the transition to radiative stabil-
ity – are robust to moderate changes in the star forma-
tion rate. Nevertheless, a simulation suite that correctly
reproduces the SHMR will be needed to place our find-
ings on a fully quantitative footing, and we defer this to
future work.
A second caveat concerns our sample size of ten halos,
which limits our ability to characterize halo-to-halo scat-
ter and to identify systematic trends with mass or as-
sembly history within the M h ∼10 10 −10 11 M⊙ range.
Finally, our OVI column densities fall short of recent
observations. As discussed in§4.2.8, this likely reflects
a combination of missing stellar radiation fields, the as-
sumption of ionization equilibrium in ourcloudyta-

29
bles, and the inability to resolve radiative turbulent mix-
ing layers at the cloud-CGM interface (Fielding & Bryan
2022).
5.SUMMARY & CONCLUSION
In this work, we investigate the role of SNe-driven out-
flows in regulating the thermodynamic structure of the
CGM in M h ∼10 10 −10 11 M⊙ halos. We run a suite
of cosmological zoom-in simulations with the adaptive
mesh refinement codeenzo, combined with a discrete
SNe feedback model, and simulate ten halos down to
z= 0. We uniquely resolve and track the flow of energy
and mass using high cadence outputs every∼42 Myr
(332 total outputs for each zoom-in). This enabled us
to capture the energy and mass flow between the ISM,
CGM, and IGM and explore how this is directly related
to other quantities, including the CGM cooling rate,
fCGM, and the t cool/tff profile.
Our main conclusions are as follows:
1.SNe wind-induced shock heating sustains
the CGM of dwarf galaxies at∼T vir.We
find that episodic SNe outbursts drive shock fronts
that propagate from the star-forming disk outward
through the CGM and into the IGM. In particular,
the inner CGM (≲0.5 R vir) is continuously main-
tained near T vir by z∼0, while the outer CGM
and IGM are temporarily heated before cooling
on∼Gyr timescales (Figures 3 and 4).
2.Hot outflows dominate the energy budget;
warm outflows dominate the mass budget.
We find that the hot outflows (T>3×10 5 K)
have a specific energy∼10×larger than the warm
phase (T≤3×10 5 K) and consistently exceed the
halo escape threshold, driving outflows and pre-
venting inflows at both the CGM and IGM scale
(Figure 5). Although hot outflows are present for
a smaller fraction of time compared to the warm
outflows, the cumulative energy of the hot outflows
entering the CGM from the ISM is∼5−10×
more than the warm outflows. However, the cu-
mulative mass in warm outflows is∼5−10×more
than the hot outflows (Figures 6 and 8).
3.Early evolution dominated by infrequent
yet large SNe outflows and mass entrain-
ment.Although ˙Ecool,CGM > ˙Eout,ISM for a larger
fraction of time at early times, the infrequent yet
powerful ˙Eout,ISM outflows quickly dominate the
cumulative energy placed into the CGM compared
to the cumulative CGM cooling rate within the
first∼5 Gyr (Figure 7). During this time pe-
riod, we also find ˙Mout,CGM > ˙Mout,ISM, demon-
strating that outflows are able to ‘sweep’ up CGM
and IGM material and gradually lower the CGM
baryon mass, traced using either M gas orf CGM
(Figures 9 and 11).
4.SNe feedback drives the CGM toward a ra-
diatively stable regime after∼5 Gyr.At
early times, the highf CGM is able to maintain the
CGM in the t cool/tff ≪10, despite ongoing SNe-
induced shock heating. However, during the time
period∼3−7 Gyr, the decline inf CGM and in-
crease in t cool drive tcool/tff to∼10 in the outer
CGM and nearby IGM (r/R vir ∼0.4−1.5). In
contrast, the inner CGM (r/R vir ∼0.1−0.4) is
maintained at tcool/tff ∼1−10 (Figure 10).
5.There is a transition at∼5 Gyr where the
dwarf galaxy CGM enters an equilibrium.
We find that after 5−8 Gyr,f CGM is driven down
from near unity to∼0.1 and∼75% of the ex-
pected baryon accretion rate is prevented (Fig-
ure 11). This transition is also connected to
heavy mass entrainment in the outflows (which in-
evitably drive downfCGM) and when the CGM be-
comes radiatively stable. Importantly, leading up
to this transition is also when the cumulative SNe
outflow energy rate begins to exceed the cumu-
lative CGM cooling rate and the baryon binding
energy (see§4.1 for a more detailed discussion).
6.HIand low ions agree with observations
but OVIis underestimated.While our HIand
low-ionization ion (e.g., SiII, SiIII, SiIV, CII, CIV)
column density profiles broadly agree with obser-
vations (Johnson et al. 2017; Zheng et al. 2024),
our predicted OVIcovering fraction falls short of
Mishra et al. (2024) (Figure 13).
In future work, it would be interesting to further in-
vestigate SNe-induced shock-heating in low mass halos
and its role in driving the CGM to a radiatively stable
regime. In particular, this would include incorporating
tracer particles inenzoto better estimate the gas that
is on first infall versus gas that has been recycled. In ad-
dition, it would be useful to increase the sample size to
get a better sense of the halo-to-halo scatter, especially
when comparing to IllustrisTNG and EAGLE.
Lastly, it would be worthwhile to make a more direct
comparison to previous simulation work. For example,
examining how the tcool/tff profile evolves as a function
