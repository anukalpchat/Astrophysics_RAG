ABSTRACT
Identification and/or association of unidentifiedγ-ray sources with lower-energy counterparts represents a key
challenge in modern astronomy, due to the relatively large positional uncertainty provided byγ-ray detectors.
We selected Unidentified Fermi Objects (UFOs) positionally compatible with hard X-ray sources in the latest
PalermoSwift-BAT hard X-ray Catalog and in the SRG/ART-XC all-sky X-ray survey Catalog, to identify
lower-energy sources and possibly associate them to the UFOs.
We found 17 UFOs with overlapping hard X-ray sources. We then collected soft X-ray data fromSwift-XRT,
Chandra-ACIS,XMM-Newton-EPIC, and eROSITA, identified 16 soft X-ray counterparts to the hard X-ray
sources, and associate 15 with known astronomical objects, classified as: blazars/blazars candidates (2 sources),
Seyfert galaxies (5 sources), X-ray binaries (2 sources), generic X-ray sources (1 source), cataclysmic variables
(2 sources), and variable stars (3 sources).
Blazars and Seyfert galaxies are likely lower-energy counterparts to the UFOs, since their mid-IR colors and
broad-band spectral energy distributions suggest significant jetted, non-thermal emission. X-ray binaries can
be potential lower-energy counterparts to the UFOs, since this class of sources has been already observed to
emitγ-rays. The generic X-ray source has been proposed as a pulsar candidate, and we therefore suggest that
it can be the lower-energy counterpart to the UFO. Cataclysmic variables have been suggested as potentialγ-
ray emitters so, if confirmed, the 2 sources classified as cataclysmic variables would represent the firstγ-ray
emitting sources of this kind. Finally, we consider the association of the 3 variable stars with the UFOs unlikely.

1.INTRODUCTION
Since the launch in April 1991 of theCompton Gamma Ray Observatory(CGRO) with its Energetic Gamma Ray Experiment
Telescope (EGRET; Hughes et al. 1980; Kanbach et al. 1989; Thompson et al. 1993; Esposito et al. 1999), one of the most
important challenges has been the study of unidentified/unassociatedγ-ray sources. In fact, about 46% of the 188 sources listed
in the revised EGRET catalog (Casandjian & Grenier 2008) are unassociated to lower-energy counterparts.
TheFermiGamma-ray Space Telescope, launched in June 2008 dramatically increased the number of detectedγ-ray sources.
As a matter of fact, the latest release of the Large Area Telescope (LAT) 14-year Source Catalog (4FGL-DR4, Ballet et al. 2023;
Abdollahi et al. 2022) lists 7194γ-ray sources detected at significance>4σ. However,∼34% of the sources listed in 4FGL-
DR4 catalog (2423 sources) are not yet identified or likely associated with a lower-energy counterpart. These Unidentified Fermi
Objects (UFOs) are almost equally found at low (|b|<15 ◦) (1352 sources,∼56%) and high (|b| ≥15 ◦) Galactic latitudes (1071
sources,∼44%).

In general, theγ-ray sky is dominated by blazars (Abdo et al. 2010a), a rare class of radio-loud active galactic nuclei (AGNs),
launching relativistic jets closely aligned with our line of sight (Blandford & Rees 1978; Blandford et al. 2019; Hovatta &
Lindfors 2019). As a matter of fact, blazars (or blazar candidates) represent∼71% of the high Galactic latitude 4FGL sources,
and∼55% of the whole catalog. Therefore, we expect UFOs outside the Galactic plane to be dominated by blazars, although
a contribution from Galactic sources (e.g., Reimer 2001) including pulsars (e.g., Nolan et al. 2012) is also expected. On the
other hand, we expect the majority of low Galactic latitude UFOs to be associated with Galactic sources, like molecular clouds,
supernova remnants, massive stars, OB associations, pulsars, transitional pulsars, pulsar wind nebulae, or X-ray binaries (see
e.g., Kaaret & Cottam 1996; GLAST Facility Science Team et al. 1999; Casanova et al. 2010; Tam et al. 2010; Ackermann et al.
2013; Dermer & Powale 2013; de Martino et al. 2013).
Several attempts towards characterization and possibly identification/association of UFOs have been carried out, through ob-
servations in radio bands (e.g., Mahony et al. 2010; Petrov et al. 2013), near infrared (e.g., Stern et al. 2005; Eckart et al. 2010;
Park et al. 2010; Stern et al. 2012; D’Abrusco et al. 2013), and soft X-rays (e.g., Kaur et al. 2019, 2021), as well as with statistical
approaches (e.g. Ackermann et al. 2012b) and machine learning methods (Liodakis & Blinov 2019; Cooper et al. 2023; Kaur et
al. 2023). Recently, Mayer & Becker (2024) built a probabilistic catalog of possible pulsar and/or blazar type X-ray counterparts
to UFOs from the internal eRASS:4 single-band source catalog, using a Bayesian cross-matching scheme.
The hard X-ray (that is, at energies≳10 keV) sky has been observed since the launch of the first X-ray telescope Uhuru in
1972 (Giacconi et al. 1979), and subsequently by the High Energy Astronomy Observatory (HEAO) A4 detector on board of the
HEAO-1 mission (Rothschild et al. 1979), the INTErnational Gamma-Ray Astrophysics Laboratory (INTEGRAL, Winkler et al.
2003) and its Imager on Board the INTEGRAL Satellite (IBIS Ubertini et al. 2003), the Burst Alert Telescope (BAT, Barthelmy
2004) on board of the Neil GehrelsSwiftObservatory (Gehrels et al. 2004), and the and the Mikhail Pavlinsky Astronomical
Roentgen Telescope–X-ray Concentrator (ART-XC, Pavlinsky et al. 2021) on board of the Spektrum-Roentgen-Gamma (SRG)
orbital observatory (Sunyaev et al. 2021).
Association studies of hard X-ray and gamma-ray in AGNs have been performed in the past focusing on Seyfer galaxies (Teng
et al. 2011; Ackermann et al. 2012a; Liu et al. 2025), while a cross-match between the 105 monthSwift-BAT catalog (Oh et al.
2018) and the second release of the fourth Fermi-LAT Source Catalog (4FGL-DR2, Ballet et al. 2020) has been performed by
Tsuji et al. (2021) finding 156 matches for point-like source and 31 extended sources.
In this work we focus on UFOs with one (or more) hard X-ray counterparts listed in the 100-month Palermo BAT Catalog
(4PBC, Cusumano et al. 2014) and/or in SRG/ART-XC all-sky X-ray survey Catalog (Sazonov et al. 2024). We refine the
position of these X-ray counterparts (with hard X-ray positional errors ranging from∼15 ′′ to a few arcminutes) making use of
soft X-ray data either through archival pointed observations or through the SRG/eROSITA all-sky survey (with positional errors
of a few arcseconds), and collect additional multi-frequency data for these sources in the attempt to characterize them and identify
possible lower-energy counterparts to the UFOs.
The paper is organized as follows: Sect. 2 is devoted to the UFO sample definition, Sect. 3 describes the main data reduction
procedure adopted for theSwift-XRT,Chandra-ACIS andXMM-Newton-EPIC observations. In Sect. 4.1 we illustrate the selec-
tion of soft X-ray counterparts to hard X-ray source, in Sect. 4.2 we briefly discuss the collection of additional, multi-wavelength
data for our selected sources, while in Sect. 4.3 we discuss our results and compare them with different, previous selections.
Finally, Sect. 5 is dedicated to our conclusions.
2.SAMPLE SELECTION
For this work we made use of the latest release of Fermi-LAT Source Catalog (4FGL-DR4, Ballet et al. 2023; Abdollahi et al.
2022), the fourth and latest PalermoSwift-BAT hard X-ray Catalog (4PBC, Cusumano et al. 2014), and the SRG/ART-XC all-sky
X-ray survey Catalog (Sazonov et al. 2024).
The 4FGL-DR4 is based on the first 14 years of all-sky Fermi-LAT data, and lists 7195γ-ray sources in the 50 MeV−1 TeV
energy range, with properties including coordinates, 95% uncertainty ellipses, fluxes in different energy bands, etc. The 4PBC
covers∼50% of the sky down to a 15−150 keV flux limit of∼5.4×10 −12 erg cm−2 s−1. This catalog includes 1710 hard X-
ray sources with properties including coordinates, 95% error radius, 15−150 keV flux and power-law slope. The SRG/ART-XC
catalog lists sources detected in the 4−12 keV energy band down to a flux limit of∼4×10 −12 erg s−1 cm−2 near the ecliptic plane
and∼7×10 −13 erg s−1 cm−2 near the ecliptic poles. This catalog includes 1545 hard X-ray sources with properties including
coordinates, 98% error radius, and 4−12 keV flux.
Since hard X-ray andγ-ray bands are very close, one would intuitively assume these emissions to be correlated. However,
to quantitatively assess the reliability of a tentative association ofγ-ray sources with lower energy counterparts through 4PBC
and SRG/ART-XC hard X-ray catalogs, we first cross-matched the latter with the associated/identified sources in 4FGL-DR4

3
(excluding extended sources). To do this we used of the associated/identified 4FGL counterpart coordinates (RA Counterpart
and DEC Counterpartcolumns in 4FGL-DR4) and their uncertainties (Unc Counterpartcolumn in 4FGL-DR4) together with
the hard X-ray sources coordinates and uncertainties as listed in the respective hard X-ray catalog. For the sake of uniformity, in
the following we will consider uncertainty radii at 99% confidence level for all catalogs.
We obtained 167 and 188 matches for 4PBC and SRG/ART-XC catalog, respectively. We then checked which hard X-ray
sources were associated in the respective hard X-ray catalog with the same low-energy counterpart associated to the 4FGL
source in 4FGL-DR4, finding that this happens for 151 (∼90%) and 180 (∼96%) sources in 4PBC and SRG/ART-XC catalog,
respectively. We therefore conservatively estimate the hard X-ray selection to have a reliability of correctly pin-point the lower-
energy counterpart of aγ-ray source of∼90% and∼96% when using 4PBC and SRG/ART-XC catalog, respectively. In the
following we refer to these samples of 151 and 180γ-ray/hard X-ray sources as cross-match samples.
Then, we investigate possible selection effects associated to a tentative association ofγ-ray sources with lower energy counter-
parts through hard X-ray catalogs. We note that for associated/identified sources in 4FGL-DR4, high Galactic latitude (|b|>15 ◦)
sources are dominated by blazars (∼93.2%), with a small contribution from pulsars (∼3.2%). For low Galactic latitude
(|b|<15 ◦) associated sources in 4FGL-DR4 we still have a major contribution from blazars (∼53.8%), followed by pulsars
(∼17.4%). Considering the 4FGL-DR4 4PBC and 4FGL-DR4 SRG/ART-XC cross-match samples we obtain similar percent-
ages, with blazars and pulsars representing∼90% and∼1.2% of the high Galactic latitude sources, respectively, while blazars
and pulsars represent∼48.8% and∼12.8% of the low Galactic latitude sources, respectively. We therefore do not expect ten-
tative association ofγ-ray sources with lower energy counterparts through hard X-ray catalogs to introduce significant selection
effects with respect to specific source classes.
We also investigated the connection between emission inγ-rays and hard X-rays in associated/identified sources in 4FGL-
DR4. We note that 2 sources in the 4FGL-DR4 4PBC cross-match sample do not have a reliable hard X-ray flux estimate and
were therefore excluded from this analysis. In Fig. 1 we present the 100 MeV−100 GeVγ-ray flux (Energy Flux100 column in
4FGL-DR4) versus the 15−150 keV flux for the 4FGL-DR4 4PBC cross-match sample (left panel) and versus the 4−12 keV flux
for the 4FGL-DR4 SRG/ART-XC cross-match sample (right panel). In particular we see that hard X-ray andγ-ray fluxes appear
to be correlated with a Spearman rank correlation coefficient of 0.34 both for 4FGL-DR4 4PBC and 4FGL-DR4 SRG/ART-XC
cross-match samples. When considering only sources associated to AGNs, the correlation coefficients are 0.43 and 0.37 for the
4FGL-DR4 4PBC and 4FGL-DR4 SRG/ART-XC cross-match sample, respectively. Similar correlation coefficient have been
found for the connection between radio flux density andγ-ray flux (Ackermann et al. 2011).
We then proceeded to look for hard X-ray counterparts to unidentifiedγ-ray sources. As mentioned in Sect. 1, 2423 sources
in 4FGL-DR4 lack an identified or likely associated lower-energy counterpart, and are therefore classified as UFOs. We then
cross-matched the UFOs in 4FGL-DR4 with sources in 4PCB and SRG/ART-XC catalogs to find hard X-ray sources overlapping
(considering their 99% positional uncertainties) with the UFO 99% uncertainty ellipses.
The result of this cross-matching is presented in Tables 1 and 2. In total, we find 17 UFOs with at least one overlapping hard
X-ray source. In particular, we find 7 UFOs with one overlapping 4PCB source, 6 UFOs with one overlapping SRG/ART-XC
source, 3 UFOs with both one overlapping 4PCB source and one overlapping SRG/ART-XC source, and one UFO with two
overlapping 4PCB sources and one overlapping SRG/ART-XC source.
3.X-RAY DATA
In this section we aim at finding soft X-ray sources lying in the overlapping regions of UFOs and hard X-ray sources uncertainty
regions. To exploit the most recent X-ray data available, we analyze archival observations fromSwift-XRT,Chandra-ACIS and
XMM-Newton-EPIC covering the UFO uncertainty ellipses.
3.1.X-ray Data Reduction
We briefly describe here the data reduction procedures adopted for this work. More information can be found in Paggi et al.
(2020).
Photon counting (PC) mode data for the X-ray Telescope (XRT, Burrows et al. 2005) on board of the Neil GehrelsSwift
Observatory were downloaded from HEASARC 9 data archive. Observations were processed and reduced with the XRTDAS
software (Capalbi et al. 2005) included in the HEAsoft package (v. 6.33.2). The XRT CCD edges were monitored to excluded
time intervals with count-rate>40 counts/s (Puccetti et al. 2011). In addition, we selected time intervals for which the CCD
9 https://heasarc.gsfc.nasa.gov/

4
temperatures was<−50 ◦ C (D’Elia et al. 2013). Using thexrtpipelinetask (ver. 0.13.7), we produced calibrated and cleaned
0.3−10 keV PC mode event files and exposure maps for each observation.
Pointing observations for the Advanced CCD Imaging Spectrometer (ACIS, Nousek et al. 1987) on board of theChandraX-ray
Observatory were retrieved from theChandraData Archive 10. Data were processed and reduced with theChandraInteractive
Analysis of Observations (CIAO, Fruscione et al. 2006) data analysis system (v. 4.16) and theChandracalibration database
CALDB version 4.11.1, adopting standard procedures. Up-to-date calibrations were applied throughchandra reprotask, and
time intervals of high background (i.e., exceeding 3σfrom the average level) were excluded with thedeflaretask. For each
observation, we produced 0.3−7 keV full-band images, as well as exposure, psf, and pileup maps.
Data for the European Photon Imaging Camera (EPIC, Str ¨uder et al. 2001; Turner et al. 2001) on board of theXMM-Newton
space telescope were retrieved from theXMM-NewtonScience Archive 11. Data were reduced with the Science Analysis System
(SAS12) software (v. 21.0.0). Following Nevalainen et al. (2005), we excluded time intervals where both the high-energy count-
rate (i.e., 9.5−12 keV for MOS1 and MOS2, and 10−12 keV for PN) evaluated on the whole EPIC detector, and the soft-energy
count-rate (i.e., 1−5 keV) evaluated at the detector edge (that is, between 12 ′ and 14′ from the detector center) were more than
3σaway from their averages. We then produced full-band 0.3−10 keV images and exposure maps for all available detector in
each observation.
3.2.Source Detection and Flux estimates
When multiple observations were available for one UFO, we merged them to detect the fainter sources that would not be
detected otherwise, using thexselect,flux obsandedetect stacktasks forSwift-XRT,Chandra-ACIS andXMM-Newton-EPIC
data, respectively.

We evaluated source and background counts for each detected source using circular and annular regions centered on the source

In order to get a first estimate of fluxes for X-ray sources, we converted the observed net count-rates to 0.3−10 keV intrinsic
(i.e. unabsorbed) fluxes assuming a standard power-law model with a slope of 1.8 (as appropriate for AGNs, see for example
Tozzi et al. 2006) and an absorption component fixed to the Galactic value (Kalberla et al. 2005) using the appropriate ARF
and RMF files generated before. ForXMM-Newton-EPIC data, the source flux was evaluated as the mean of the fluxes of each
available detector weighted by its uncertainty (Webb et al. 2020). When a source is not detected in an observation with a minimum
significance of 3σlevel, we evaluate its 3σupper limit.
3.3.Spectral Extraction and Fitting
To obtain better estimates on the X-ray source fluxes, we extracted spectra for the X-ray sources for each available observation.
To avoid pile-up contamination, we excluded the inner part of spectra extraction regions as follows. ForSwift-XRT data, we
excluded regions of high pile-up by comparing the observed profiles with the updated XRT PSF analytical model13. ForChandra-
ACIS data, we excluded inner pixels with pileup larger than 5% as estimated from the pileup maps. ForXMM-Newton-EPIC data,
regions of high pileup were estimated and excluded by means of theepatplottask following the procedure explained in the SAS
Data Analysis Threads14.
Spectral fitting was performed with the Sherpa 15 modeling and fitting application (Freeman et al. 2001) in the 0.3−10 keV
energy range forSwift-XRT andXMM-Newton-EPIC spectra, and in the 0.3−7 keV energy range forChandra-ACIS spectra,
adopting Gehrels weighting (Gehrels 1986). Source spectra were binned to a minimum of 20 counts/bin to ensure the validity of
χ2 statistics. For the EPIC spectra we fitted simultaneously MOS1, MOS2 and PN spectra, if available.

3.4.eROSITA Catalog
In addition to data available from observations ofSwift-XRT,Chandra-ACIS andXMM-Newton-EPIC, we looked for X-ray
counterparts to UFO sources in the extended ROentgen Survey with an Imaging Telescope Array (eROSITA, Predehl et al. 2021)
main catalog (eRASS1, Merloni et al. 2024).
The fluxes reported in eRASS1 are derived from the count-rates assuming a model comprising a power-law with slope 2.0 and
a Galactic absorption of 3×10 20 cm−2 (Merloni et al. 2024). We therefore converted the 0.2−5 keV count-rates reported in the
catalog to 0.3−10 keV intrinsic fluxes using PIMMS, again assuming a model comprising an absorption component fixed to the
Galactic value and a power-law with a 1.8 slope. For each source we also evaluated its 99% positional uncertainty.

4.1.Counterpart Selection
Detected soft X-ray sources are presented in Fig. 2. In this figure we present for each selected UFO its soft X-ray map with

ignore these spurious sources. For 4FGL J0724.8+3016, 4FGL J1616.6-5009 and 4FGL J1817.7-2517 we have more than one
soft X-ray source positionally compatible with the hard X-ray source, and in these cases we select the source detected by more
than one instrument as the soft X-ray counterpart to the hard X-ray source. For source 4FGL J1110.3-6501 we have two sources
detected by more than one instrument positionally compatible with the hard X-ray source, namely source 1 (detected bySwift-
XRT,Chandra-ACIS,XMM-Newton-EPIC and eROSITA) and source 3 (detected bySwift-XRT andChandra-ACIS) as show in
Fig. 2. We note that source 3 positionally compatible with source 4eRASS J110947.3-645931 listed in Mayer & Becker (2024)
as possible pulsar and/or blazar type X-ray counterparts to the UFO. However the combined posterior Bayesian probabilities
for this source to be the counterpart of theγ-ray source and to be a pulsar or a blazar are∼0.026 and 0.020, respectively, and
its combined posterior probability for this source to be matched with the UFO is∼0.046. We therefore select source 1 as the
soft X-ray counterpart to the hard X-ray source. Finally, for 4FGL J0550.2+0730c we have no soft X-ray sources positionally
compatible with the hard X-ray source, so in this case we select no soft X-ray counterpart.

4.2.Multi-Wavelength Data
In order to obtain a better characterization of the selected soft X-ray sources we collected multi-wavelength data for the soft
x-ray sources listed in Table 3.
First of all, using the coordinates and positional uncertainties listed in Table 3 we looked into the SIMBAD Astronomical
Database16 for known lower-energy counterparts, and listed them along with their source type in Table 3.
Then we collected available radio data making use of the SSDC SED Builder17.
We also looked for mid-IR counterparts to the soft x-ray sources in the Wide-Field Infrared Survey Explorer (WISE) all-sky
data (AllWISE, Wright et al. 2010), and present the result of this cross matching in Table 8, where all magnitudes have been then
corrected for Galactic absorption using reddening estimates from Schlafly & Finkbeiner (2011) and the extinction model from
Fitzpatrick & Massa (2007). Using the same procedure presented in Paggi et al. (2020), we use these data to selectγ-ray blazar
candidates by comparing their position in the two WISE color-color planes with the 90% KDE isodensity contours of known
γ-ray blazars of different classes, as shown in Figure 3.
We then collected ultraviolet-optical data available through theSwiftUltraviolet and Optical Telescope (UVOT) (UVOT,
Roming et al. 2005). The UVOT data were downloaded from HEASARC 18 data archive and reduced using standard proce-
dures19. After checking the correct World Coordinate System alignment of UVOT images with USNO-B Catalog (Monet et al.
2003), all extension in each image were summed usinguvotimsum; the same procedure was applied to produce merged exposure
maps. We performed source photometry using theuvotsourcetask using the appropriate exposure map. We adopted an aperture
radius of 5′′ for all filters, and used as background region an annulus centered at the source coordinates with inner and outer radii
of 10′′ and 15′′, respectively. All magnitudes have been then corrected for Galactic absorption, with exception of source 1 in the
field of UFO 4FGL J1424.2-6111c and source 1 in the field of UFO 4FGL J1652.2-4516, since these two sources are very close
to the Galactic plane (|b|<1 ◦), yielding very high reddening at their location. The result of this analysis is presented in Table 9.
We also collected Gaia (Gaia Collaboration et al. 2016) parallax and proper motion data from the Gaia DR3 database (Gaia
Collaboration et al. 2023) and present them in Table 10. In Figure 4 we show the Gaia counterparts to the soft X-ray sources
present in Table 3 in a proper motion Dec vs. RA plot, highlighting in red the sources that have proper motions compatible with
zero at a 3σlevel.
Finally, we looked in the SDSS DR18 (Almeida et al. 2023) and LAMOST (Cui et al. 2012) DR10 for available optical spectra
for the soft X-ray sources listed in Table 3. We only found optical spectra (both in SDSS DR18 and LAMOST DR10) for the
selected soft X-ray source in the field of 4FGL J1346.5+5330 (presented in Figure 5), showing broad, quasar-like lines.
These multi-wavelength data were used to build broad-band spectral energy distributions (SEDs) of these soft X-ray sources,
augmented by the X-ray data collected in this work and assuming that theirγ-ray emission is that of UFO as reported in
4FGL, with the goal of modeling them with the JetSeT fitting tool (Tramacere 2020). However, we were only able to col-
lect enough multi-wavelength data to build reasonable SEDs for two sources, namely the soft X-ray sources in the field of 4FGL
J1346.5+5330 and 4FGL J2109.3+3531, presented in Figure 6. In the same figure we show the best fit model to the SEDs, whose
parameters are presented in Table 11.
4.3.Discussion
We found 22 hard X-ray sources from 4PBC and SRG/ART-XC all-sky X-ray survey Catalog compatible with the 99% LAT
uncertainty ellipses of 17FermiUFOs. In particular we have 13 UFOs whose uncertainty region is compatible with one hard

We were able to collect soft X-ray data for these 17 UFOs, and identify 16 unique soft X-ray counterparts to the hard X-ray

Despite the small sample size of 16 soft X-ray counterparts, we can compare these results with the expectations from the
composition of theγ-ray sky. Considering the 16 soft X-ray counterparts we found, we note that 4 are located at high Galactic
latitude (|b|>15 ◦) and 12 at low Galactic latitude (|b|<15 ◦). For associated sources in 4FGL-DR4, we see that high Galactic
latitude sources are dominated by blazars (∼93.2%) with a small contribution from pulsars (∼3.2%). For low Galactic latitude
associated sources in 4FGL-DR4 we still have a major contribution from blazars (∼53.8%), followed by pulsars (∼17.4%) and
sources of unknown nature (∼10.5%). Assuming that the same source class composition holds also for UFOs, in our sample of
16 UFOs we would expect to find∼10 blazars and∼2 pulsars. To investigate if the hard X-ray selection used in this analysis can
disfavor blazar of pulsar selection, we cross matched the associated sources from 4FGL-DR4 with both 4PBC and SRG/ART-XC
catalogs. We find however a representation in the matched sources of blazars and pulsars that is similar to that of associated
4FGL-DR4 sources, since in the high and low Galactic latitude sources blazars represent∼88.7% and∼54.9% of the matched

We compare our results with those of Tsuji et al. (2021). These authors cross-matched the 105 monthSwift-BAT catalog (Oh
et al. 2018) - listing 1632 14–195 keV sources at detected at>4.8σsignificance level - and a previous release of 4FGL, namely
4FGL-DR2 (Ballet et al. 2020) listing 5788γ-ray sources. The authors find 132 matches for point-like sources by means of
spatial cross-match (with a separation threshold of 0.08 ◦ between theγ-ray source and the hard X-ray source) and 24 matches
for point-like sources by means of identifier match, for a total of 156 point-like source matches. In particular, we note the UFOs
4FGL J1110.3-6501, 4FGL J1424.2-6111c and 4FGL J2109.3+3531 are not present in 4FGL-DR2, so they are not included
in Tsuji et al. (2021) analysis. For 4FGL-DR2 UFO 4FGL J1808.5-3701 the authors report a cross match with aSwift-LAT
source, but thisγ-ray source has been associated with the LMXB SAX J1808.4-3658 in 4FGL-DR4, so it not considered a UFO
anymore. For UFOs 4FGL J0552.0+2656c, 4FGL J0955.8-4106, 4FGL J1015.1-6353, 4FGL J1401.3-5012, 4FGL J1816.1-3908
and 4FGL J2052.3+4437 we find an overlapping hard X-ray source only in the SRG/ART-XC catalog (not in the 4PBC), so for
these sources Tsuji et al. (2021) do not report any cross-match. For UFOs 4FGL J1008.2-1000, 4FGL J1346.5+5330, 4FGL
J1616.6-5009 and 4FGL J1817.7-2517 the authors do not report any cross-match, but these associations were likely excluded
because the separation between theγ-ray source and theSwift-LAT source is>0.15 ◦. Finally, for UFOs 4FGL J0724.8+3016,
4FGL J1407.7-3017, 4FGL J1652.2-4516 and 4FGL J1817.6-3251 the authors report a cross-match between theγ-ray source
and theSwift-LAT source, identifying the same lower-energy counterparts reported in our analysis.
To conclude this section we briefly mention the soft X-ray source for which we did not find any lower-energy counterpart,
namely source 1 in the field of UFO 4FGL J1401.3-5012 (see Fig. 2). This source lies in the uncertainty region of SRG/ART-XC
source SRGA J140146.3-501333 and is detected both bySwift-XRT and eROSITA. It appears quite bright in soft X-rays with
fluxes of 5.6 and 4.5×10 −12 erg cm−2 s inSwift-XRT and eROSITA observations, respectively. The source is also detected in
AllWISE but only in the first three WISE bands, and in GAIA DR3 with significant parallax and proper motion, indicating that
this source is likely Galactic.
5.CONCLUSIONS
We have looked in the 100-month Palermo BAT Catalog and in SRG/ART-XC all-sky X-ray survey Catalog for hard X-ray
sources compatible with the uncertainty regions of unidentifiedγ-ray sources in the 4FGL catalog, finding 22 hard X-ray sources
compatible with the 99% LAT uncertainty ellipses of 17FermiUFOs.
We collectedSwift-XRT,Chandra-ACIS,XMM-Newton-EPIC and eRASS soft X-ray data for these sources, identifying 17 soft
X-ray counterparts for the hard X-ray sources, and associating 16 of these sources with known astronomical objects. In addition,
we collected available multi-wavelength data for these sources.

shows mid-IR WISE colors compatible with knownγ-ray blazars, a blazar-like broad band SED, and quasar-like optical
spectra. We suggest that this sources is the lower-energy counterpart to the UFO 4FGL J1346.5+5330.
•1 of these these associated sources (namely PMN J0954-4108) has been classified as aγ-ray BL Lac candidate on the basis
of its mid-IR WISE. We confirm this classification, and suggest that this sources is likely the lower-energy counterpart to
the UFO 4FGL J0955.8-4106.

0954510, IGR J10147-6354, 2MASX J14080674-3023537, ICRF J210931.8+353257). In addition these sources show
mid-IR WISE colors compatible with knownγ-ray blazars, and one (ICRF J210931.8+353257) shows a blazar-like broad
