ABSTRACT
The origins of sub-luminous ( 𝐿 𝛾, iso < 1049.5 erg s−1) gamma-ray bursts (GRBs) associated with broad-lined
T ype Ic supernovae (Ic-BL SNe) are poorly understood, in part due to the low discovery rate and faint afterglows.
Here we present the identification of the optical afterglow of Fermi-GBM-detected GRB 260310A (AT 2026fgk)
as a rapidly rising ( > 1 mag d−1), red ( 𝑔 − 𝑟 = 0.4 mag) transient using the Gravitational-wave Optical Transient
Observatory, Large Array Survey Telescope, and Zwicky Transient Facility (ZTF) data streams. We present
multiwavelength follow-up observations from the first 50 days, which reveal that GRB 260310A/AT 2026fgk
was sub-luminous ( 𝐿 𝛾, iso = 1048.8 erg s−1); it was the most nearby ( 𝑧 = 0.153) afterglow identified blindly by
an optical survey; and that it is one of the brightest afterglows ever observed at X-ray, optical, and radio (cm to
mm) wavelengths. We spectroscopically confirm an underlying Ic-BL SN with properties typical of GRB-SNe
(𝑀ej ≈ 3 𝑀⊙, 𝐸K ≈ 1052 erg). With basic modeling of the afterglow, including the long optical rise ( ≈ 103 s),
we infer either a low initial Lorentz factor ( Γ0 ≈ 40) or a slightly off-axis viewing angle ( ≲ 3◦). The host
galaxy’s mass and star formation rate are similar to the hosts of other sub-luminous GRBs. ZTF’s flux-limited
survey gives a volumetric rate of AT 2026fgk-like events of 0.30+1.37
−0.29 Gpc −3 yr−1, which is consistent with the
on-axis, high luminosity ( 𝐿 𝛾, iso > 1049.5 erg s−1) long-GRB rate. The similarity in the rates strongly constrains
the prevalence of low- Γ0 bursts and the beaming of the initial relativistic material in GRBs.

1. INTRODUCTION
The majority of long gamma-ray bursts (LGRBs) are pow-
ered by the core collapse of massive stars, in which an ultra-
relativistic jet drives both the prompt gamma-ray emission

3
and a subsequent broadband afterglow (e.g., M. J. Rees & P .
Meszaros 1992; S. E. Woosley 1993; T. Piran 2004; Z. Cano
et al. 2017)1. The discovery of LGRB 980425—associated
with supernova (SN) 1998bw ( T. J. Galama et al. 1998; K.
Iwamoto et al. 1998; S. R. Kulkarni et al. 1998)—and over 30
spectroscopically confirmed LGRB-SNe since then ( Z. Cano
et al. 2017; A. Fiore et al. 2025; G. Finneran et al. 2025) es-
tablished a robust connection between collapsar LGRBs and
broad-lined T ype Ic supernovae (Ic-BL SNe). LGRB-SNe
can have kinetic energies ( 𝐸𝐾 ) over an order of magnitude
greater than the canonical 1051 erg of neutrino-driven super-
novae (Z. Cano et al. 2017).
Cosmological LGRBs are typically defined as having
isotropic-equivalent gamma-ray energies 𝐸𝛾, iso ∼ 1051–
1054 erg , with luminosities 𝐿 𝛾, iso ≳ 1050–1054 erg s−1 and
prompt emission peak energies 𝐸peak ∼100–1000 keV (e.g.,
D. A. Frail et al. 2001; L. Amati et al. 2002; Z. Cano
et al. 2011). However, a number of nearby ( 𝑧 ≤
0.3) LGRBs have been discovered with low luminosities
(𝐿 𝛾, iso < 1049.5 erg s−1 ; Table 1), initially by BeppoSAX
and HETE-2 (e.g., J. Heise et al. 2001; T. Sakamoto et al.
2005; S. Campana et al. 2006; R. L. C. Starling et al.
2011). Sub-luminous LGRBs were firmly tied to Ic-BL SNe
through Swift-era events such as XRF 060218/SN 2006aj and
GRB 100316D/SN 2010bh ( S. Campana et al. 2006; E. Pian
et al. 2006; R. L. C. Starling et al. 2011). Sub-luminous
LGRBs are challenging to detect due to their low 𝐸𝛾, iso, low
𝐸peak, and often longer prompt durations (e.g., E. Liang et al.
2007; F. J. Virgili et al. 2009; O. Bromberg et al. 2011; R.
Margutti et al. 2013; E. Nakar 2015; Z. Cano et al. 2017; V .
D’Elia et al. 2018). Modeling of the radio emission from sev-
eral sub-luminous LGRBs implies quasi-isotropic emission
(e.g., A. M. Soderberg et al. 2006). The volumetric rate of
sub-luminous LGRBs, found to be R = 164+98
65 Gpc −3 yr−1
(above 𝐿iso = 5 × 1046 erg s−1 , see H. Sun et al. 2015,

The study of sub-luminous LGRBs has been reinvigorated
by the launch of soft wide-field X-ray missions such as the
Einstein Probe Wide-field X-ray Telescope (EP-WXT; W .
Yuan et al. 2022, 2025) and the ECLAIRs camera onboard the
Space-based multi-band Variable Object Monitor (SVOM; J.
Wei et al. 2016; J.-L. Atteia et al. 2022). EP and SVOM
have already identified a number of nearby, sub-luminous,
soft-spectrum GRB-SNe (Table 1; e.g., J. C. Rastinejad et al.
2025; G. P . Srinivasaragavan et al. 2025b,a; S.-Q. Jiang et al.
2025; W .-X. Li et al. 2025). In parallel, wide-field optical sur-
veys are now capable of finding afterglows independently of a
gamma-ray trigger ( S. B. Cenko et al. 2013, 2015; B. Stalder
et al. 2017; V . Bhalerao et al. 2017; A. Melandri et al. 2019;
I. Andreoni et al. 2020; A. Y . Q. Ho et al. 2020), enabling
the discovery of events whose prompt emission is below the
trigger threshold for high-energy satellites ( S. B. Cenko et al.
2013; A. Y . Q. Ho et al. 2020, 2022; V . Lipunov et al. 2022;
F. Xu et al. 2023; D. A. Perley et al. 2025; M. L. Li et al. 2025;
G. P . Srinivasaragavan et al. 2025c).
AT 2026fgk is one of the most recent afterglows blindly
identified in optical-survey data. It was confirmed to be as-
sociated with long-duration GRB 260310A ( R. Konno et al.
2026a; D. O’Neill et al. 2026). Optical spectroscopy ( K.
Hinds et al. 2026; A. de Ugarte Postigo et al. 2026a; B. Hsu
et al. 2026) confirmed 𝑧 = 0.153 ( 𝑑L = 762 Mpc) 2 and es-
tablished the presence of a Ic-BL SN, making this one of the
closest sub-luminous LGRBs detected this decade, and by far
the most nearby afterglow identified by an optical survey. In
this paper, we present detailed, early (up to 50 d post-trigger)
multi-wavelength data of AT 2026fgk. We present the data in
Section 2, and the inferred properties of the supernova in Sec-
tion 3. In Section 4, we compare the properties of AT 2026fgk
to other GRBs and Ic-BL SNe. In Section 5 we use the ZTF
flux-limited experiment to infer the volumetric rate of similar
events. We measure the host galaxy properties in Section 6.
We perform basic modeling and discuss possible physical ori-
gins of AT 2026fgk in Section 7, and summarize in Section 8.
Throughout, magnitudes are reported in the AB system.

2.2. Discovery of AT 2026fgk and Association with GRB
260310A
2.2.1. GOTO
The Gravitational-wave Optical Transient Observer
(GOTO; D. Steeghs et al. 2022; M. J. Dyer et al. 2024;
J. D. Lyman et al. 2026, Steeghs et al., in prep.) is a robotic
telescope network distributed across two observing sites:
the Roque de los Muchachos Observatory on La Palma, Ca-
nary Islands, and Siding Spring Observatory in New South
Wales, Australia. Each site comprises two mounts, each with
eight 0.4 m telescopes equipped with ∼50 Megapixel CCDs,
whose field of view slightly overlaps, providing a continu-
ous sky footprint ∼45◦. GOTO responded autonomously to
the GRB 260310A Fermi/GBM alert, with targeted imag-
ing beginning ∼12 minutes post-trigger. Difference imaging
was performed against deeper pre-trigger site-specific tem-
plate observations using a modified implementation of HOT-

5
PANTS3(A. Becker 2015). Following this process, AT 2026fgk
was detected in the difference images at > 5𝜎 level. Forced
photometry was subsequently performed using a forced pho-
tometry service (Jarvis et al. in prep.), and the resulting
values are those presented here and shown in Figure 2.
AT 2026fgk was discovered by GOTO on 2026 March 10
05:14 UTC (MJD 61109.22; 𝑇0 +0.012 d or 17 minutes), at a
position (J2000) R.A. = 14 h37m16.14s, Dec. = 71◦50′30.31′′,
corresponding to a Galactic latitude 𝑏 = +42.97 deg, and
discovery magnitude of GOTO 𝐿 = 18.84 ± 0.06 mag
(𝜆eﬀ =5325.68Å, see D. Steeghs et al. 2022, for details). The
internal source name was GOTO26buh. The detection was re-
ported to the Transient Name Server ( D. O’Neill et al. 2026).
2.2.2. ATLAS
Following GOTO, the next detection came from the Aster-
oid Terrestrial-impact Last Alert System (ATLAS; J. L. Tonry
et al. 2018a). The ATLAS system comprises five wide-field
optical telescopes. Four of the five telescopes (those situated
in Haleakala and Mauna Loa in Hawaii, El Sauce in Chile, and
Sutherland in South Africa) have identical designs, consisting
of 0.5 m aperture telescopes equipped with broad-band cyan
(𝑐) and orange (𝑜) filters (J. L. Tonry et al. 2018a). The fifth
unit (located in Tenerife in Spain) is composed of four mod-
ules, each of which contains four commercial telescopes that
combine to provide performance equivalent to a 56 cm aper-
ture telescope ( J. Licandro et al. 2023, 2025). All telescopes
possess the same broad-band wide (𝑤) filter (J. L. Tonry et al.
2025).
All ATLAS data is processed following the same standard
pipeline. Target images are astrometrically and photometri-
cally calibrated using RefCat2 ( J. L. Tonry et al. 2018b) be-
fore template subtraction is performed utilizing deep refer-
ence sky images. PSF photometry is then performed on these
difference images (as outlined by J. L. Tonry et al. 2018b).
The ATLAS Forced Photometry Server 4 provides public ac-
cess to ATLAS forced photometry for any sky location; how-
ever, it does not yet include 𝑤-band data.
Combined, the ATLAS telescopes robotically survey the
night sky every ≈ 12−36 hours in a dithered 4×30 s exposure
pattern (spaced across a 1 hr period). ATLAS first detected
the optical counterpart to GRB 260310A on MJD 61109.50
(𝑇0 + 7 hr) in the 𝑜-band, and continued to significantly detect
the transient across 𝑐, 𝑜 and 𝑤 until MJDs 61124.5, 61125.5
and 61126.1, respectively (see Table 8 and Figure 2).

2.2.4. ZTF
AT 2026fgk was serendipitously detected by the Zwicky

association at the time of flagging. The first ZTF detec-
tion of AT 2026fgk (ZTF26aakjzdt) was on MJD 61110.405
(𝑇0+1.2 d), yielding 𝑔 = 18.09 ± 0.02 mag, followed 2.1 hr
later by 𝑟 = 17.68 ± 0.02 mag, and was preceded by a
ZTF forced-photometry non-detection at 𝑔 > 20.40 mag on
MJD 61108.23 ( 𝑇0 − 0.97 d). After correction for Galac-
tic extinction ( 𝐸 (𝐵 − 𝑉) = 0.030 mag; D. J. Schlegel et al.
1998) using the E. F. Schlafly & D. P . Finkbeiner (2011)
law ( 𝐴𝑔 = 0.099 mag, 𝐴𝑟 = 0.069 mag), the intrinsic color at
first detection in ZTF is (𝑔 − 𝑟)0 = 0.38 ± 0.03 mag. The
non-detection limit and the first detection implied a rise of
≳ 1 mag d−1 in 𝑔; together with its red color, this prompted
high-priority flagging by the fast-transients filter. We present
forced photometry on the ZTF images ( F. J. Masci et al. 2023)
in Table 8 and Figure 2.

2.4. Nordic Optical Telescope - Polarimetry
We also obtained 𝑟-band polarimetry starting on March 13,
2026 (MJD 61112.07; 𝑇0 +2.8 d), over the half-wave plate an-
gles 0.0, 22.5, 45.0 and 67.5◦. The data was reduced with a
custom pipeline (M. Pursiainen et al. 2025), using a radius of
2 × FWHM to extract the extraordinary and ordinary beam
photometry ( M. Pursiainen et al. 2023). The pipeline also
performs polarization bias correction following the canonical
case of S. Plaszczynski et al. (2014). Measured polarization
is low at 𝑃 = 0.60 ± 0.30 %, and consistent with 0% at 2𝜎
(see Figure 4). Milky Way interstellar polarization (ISP) ap-
pears low in the direction of the event. The maximum level
of MW ISP ( 9 % × 𝐸 (𝐵 − 𝑉); K. Serkowski et al. 1975) is
𝑃MW = 0.20 %, supported by the two stars with 5◦ in the
Heiles catalog ( C. Heiles 2000) that show low polarization
consistent with 0%. As such the intrinsic polarization of the
event is low, and consistent with Galactic ISP at ∼ 2𝜎.

3.3. Explosion Properties
The peak bolometric luminosity of stripped-envelope
SNe is set, at first order, by the mass of the radioactive
56Ni synthesized in the explosion ( W . D. Arnett 1982; S.
Valenti et al. 2008). The rate of post-peak decline—and in
particular the extended tail at late times—reflects the trans-
parency of the ejecta to 𝛾-rays from the decay chain of 56Ni to
56Co and 56Fe , itself sensitive to 𝑀ej and the density profile
(e.g., A. Clocchiatti & J. C. Wheeler 1997; J. C. Wheeler et al.
2015).
As the photometric coverage spans the GRB afterglow
and the SN bump, we infer SN properties from a joint fit
that models both the components together rather than from
a SN-only fit to an afterglow-subtracted light curve. We
use the modified Arnett implementation ( W . D. Arnett 1982,
see also S. Valenti et al. 2008) provided by REDBACK ( N.
Sarin et al. 2024), which couples the radioactive heating from
56Ni → 56Co → 56Fe decay chain to a one-zone diffusion cal-
culation with the late time 𝛾-ray leakage prescription of S.
Valenti et al. (2008) and a temperature-floor photosphere ( M.
Nicholl et al. 2017) radiating a blackbody SED. The free Ar-
nett parameters are the nickel mass fraction, 𝑓Ni, the ejecta
mass, 𝑀ej , and a characteristic ejecta velocity, 𝑣ej; the opti-
cal opacity is fixed at 𝜅 = 0.07 cm−2g−1 (e.g., N. N. Chugai
2000; F. Taddia et al. 2018; L. Tartaglia et al. 2021; C. Bar-
barino et al. 2021), and the 𝛾-ray opacity 𝜅𝛾 =0.03 cm2g−1.
The afterglow is modeled, as before, by a smoothly broken
power-law parameterized by 𝛼1, 𝛼2 and break time 𝑡𝑏.
The afterglow parameters ( 𝛼1, 𝛼2 and 𝑡𝑏) are anchored

4.2. Afterglow
In addition to exhibiting large heterogeneity in their prompt
emission properties (Section 4.1), sub-luminous GRBs also
show diverse multiwavelength afterglow properties. In this
section we compare the afterglow properties in each wave-
band to other LGRBs in the literature. Basic modeling of the
afterglow+SN is presented in Section 7; more thorough mod-
eling will be presented in a companion paper, along with our
late-time multiwavelength data.
4.2.1. Optical
As shown in Figure 2, the optical light curve of AT 2026fgk
shows a clear rise, then a power-law decay with a promi-
nent break at ∼ 7.70 d. The only other sub-luminous GRBs
with early afterglow-dominated light curves are 020903 and
190829A. The optical light curves of the other events—
GRB 980425, 060218, 100316D, 171205A, 031203—are
dominated by the SN and/or an early (first few days) thermal
shock-cooling peak.
GRB 260310A is sub-luminous near peak (Figure 10): the
optical peak ( 𝑀𝑟 ≲ −21.81 mag) lies ∼ 1–2 mag below the
median peak luminosity of Swift-era on-axis LGRB after-
glows (e.g., D. A. Kann et al. 2010). Both pre- and post-decay
slopes are inconsistent at > 3𝜎 with the population means
from A. Zeh et al. (2006) and D. A. Kann et al. (2010). The
pre-break slope ( 𝛼1 = −0.52 ± 0.02) is significantly shal-

than the means ( ⟨𝛼2⟩ ≈ − 2.12 to −2.34). The break time

As shown in Figure 2, the rising phase of GRB 260310A

4.2.3. Radio
The radio panel in Figure 10 shows the cm-band
radio light curves of GRB 260310A alongside several
other sub-luminous GRBs associated with SNe—GRBs
980425, 060218, 171205A and 190829A—and the energetic
GRB 030329/SN 2003dh ( T. Matheson et al. 2003; K. Z.
Stanek et al. 2003; A. Tiengo et al. 2004; A. J. van der Horst
et al. 2008; J. Hjorth et al. 2003). Radio emission arises from
synchrotron radiation; electrons are accelerated to relativistic
energies at the expanding blast-wave front ( R. A. Chevalier
1998). At the earliest epochs ( ≲few days), a reverse shock
propagating back through the decelerating ejecta can produce
a short-lived radio flare as the shock shell rapidly cools (e.g.,
R. Sari et al. 1999; S. Kobayashi & R. Sari 2000; S. Kobayashi
& B. Zhang 2003); this component fades steeply and becomes
negligible once the reverse shock has crossed the ejecta shell,
leaving the forward shock to dominate thereafter (J. Granot &
A. J. van der Horst 2014).
GRB 260310A peaks at 𝐿 𝜈 ≈ 7 × 1030 erg s−1 Hz−1 at
11 GHz ( ∼10 d rest frame), roughly 100 times more lumi-
nous than GRB 980425/SN 1998bw and consistent with the
median peak luminosity ∼ 1031 erg s−1 Hz−1 measured for
LGRBs across a large redshift distribution ( P . Chandra &
D. A. Frail 2012). Among sub-luminous GRBs, it is the
most radio-luminous event at comparable epochs, exceeding
GRB 031203/SN 2003lw and GRB 190829A.
The broadband radio SED of GRB 260310A (Figure 11)
exhibits a typical broken power law shape (e.g., R. Sari et al.
1998; R. A. Chevalier 1998; R. A. Chevalier & Z.- Y . Li 2000;
J. Granot & R. Sari 2002): a steep low-frequency rise, a single
peak, and an optically-thin decline at high frequency.

5. VOLUMETRIC RATE
In this section, we take advantage of the proximity and op-
tical brightness of GRB 260310A to estimate the volumet-
ric rate of optically selected afterglows. By comparing this
rate measurement to the 𝛾-ray-selected LGRB rate, we can
constrain the angular structure of the initial relativistic mate-
rial, as well as the rate of any other phenomena that might be
preferentially selected in the optical band but missed by 𝛾-ray
satellites.
Over the seven years of ZTF operations (2018–2024), fast
transient searches in the ZTF alert stream have resulted in the
blind identification of close to 20 afterglows (I. Andreoni et al.
2020; A. Y . Q. Ho et al. 2020; I. Andreoni et al. 2021; A. Y . Q.
Ho et al. 2022; M. L. Li et al. 2025; D. A. Perley et al. 2025;
G. P . Srinivasaragavan et al. 2025c). Almost all of the events
with redshift measurements have been at cosmological dis-
tances (𝑧 > 1). GRB 260310A is the closest ( 𝑧 = 0.153) and
optically brightest of the ZTF afterglow sample: at < 18 mag
it even passes the criteria of the ZTF BTS.
We use the BTS to estimate the rate, following D. A. Per-
ley et al. (2026b). For rapidly fading events, a transient must
both survive ZTF automated filtering ( ∼hours–days cadences)
and receive spectroscopic follow-up within ∼1–3 d—the lat-
ter imposed to preserve classification completeness before
the source drops below the nominal BTS follow-up limit.

7. DISCUSSION
There are several proposed models for sub-luminous
GRBs: that they are classical LGRBs viewed off-axis ( L.
Izzo et al. 2019) or stifled in an extended stellar envelope or
wind ( S. Campana et al. 2006; E. Nakar 2015; C. M. Irwin
& K. Hotokezaka 2025), or low-power jets from alternative
central engines ( B. D. Metzger et al. 2011; C. M. Irwin &
R. A. Chevalier 2016). Unlike in most previous sub-luminous
GRBs, the strong afterglow emission from AT 2026fgk dis-
favors a choked jet or low-power jet origin. In this section
we perform basic modeling of AT 2026fgk and find that it is
consistent with being an LGRB viewed slightly off-axis, or
a low-Lorentz factor jet viewed on-axis. We adopt fiducial
values of 𝐸K,iso = 1053 erg and 𝑛ism = 1 cm−3, typical of cos-
mological LGRB afterglow modeling (e.g., D. A. Frail et al.
2001; A. Panaitescu & P . Kumar 2002).

7.4. Physical Origin of the X-ray Rebrightening
As discussed in Section 4.2.2 , a prominent X-ray rebright-
ening at such a late phase (tens of days) has, to our knowledge,
not been observed in any past LGRB afterglow, although
such late-time observations have only been obtained for a rel-
atively small number of events. The plateau/flare reaches
𝐿 𝑋 ≃ ( 5–7) × 1043 erg s−1 at Δ𝑡 ∼20–30 d, with no evolu-
tion of the photon index ( Γ ≈1.3–1.6) that is notably beyond
calibration differences in instruments (see Appendix A.1 ).
Under the assumption of a flare (taking into account the
SAA point), the variability timescale is roughly Δ𝑡 ∼ 6 d. A
useful metric for discriminating between models of late-time
variability is the ratio Δ𝑡/𝑡, where 𝑡 is the duration after the
burst at which this variability occurs. In this case, 𝑡 ∼ 25 d,
so Δ𝑡/𝑡 ∼ 1/4. Assuming a plateau, the timescale of this

23
variability (before it begins to decay again) is roughly 15 d,
making Δ𝑡/𝑡 roughly 0.6. K. Ioka et al. (2005) discuss three
physical causes for afterglow variability. The first possibility
arises from angular structure on the emitting surface, which
can result in variability on timescales Δ𝑡 ≳ 𝑡; however, this is
unlikely here, given our constraint that Δ𝑡/𝑡 < 1. The second
arises from refreshed shocks, which should have a variability
timescale of Δ𝑡/𝑡 > 1/4, which could be plausible here. In
this scenario, an “inner shell” is ejected with a slower velocity
than an “outer shell”; once the Lorentz factor of the outer
shell drops to a value comparable to that of the inner shell,
the two shocks interact and lead to a deviation from the typical
afterglow decay. Another consequence of this theory is that
the afterglow energy should not return to the original decay
(as now the afterglow has been “refreshed” with energy)—
which we observe in this GRB’s X-ray afterglow, after the
plateau. The third possibility is a long-acting engine, whether
black-hole accretion or a millisecond magnetar.
Another possibility is that the X-rays originate from CSM
shock-interaction. However, the luminosity is ∼50–100×
brighter than the most X-ray luminous CSM-interacting SN
known (SN 2010jl, 𝐿 𝑋 ∼ 1042 erg s−1 ; E. O. Ofek et al. 2013,
2014; C. Fransson et al. 2014; P . Chandra et al. 2015), and 2–
3 orders of magnitude above T ype IIn/Ib/c interacting pop-
ulations at comparable phases (e.g., SN 1998Z, SN 2010jl,
SN 2014C; E. M. Schlegel & R. Petre 2006; D. Brethauer
et al. 2022; Ferdinand et al. 2026).
A final possibility is that the plateau arises from the slower
wings of a structured jet entering the line of sight (e.g., J. Gra-
not et al. 2002; E. Troja et al. 2022; G. P . Srinivasaragavan
et al. 2025c). We consider this the most likely explanation
in light of other properties of this event. A structured-jet fit
to the full multiwavelength data out to late times will be per-
formed in a follow-up paper.
7.5. GRB 260310A/AT 2026fgk as an Off-axis GRB?
We showed that the slow optical rise could be attributed to
an LGRB viewed slightly off-axis. A slightly off-axis view-
ing angle could also help explain the relatively strong break
between 𝛼1 = −0.52 ± 0.02 and 𝛼2 = −1.45+0.02
−0.01. A similar
explanation was invoked for both GRB 171205A ( V . D’Elia
et al. 2018; Y . Urata et al. 2019; A. Kumar et al. 2022) and
GRB 190829A (e.g., V . Chand et al. 2020; L. Rhodes et al.
2020; H. E. S. S. Collaboration et al. 2021; N. Fraija et al.
2021; Y . Sato et al. 2021; Y . D. Hu et al. 2021; O. S. Salafia
et al. 2022; S. Dichiara et al. 2022). The X-ray rebrighten-
ing could potentially be interpreted as arising from additional
material entering the line of sight, although this would need
to be tested in detail.
The placement of GRB 260310A on the Amati diagram
and its comparison to other events are of note in the con-
text of off-axis models. In an off-axis geometry, the rela-
tivistic Doppler factor 𝛿 = [Γ(1 − 𝛽 cos(𝜃obs − 𝜃jet))] −1 de-
creases as the viewing angle 𝜃obs − 𝜃jet increases, leading to
𝐸peak ∝ 𝛿 and 𝐸iso ∝ 𝛿1− 𝛼, where 𝛼 is the low-energy photon
index, as shown by R. Y amazaki et al. (2003); L. Amati et al.
(2007), and discussed by V . D’Elia et al. (2018) in the context
of GRB 171205A. For the spectral indices typical of GRBs
(𝛼 < 0), 1 − 𝛼 > 1, so 𝐸iso is suppressed more steeply than
𝐸peak as 𝛿 decreases, displacing off-axis events to a region of
the Amati plane characterized by higher 𝐸peak /𝐸iso ratios—
to above the canonical 𝐸peak -𝐸iso relation. This is an inter-
pretation for the subset of sub-luminous GRBs that occupy
this region of the Amati diagram, including GRB 171205A
(V . D’Elia et al. 2018; J. Wang et al. 2018; A. Suzuki et al.
2019; B. Maity & P . Chandra 2021). Canonical shock break-
out sub-luminous GRBs—likely GRB 980425, XRF 060218,
and XRF 100316B—instead lie below the Amati relation,
typically being interpreted as intrinsically less collimated or
quasi-spherical outflows rather than off-axis events (e.g. S.
Campana et al. 2006; E. Nakar & R. Sari 2012; R. Margutti
et al. 2013).
Other studies on GRB 260310A ( B. O’Connor et al. 2026;
Y .-H. Y ang et al. 2026b; R. Gill et al. 2026) have also sug-
gested that we are viewing the event slightly off-axis ( 𝜃v −
𝜃jet ≲ 10◦).
7.6. Implication of the Volumetric Rate for Jet Structure
In Section 5, we used the ZTF flux-limited survey to infer
that the volumetric rate for fast (and therefore observed close
to the jet axis) optical afterglows is consistent with the lo-
cal on-axis GRB rate. Since the ZTF optical afterglows are
typically detectable for hours up to ≈ 1 d, this suggests that
the beaming factor of the material at 1 d is comparable to the
beaming factor of the initial relativistic material producing
the gamma-rays. This is consistent with the fact that approx-
imately half of the optically discovered afterglows are found
to have an associated detected GRB ( A. Y . Q. Ho et al. 2022).
It also strongly limits the existence of other phenomena with
similarly energetic and collimated relativistic jets.

8. SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS
AT 2026fgk is by far the closest LGRB afterglow discov-
ered blindly by wide-field optical surveys, and the first con-
firmed to be associated with a sub-luminous GRB. This paper
presents detailed multiwavelength data of AT 2026fgk from
the first 50 days post-burst. The event is clearly in a rarely-
studied regime of parameter space. GRB 260310A had a high
peak gamma-ray energy given its low 𝐸𝛾, iso. The optical light
curve has a slow rise to peak, a shallow decay, a late achro-
matic break observed in the X-ray and optical bands, and a
shallow post-break decay slope. The X-ray afterglow exhibits
a very late ( > 20 d) rebrightening or plateau. One possi-
ble explanation for these characteristics is that the event was
observed marginally off-axis, but late-time afterglow moni-
toring will be needed to distinguish between possible mod-
els. Despite the unusual afterglow, the properties of the un-
derlying broad-lined T ype Ic SN are fairly typical for GRB-
SNe. We infer a peak 𝑀𝑟 ≈ − 18.3 mag, an ejecta mass
𝑀ej ≈ 3.5,M⊙ , and a kinetic energy 𝐸K ≈ 5–9 × 1051 erg,
depending on modeling assumptions.
Almost two dozen afterglows have been discovered blindly
by wide-field optical surveys, but AT 2026fgk is only the sec-
ond afterglow bright enough to be detected in ZTF’s flux-
limited (𝑚 < 18.5 mag) survey. We use the results of the flux-
limited experiment to infer a volumetric rate for AT 2026fgk-
like events of 0.30+1.37
−0.29 Gpc −3 yr−1, consistent with the local
on-axis LGRB rate of ∼ 1 Gpc −3 yr−1. Therefore, we con-
clude that the opening angle of the material producing the
optical afterglow at ≈ 1 d is similar to the opening angle of the
initial relativistic material producing the gamma-rays, consis-
tent with conclusions from the ratio of optical afterglows with
and without associated detected GRBs.
Clearly, sub-luminous GRBs are highly diverse;
some do not show any luminous afterglow (e.g.,
GRB 60218/SN 2006aj), and modeling the multiwavelength
emission suggests a quasi-isotropic outflow. Others, such
as GRB 260310A/AT 2026fgk, clearly harbor a successful
and energetic collimated jet. The blind identification of
GRB 260310A /AT 2026fgk demonstrates that optical wide-
field surveys can access the population independently of
satellite coverage, providing a complementary route to mea-
suring the true volumetric rates of long- and low-luminosity
GRBs.

APPENDIX
A. GMOS ADR CORRECTION
To correct for the residual slit losses, we compute a multiplicative throughput correct 𝐶 (𝜆) = 𝑇 (𝜆ref)/𝑇 (𝜆) for each spectrum
where 𝑇 (𝜆) is the fraction of a Gaussian PSF transmitted by a 1 ′′ slit when the centroid is placed by the cross slit ADR offset. The
wavelength dependent offset is computed fromB. Edlén (1966) refractive index formula using pressure and temperature measured
by the Gemini summit weather station at the time of each observation. The wavelength dependent PSF size is scaled from the
median seeing measured by the Mauna Kea Weather Center DIMM monitor during each exposure. The correction is normalized
to unity at 6200Å(the central wavelength), so that the flux at the reference wavelength is preserved and the corrections are largest
at the blue end. The corrected spectra are presented in this work, with both the corrected and raw spectra available.
A.1. Cross-Calibration between X-ray observatories
The photon indices and fluxes we measure from EP/FXT, NuSTAR, and Chandra observations show differences that are larger
than their statistical uncertainties (Table 7, most strikingly between the simultaneous EP/FXT & Chandra observations at ∼41
days (ΓFXT = 1.31 ± 0.13 versus ΓCXO = 2.06 ± 0.24). While rapid spectral evolution cannot be fully excluded, systematic cross-
calibration differences between X-ray observatories are well-documented and likely contribute to these discrepancies. Studies by
the International Astronomical Consortium for High-Energy Calibration (IACHEC; M. Ishida et al. 2011; K. K. Madsen et al.
2017) have shown that photon indices measured by different observatories can disagree by upto ∼10%, and fluxes by ∼15%,
even for simultaneous observations of the same source. These systematic differences persist across missions and arise from
the uncertainties in the effective area calibration — particularly at soft energies ( J. Nevalainen et al. 2010). Furthermore, EP
is a recently launched mission ( W . Yuan et al. 2022) and its cross-calibration with observatories like Chandra and NuSTAR is
currently being characterized (EP collaboration, in-prep). We therefore caution that the spectral parameters measured by different
instruments should not be directly compared without accounting for these systematic uncertainties.
