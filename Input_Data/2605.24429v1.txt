ABSTRACT
Although more than 1000 optical changing-look active galactic nuclei (CLAGNs) have been reported
to date, their physical origin remains unclear, and repeating CLAGNs (RCLAGNs) are still rare. Ex-
panding the CLAGN sample, especially RCLAGNs, is therefore important for constraining the under-
lying mechanism. We systematically search for CLAGNs by cross-matching spectroscopic observations
from the Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS) and the Large Sky Area Multi-Object Fiber Spectroscopic
Telescope (LAMOST), and further use spectra from the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI)
to investigate repeating CL behavior. We identify 45 CLAGNs, including 40 newly reported sources.
The sample is dominated by turn-off events, with 43 turn-off and 2 turn-on sources, possibly because
Type 2 AGNs either lack a detectable broad-line region or have their broad emission lines obscured by
circumnuclear dust. Using DESI as a third spectroscopic epoch, we identify 12 RCLAGNs. This high
detection rate of repeated CL behavior suggests that CL transitions may arise from recurrent physi-
cal processes, such as accretion-rate fluctuations or disk instabilities. In the logM BH–log(Lbol/LEdd)
plane, RCLAGNs further show a clear high–low–high accretion-state evolution, supporting a close link
between CL behavior and recurrent changes in accretion power. Finally, the rest-frame upper limits
on the transition timescales are∼10 yr for the first transition and∼4 yr for the second, reflecting
different survey time baselines rather than intrinsic differences in physical transition timescales.

1.INTRODUCTION
Active galactic nuclei (AGNs) are powered by supermassive black holes (SMBHs) at their centres (Kormendy & Ho
2013). During accretion of surrounding gas and dust, an accretion disk forms, in which a portion of the gravitational
potential energy of the infalling material is converted into electromagnetic radiation. At larger distances, the accretion
disk is surrounded by the broad-line region (BLR), followed by the narrow-line region (NLR) on still larger scales,
while a torus-like dusty structure may enshroud the system on intermediate scales (Urry & Padovani 1995). AGNs are
observationally divided into two main types based on optical spectroscopic features. Type 1 AGNs display both broad
emission lines (BELs; line widths of 1000–20000 km s −1) and narrow emission lines (NELs; 300–1000 km s −1), while
Type 2 AGNs show only NELs. According to the unified model, this difference arises from orientation: in Type 2
AGNs, the BELs are thought to be obscured from our line of sight by a dusty torus (Antonucci 1993; Riffel et al. 2006).
However, it is now established that a population of “true” Type 2 AGNs exists, which appear to lack a BLR rather

than being obscured Type 1 AGNs (Tran 2001; Wang & Zhang 2007; Elitzur & Ho 2009; Shen & Ho 2014; Collin-Zahn
2022). In recent years, spectroscopic observations have revealed a class of optical changing-look AGNs (CLAGNs), in
which the BELs in the optical spectra can appear (turn-on) or disappear (turn-off) on timescales ranging from months
to years (Ricci & Trakhtenbrot 2023). Since their discovery, this phenomenon has attracted significant attention in
astronomy.
A robust identification of CLAGNs requires both the emergence or disappearance of BELs and significant photometric
variability. To date, more than 1000 optical CLAGNs have been identified, primarily through two main approaches
(Potts & Villforth 2021; Jin et al. 2022; Yang et al. 2023; Guo et al. 2024, 2025; Dong et al. 2025b; Chen et al. 2026b).
One approach relies on repeat spectroscopic observations from multi-epoch spectral surveys, such as the Sloan Digital
Sky Survey (SDSS; Pˆ aris et al. 2018), the Large Sky Area Multi-Object Fiber Spectroscopic Telescope (LAMOST;
Cui et al. 2012), the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI; DESI Collaboration et al. 2025), and the 6dF
Galaxy Survey (Jones et al. 2009). Sources exhibiting changes in BELs are identified, and photometric variability
is further examined to rule out BEL variations caused by instrumental effects or other observational uncertainties.
Another approach begins with the selection of candidates based on large-amplitude photometric variability, which are
subsequently confirmed as CLAGNs through follow-up spectroscopic observations that capture such BEL transitions
(e.g. Yang et al. 2018, 2025; MacLeod et al. 2019; Sheng et al. 2020; Zhu et al. 2024, 2025). In both approaches, visual
inspection is often applied as a final verification step.
Studies of CLAGNs point to three main mechanisms for their CL behavior: intrinsic variations in accretion rate,
dust obscuration effects, and tidal disruption events (TDEs). Each mechanism has observational limitations and
cannot explain all cases. Among them, accretion-rate variations are most widely supported, with accretion disk state
transitions driving the observed spectral changes (Noda & Done 2018; Ruan et al. 2019; Sniegowska et al. 2020;
Feng et al. 2021; Ricci & Trakhtenbrot 2023; Wu & Gu 2023). In contrast, the obscuration scenario attributes BEL
variations to dusty material moving into and out of the line of sight (Holt et al. 1980). For most optical CLAGNs, the
transition timescales are much shorter than the expected dynamical timescales of obscuring dust (LaMassa et al. 2015;
Runnoe et al. 2016; Sheng et al. 2017), and this scenario cannot explain the significant variability observed in their
light curves (MacLeod et al. 2016; Ruan et al. 2016; Runnoe et al. 2016). Nevertheless, Zeltyn et al. (2022) reported
a rapid CLAGN transition on a rest-frame timescale of≲2 months, whose spectral evolution favored obscuration,
although the short timescale and mid-infrared variability made this interpretation physically challenging. The TDE
scenario (Merloni et al. 2015; Padmanabhan & Loeb 2021) can explain only a limited number of cases (Merloni et al.
2015) and has difficulty accounting for repeating CLAGNs (RCLAGNs).
It remains unclear whether all CLAGNs undergo repeated CL transitions. Despite the growing number of identified
CLAGNs, recurrent CL events are still rare, because their confirmation requires multi-epoch spectroscopic evidence for
multiple state changes, which is often obtained through long-term monitoring over years to decades. In this context,
the nearby Seyfert galaxy Mrk 1018 provides one of the earliest and most striking examples of repeating CL behavior.
Its Hβemission increased by more than a factor of six within a few years, before fading again and disappearing several
decades later (Cohen et al. 1986; McElroy et al. 2016). Long-term spectroscopic monitoring further revealed that Mrk
1018 experienced a complete sequence of spectral-type transitions, evolving through Seyfert types 1.0, 1.2, 1.5, 1.8,
and 2.0 over a span of approximately 45 years (Lu et al. 2025). A similar evolutionary pattern has been observed in
Mrk 590. The Hβline first emerged in 1989, vanished by 2003 (Denney et al. 2014), and subsequently reappeared
in 2017 together with strong continuum variability (Raimundo et al. 2019; Mandal et al. 2021). More recently, Palit
et al. (2025) reported that Mrk 590 is once again transitioning toward a type 1.2 state, closely resembling its spectral
classification in the 1990s. To date, the number of confirmed RCLAGNs remains small, with fewer than about 50
sources reported in the literature (Wang et al. 2024, 2025; Lyu et al. 2025; Dong et al. 2025a,b).
Therefore, identifying more RCLAGNs through long-term photometric monitoring and multi-epoch spectroscopic
observations is essential for constraining the physical origin and characteristic timescales of the CL phenomenon,
and constitutes one of the main motivations of this work. Low-redshift (z <0.9) CLAGNs have been systematically
identified using SDSS in combination with DESI (Guo et al. 2024, 2025), as well as SDSS together with LAMOST (Dong
et al. 2025b). At higher redshifts, Chen et al. (2026b) explored CLAGNs using SDSS and DESI; however, a systematic
search for high-redshift CLAGNs based on SDSS and LAMOST remains lacking. To address this gap, we conduct
a systematic search for CLAGNs using SDSS and LAMOST without relying on [O III]-based flux recalibration, and
further incorporate DESI spectra to investigate repeating CL behavior and construct an enlarged RCLAGN sample.

2.DATA AND ANALYSIS
2.1.Spectroscopic Data
2.1.1.SDSS Spectroscopy
The SDSS spectroscopic observations were primarily carried out with the Sloan Foundation 2.5 m telescope (Gunn
et al. 2006) at Apache Point Observatory in New Mexico, USA, as part of several successive phases of the survey. In
this work, we adopt spectra from the SDSS Seventeenth Data Release (SDSS DR17; Abdurro’uf et al. 2022), which
contains a total of 5801200 spectroscopic observations. The SDSS spectroscopic pipeline classifies objects as galaxies
(“GALAXY”), quasars (“QSO”), or stars (“STAR”) through template fitting to observed spectra (Hutchinson et al.
2016; Bolton et al. 2012). Among these, 1192886 spectra are classified as stars, while 4608314 spectra are classified
as galaxies or quasars. The SDSS spectra were obtained over multiple survey phases using different instruments. The
spectra from SDSS-I and SDSS-II cover a wavelength range of 3800–9100 ˚A, while those from SDSS-III, obtained
mainly by the Baryon Oscillation Spectroscopic Survey (BOSS), extend the coverage to 3600–10400 ˚A, with a typical
spectral resolution ofR∼2000 (Adelman-McCarthy et al. 2008; Alam et al. 2017).
2.1.2.LAMOST Spectroscopy
LAMOST is a 4 m reflecting Schmidt telescope located at Xinglong Observatory, China, designed for large-scale
spectroscopic surveys. It is equipped with 4000 fibers over a 5 ◦ field of view, enabling the simultaneous acquisition of
thousands of spectra (Cui et al. 2012; Zhao et al. 2012). Our analysis is based on the LAMOST Twelfth Data Release
(LAMOST DR12), which contains a total of 12605485 spectra. Among them, 12236412 are classified as stars, while
369073 are identified as galaxies or quasars by the LAMOST pipeline. In this work, we use only the low-resolution
LAMOST spectra, which span the optical wavelength range from 3700 to 9000 ˚A and have a characteristic spectral
resolution ofR∼1800. The LAMOST spectrograph adopts a dual-arm design, with a blue arm covering 3700–5900
˚A and a red arm covering 5700–9000 ˚A (Luo et al. 2012; Du et al. 2016).
2.1.3.DESI Spectroscopy
DESI is a Stage IV ground-based spectroscopic survey carried out with the 4 m Mayall Telescope at Kitt Peak
National Observatory (Levi et al. 2013; DESI Collaboration et al. 2016a,b). The instrument is equipped with 5000
robotic fibers over an 8 deg 2 field of view, allowing for the simultaneous observation of thousands of targets. DESI
spectra span a wavelength range of 3600–9800˚A, divided into three channels: blue (3600–5900˚A), green (5660–7220 ˚A),
and red (7470–9800 ˚A), with typical spectral resolutions ofR∼2100, 3200, and 4100, respectively (DESI Collaboration
et al. 2016b, 2022). In this work, we use DESI spectra from Survey Validation and early Year 1 observations released
as part of DESI Data Release 1 (DR1). We further adopt the AGN/QSO Value-Added Catalog (VAC) for DESI
DR1, which provides improved galaxy and quasar classifications and redshift estimates based on the Redrock pipeline,
QuasarNet, and FastSpecFit measurements (Alexander et al. 2023; Chaussidon et al. 2023; Moustakas et al. 2023).
2.2.Cross-matching of SDSS and LAMOST Data
We perform a positional cross-matching between the LAMOST DR12 and SDSS DR17 catalogs using the TOPCAT
software (Taylor 2005). A matching radius of 2 ′′ is adopted, and all possible matches within this radius are retained.
We further require a redshift consistency of ∆z <0.002 between the SDSS and LAMOST spectra. Applying these
criteria, we obtain a total of 272924 matched SDSS–LAMOST spectral pairs. From this parent sample, we retain only
sources classified as GALAXY or QSO, yielding 25904 GALAXY–QSO and QSO–QSO spectral pairs for subsequent
analysis. These spectral pairs form the parent sample for the subsequent CLAGN selection procedure described in
Section 3.

and its narrow component with one Gaussian, while the C IV broad component is modeled with three Gaussians (Shen

2.5.Photometric Data
Although the spectra show clear BEL variations, establishing a CLAGN typically requires corresponding photometric
variability. We therefore examine multi-epoch light curves to confirm that the observed changes are intrinsic rather
than caused by observational effects. In the optical band, we utilize data from the Catalina Real-Time Transient Survey
(CRTS; Drake et al. 2009), the All-Sky Automated Survey for SuperNovae (ASAS-SN; Kochanek et al. 2017), and the
Zwicky Transient Facility (ZTF; Bellm et al. 2019). CRTS has provided V-band monitoring since 2007, ASAS-SN has
conducted all-sky observations since 2013, and ZTF has delivered high-cadenceg- andr-band data since 2018. In the
mid-infrared, we use data from the Wide-field Infrared Survey Explorer (WISE; Wright et al. 2010), including both
the original WISE mission (since 2010) and its reactivation phase (NEOWISE; since 2013).
For the CRTS data, we applied a density-based filtering procedure to remove outliers. Specifically, we constructed a
magnitude histogram with 20 bins, identified the most populated bin corresponding to the dominant magnitude distri-
bution, and retained only data points within±0.5 mag of the peak-bin center. For ZTF, we first selected measurements
withcatflags= 0, indicating good photometric quality. The data were then grouped by filter (g, r, i), and outliers
were removed in each band using the same density-based method as adopted for CRTS. For ASAS-SN, we retained
only measurements withQuality= ’G’ and excluded upper-limit detections. We further removed measurements with
unusually large photometric uncertainties using a 3σcriterion applied to the distribution of magnitude errors. For
WISE/NEOWISE, we retained only measurements with reliable photometric quality flags (ph qual= AA, AB, BA,
or BB).
2.6.Photometric Intercalibration and Final Spectral Flux Correction
Given the differences in photometric apertures and zero-points among the surveys, intercalibration is required before
combining the light curves. We adopt the ZTFr-band light curve as the reference system and align the CRTS and
ASAS-SN measurements to it. Since the variability amplitude differences between nearby optical bands are small
(Wilhite et al. 2005), we assume that the intrinsic variability amplitudes are comparable and correct only for a
constant magnitude offset between surveys (Dong et al. 2025b). We apply a simple additive scaling (Peterson et al.
1991),m ′ =m+C, wheremandm ′ are the magnitudes before and after alignment, andCis a constant offset relative
to the ZTFr-band system. To determine the calibration offsets, we adopt a sequential two-step procedure. First, the
ASAS-SN V-band light curve is aligned to the ZTFr-band reference system. Matched data pairs are constructed by
selecting measurements obtained within±5 days. The magnitude differences of all matched pairs are calculated, and
the mean difference is adopted as the offset between ASAS-SN V and ZTFr, denoted asC V−r . Second, the CRTS
V-band light curve is aligned to the ASAS-SN V-band light curve using the same±5 day matching criterion. The
mean magnitude difference of all matched pairs is taken as the offset between CRTS and ASAS-SN, denoted asC V−V .

For each spectral pair, we retain it if at least one of the two spectra exhibits a reliable BEL component. In this
subsection, we take the Hβline as an example; the same criteria are applied to the other BELs. We rely on the spectral

broad components or weak broad-line contributions dominated by noise. To ensure robustness, we apply the following
criteria on the broad Hβcomponent, following Dong et al. (2025b) with minor modifications:
1. Objects with best-fit broad HβFWHM exceeding 13000 km s −1 are excluded to avoid spurious fitting solutions.

3. The broad Hβcomponent must contribute significantly to the total flux around the line center, requiring (FHβ,br +

3.2.Identification of RCLAGNs

9
To search for repeating CL behavior, we first focus on the 40 newly identified CLAGNs in this work and cross-match
them with DESI DR1 using the same positional and redshift criteria adopted in Section 2.2, requiring an angular
separation smaller than 2 ′′ and a redshift difference of ∆z <0.002. Among these 40 sources, 14 have available DESI
spectra and therefore provide three-epoch spectroscopic coverage from SDSS, LAMOST, and DESI.
For these 14 sources, we examine BEL variability across the three spectroscopic epochs using the same criteria
adopted for the CLAGN selection. We require significant broad-line variability, withR p >0.4 andR s >0.4, to-
gether with visual confirmation of the appearance or disappearance of broad emission-line components. A source is
classified as an RCLAGN if it exhibits two distinct spectral-state transitions across the three epochs, corresponding
to either an “appearance–disappearance–reappearance” or a “disappearance–appearance–disappearance” sequence of
BELs. Among the 14 newly identified CLAGNs with DESI spectroscopy, 7 satisfy these criteria and are classified as
RCLAGNs. This corresponds to an RCLAGN detection rate of 50% among the newly identified CLAGNs with DESI
spectroscopy.
Our original aim was to report RCLAGNs among the newly identified CLAGNs in this work. However, the high
fraction of RCLAGNs motivated us to apply the same procedure to the 51 SDSS–LAMOST CLAGNs reported by
Dong et al. (2025b). Sixteen of these sources have available DESI spectra, among which 5 show clear repeated CL
transitions. Although these 5 objects were previously known as CLAGNs, they exhibit repeated CL transitions and
are therefore included in the final RCLAGN sample. Combining the two subsamples, we obtain 12 RCLAGNs. A
example is shown in Figure 3(b).
In terms of photometric variability, all RCLAGNs show significant brightness changes consistent with their spec-
tral evolution. Multiwavelength light curves from ZTF, ASAS-SN, CRTS, and WISE/NEOWISE reveal pronounced
brightening or dimming during the corresponding epochs, with variability trends that closely track the strengthening
or weakening of the BELs. This consistency further rules out instrumental or observational effects and supports an
intrinsic origin of the observed changes.

4.DISCUSSION
4.1.Comparison with Previous CLAGN Searches

10
Dong et al. (2025b) identified 51 CLAGNs by cross-matching SDSS and LAMOST, with the redshift limited to
z <0.9 due to the requirement of using the [O III]λ5007 emission line for flux recalibration of LAMOST spectra. In
this work, we adopt an alternative calibration strategy based on SDSSgriphotometric magnitudes (Fukugita et al.
1996; Wang et al. 2018; Liu et al. 2022), which does not rely on NELs and thus allows us to extend the search for
CLAGNs beyond this redshift limitation. As described in Section 2.3, we first perform an initial flux scaling using
SDSSpsfMagmeasurements to select candidate CLAGNs. For the refined sample, we further utilize quasi-simultaneous
photometric data from ZTF, CRTS, and ASAS-SN to improve the spectral calibration and ensure consistency with
the contemporaneous flux level. Based on this procedure, we identify a total of 45 CLAGNs, among which 40 are
newly discovered in this work. The redshift distribution of our sample spans 0.036≲z≲0.338, with no detections
atz >0.9. Despite our calibration method not relying on NELs, the absence of higher-redshift sources likely reflects
the limited depth of LAMOST, as high-redshift AGNs generally have lower signal-to-noise ratios, making it difficult
to identify changes in BELs.
In terms of spectral transitions, the sample is dominated by turn-off events, with 43 sources exhibiting this behavior,
whereas only 2 sources show turn-on transitions. Similar results have also been reported in previous studies. For
example, Zeltyn et al. (2024) found 76 dimming and 37 brightening CLAGNs in the first-year SDSS-V sample. Amrutha
et al. (2024) estimated lower-limit CLAGN rates of 1.7% for turn-on events and 9.6% for turn-off events. Similarly, only
8 of the 51 CLAGNs reported by Dong et al. (2025b) were turn-on events. This asymmetry is physically understandable.
A decline in accretion activity can reduce the ionizing continuum and weaken the BELs, causing a Type 1 AGN to
undergo a turn-off transition. By contrast, a Type 2 AGN is difficult to undergo a turn-on event, because it may either
lack a detectable BLR or have its BELs obscured by circumnuclear dust. In such cases, even if the accretion activity
increases, observable BELs may not emerge.
4.2.The Incidence of Repeating Changing-look Behavior
We cross-match these 40 newly identified CLAGNs with DESI DR1 spectroscopy and find that 14 sources have three-
epoch spectral coverage (SDSS, LAMOST, and DESI). Among these, 7 objects exhibit clear repeating CL behavior,
corresponding to a high fraction of∼50%. This unexpectedly high occurrence rate motivates us to further examine
whether such behavior is also present in previously reported CLAGN samples. We therefore cross-match the 51
CLAGNs from Dong et al. (2025b) with DESI DR1 and identify 16 sources with available DESI spectra, among which
5 are confirmed as RCLAGNs, yielding a comparable fraction of∼31%. The consistency between these independent
samples indicates that repeating CL behavior is likely common among CLAGNs when multi-epoch spectroscopy is
available.
In the general AGN population, the detection rate of CLAGNs is known to be low. For example, Zeltyn et al.
(2024) found that only∼0.4% of∼29000 AGNs with repeated SDSS-V spectroscopy exhibit CL phenomena, while
Wang et al. (2024) reported that∼0.3% of AGNs undergo CL transitions over timescales of 5–20 yr. Amrutha
et al. (2024) further derived turn-on and turn-off CLAGN detection rates of∼1.7% and∼9.6%, respectively, which
should be regarded as lower limits due to incomplete temporal sampling that may miss transitions occurring and
reverting between spectroscopic epochs. At higher redshift (z >0.9), Chen et al. (2026b) reported that the fraction
of CL quasars is as low as∼0.042% based on DESI DR1 and SDSS DR18 spectroscopy. Although these rates vary
depending on sample selection and methodology, they consistently indicate that CLAGNs are rare among the overall
AGN population. In contrast, our results suggest that the fraction of repeating CL behavior among known CLAGNs
is higher. This high detection rate of repeating CL behavior suggests that CL transitions are likely driven by recurrent
physical processes, such as accretion rate fluctuations or disk instabilities. It also implies that CL phenomena may
be more common in the AGN than currently observed, with their low overall detection rate limited by observational
cadence and time baseline.

Figure 5 shows the distribution of CLAGNs and RCLAGNs in the logM BH–log(Lbol/LEdd) plane. Compared with

high to low and back to high Eddington ratios, suggesting that they may experience multiple crossings of the critical
accretion regime during their CL phases.
For our sample, the Eddington ratios in the high state span from 0.008 to 0.276, with a mean value of∼0.07,
while those in the low state range from 0.002 to 0.077, with a mean of∼0.03. This demonstrates a clear decrease
in accretion rate from the high to low state, consistent with the CL phenomenon being associated with significant
variations in accretion power. Our results are consistent with previous studies (e.g., MacLeod et al. 2019; Green
et al. 2022; Lyu et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2024), which suggest that CLAGNs tend to have lower Eddington ratios
compared to normal quasars, and may occupy an intermediate regime between luminous QSOs and low-luminosity
AGNs. Interestingly, both the high- and low-state Eddington ratios in our sample are close to the critical value of
∼10 −2, at which a transition in the accretion mode has been proposed (Ruan et al. 2019). This critical threshold is
analogous to state transitions observed in X-ray binaries, where the accretion flow changes from a radiatively efficient
thin disk to a radiatively inefficient mode. The proximity of our sources to this regime supports the scenario that the
CL phenomenon is driven by changes in the accretion state.
5.SUMMARY
In this work, we systematically search for CLAGNs by cross-matching multi-epoch spectroscopic observations from
SDSS DR17 and LAMOST DR12. To avoid the redshift limitation imposed by narrow-line-based flux calibration, we
first recalibrate the LAMOST spectra using SDSSgriphotometry and then refine the flux scaling of visually selected
candidates with quasi-simultaneous multi-epoch photometric data. We further incorporate DESI DR1 spectroscopy
to investigate repeating CL behavior. The main results are summarized as follows:
1. We identify a sample of 45 CLAGNs from the SDSS–LAMOST matched spectra. Among them, 40 are newly
reported in this work. The sample is dominated by turn-off events: 43 sources exhibit the disappearance of BELs,
while only 2 sources show turn-on behavior. This asymmetry is physically understandable, since Type 2 AGNs may
either lack a detectable BLR or have their BEL obscured by circumnuclear dust, making the emergence of observable
BELs difficult to identify.
2. By incorporating DESI DR1 spectra as a third spectroscopic epoch, we search for RCLAGNs. Among the 40 newly
identified CLAGNs, 14 have spectra from SDSS, LAMOST, and DESI, of which 7 display clear repeated transitions.
We further examine the CLAGN sample reported by Dong et al. (2025b) and identify another 5 RCLAGNs with DESI
observations. In total, we report 12 RCLAGNs. This high detection rate of repeating CL behavior suggests that CL
transitions are driven by recurrent physical processes, such as accretion rate fluctuations or disk instabilities.
3. The rest-frame upper limits on the transition timescales show different distributions for the first and second
transitions. The first transitions, mainly constrained by SDSS–LAMOST, are clustered around∼10 yr, whereas the
second transitions, traced by LAMOST–DESI, are concentrated around∼4 yr. This difference is primarily caused by
the distinct temporal baselines of the surveys rather than by an intrinsic difference in the physical timescales of the
transitions.
4. We estimate black hole masses using the broad Hαand Hβemission lines measured from the high-state spectra.
The two mass estimates show good agreement, with a best-fit slope of 1.03 andR 2 = 0.84. Since Hαis stronger and
more robustly measured, we adopt the Hα-based estimates as the representative black hole masses of our sample. The
resulting black hole masses are distributed over log(M BH/M⊙)∼6.9−8.5.
5. The Eddington ratios of our CLAGN sample are lower than those of typical quasars. In the high state,λ Edd
spans 0.008–0.276, with a mean value of∼0.07, while in the low state it ranges from 0.002 to 0.077, with a mean
value of∼0.03. For RCLAGNs, the Eddington ratios evolve from high to low and then back to high states, closely
following the repeated spectral transitions. These results support the interpretation that CL behavior is associated
with substantial changes in the accretion power.
