ABSTRACT
Population III (Pop III) stars are the first generation of stars to form in the universe, emerging from primordial gas
composed mainly of hydrogen and helium. They play a crucial role in ending the cosmic dark ages and initiating reion-
ization. In this work, we present a comprehensive framework for modelling Pop III stars in cosmological simulations.
This includes three key components: (1) an enhanced thermochemical network that tracks the equilibrium abundances
of key catalytic species such asH+
2 andH −, which are crucial for forming molecular hydrogen in primordial gas; (2)
detailed stellar spectra of Pop III stars computed fromMESAevolutionary tracks andTLUSTYatmosphere models; and
(3) comprehensive supernova feedback, including both Core-Collapse and Pair-Instability supernovae, with detailed
elemental yields. We implement these improvements inAREPO-RTand test them using cosmological zoom-in simula-
tions of a1.95×10 9M⊙ halo atz= 3. Our results show that Pop III stars form atz >13and continue forming
untilz∼5, significantly affecting early galaxy evolution through radiation and energetic supernova feedback. The
enhanced thermochemistry enables more efficient gas cooling, while Pop III feedback creates photo-heated diffuse gas
and drives distinct metal enrichment patterns at10< z <6. The choice of IMF for Pop III stars critically determines
the balance between radiative and mechanical feedback, with top-heavy choices producing stronger feedback and
more metals but retaining less metal-enriched gas within the halo. Finally, we show that high-energy radiation from
Pop III stars is necessary to explain the recent high-equivalent-width observations of the Heiiline from a galaxy at
z∼11.

1 INTRODUCTION
The Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB) decoupled from
baryonic matter at a redshift of about 1100, allowing struc-
tures to grow unimpeded. Structure formation was then
driven by gravitational instabilities in a hierarchical, bottom-
up fashion. This led to the formation of the first collapsed
structures in the cosmic dark ages, "minihaloes" with masses
≲10 7M⊙ (Hartwig et al. 2022). These minihaloes served as
sites for the formation of the first stars in the universe, at
z≈30(Klessen & Glover 2023). This first generation of
stars is known as Population III (Pop III) stars, which are
characterized by forming in primordial gas that contains only
elements produced in Big Bang nucleosynthesis, or, more pre-
cisely, is free of metals except for trace amounts of lithium.
The birth of these stars marks the end of the cosmic dark
ages and the start of Cosmic Dawn (CD) and the Epoch of
Reionization (EoR).

As galaxy formation progresses, haloes with virial temper-
atures above8000K cool efficiently through de-excitation
lines of atomic hydrogen (atomic cooling haloes; Sutherland
& Dopita 1993). However, the pristine gas can cool further to
200K through excitation of the vibrational states of molec-
ular hydrogen (H2; Greif 2015). Further cooling can occur
only under special conditions in very massive haloes that al-
low efficient formation of deuterated hydrogen (HD) (Glover
& Abel 2008; Nagakura & Omukai 2005). Nevertheless, these
temperatures remain higher than the typical temperature of
thepresent-dayinterstellarmedium(ISM)(∼10K),achieved
through metal line cooling (Klessen & Glover 2016). This in-
creases the Jeans mass for the formation of Pop III stars and
reduces fragmentation, leading to a comparatively top-heavy
Initial Mass Function (IMF) (Klessen & Glover 2023). While
low-mass stars can still form by fragmentation of the gaseous
disk (see, e.g., Greif et al. 2012; Stacy & Bromm 2013; Prole
et al. 2022) or due to turbulence in star-forming clouds (see,
e.g., Turk et al. 2009; Clark et al. 2011), the overall consensus
is that Pop III stars are, on average, more massive than their
present-day counterparts.

Like all stars, the fate and evolution of the Pop III stars
strongly depend on the initial mass. Massive stars (≳8 M⊙)
either end their lives as a Core Collapse Supernova (CCSN)
or, in more extreme cases, as the more energetic Pair-
instability Supernova (PISN) (Fowler & Hoyle 1964; Heger
& Woosley 2002; Woosley 2017). These supernovae inject en-
ergy and metals into their host haloes, which can, in some
cases, disrupt the halo and enrich the surrounding inter-
galactic medium (IGM) with metals. The enriched ISM/IGM
cools more efficiently, initiating the formation of Population
II (PopII) stars (Greif et al. 2010; Jaacks et al. 2018; Chi-
aki & Wise 2019). While the lack of metals in Pop III stars
prevents mass loss through stellar winds (Kudritzki 2002; Kr-
tička & Kubát 2006b,a, 2009), they produce a large amount
of Lyman-Werner (LW) radiation (11.2−13.6 eV) and
hydrogen- and helium-ionizing radiation (Heger & Woosley
2010; Schauer et al. 2017; Hartwig et al. 2022). LW pho-
tons can penetrate the IGM and dissociateH 2, preventing
gas cooling and delaying star formation in distant minihaloes
(Haiman et al. 2000; Ahn et al. 2009). In addition to regulat-
ing the star formation rate (SFR) of the Pop III stars, this
process can also lead to the formation of extremely massive
stars up to10 5 M⊙ and can seed supermassive black holes
(SMBH) at high redshifts (Wise et al. 2008; Smith & Bromm
2019; Inayoshi et al. 2020).
Despite extensive searches, Pop III stars have yet to be
directly observed in the nearby local Universe (Christlieb
et al. 2002; Cayrel et al. 2004; Keller et al. 2014; Howes et al.
2015). This is because their formation peaks atz≈15−30
(Klessen & Glover 2023), making it unlikely that massive
Pop III stars would survive to the present day. Moreover,
even a small amount of metal enrichment leads to more ef-
ficient cooling and the formation of Pop II stars, which will
dominate the observed flux (Johnson et al. 2013; Sarmento
et al. 2019; Liu & Bromm 2020b). At high redshifts, even
a massive1000 M ⊙ star will be too faint to be detected
by theJames Webb Space Telescope(JWST) (Schauer et al.
2020). However, Pop III stars can be detected by observing
the energetic SN explosions that occur at the end of their
lifetime (Scannapieco et al. 2005; Hummel et al. 2012; Ven-
ditti et al. 2024), by observing highly lensed caustic transits
of individual star clusters (Welch et al. 2022), or through
observations of galaxies with significant Pop III populations
(Scannapiecoetal.2003;Maiolinoetal.2026).Unfortunately,
their short lifetimes limit the number of expected observa-
tions. Pop III stars can also be studied by quantifying their
impact on the21cm signal from neutral hydrogen (Madau &
Dickinson 2014; Gessey-Jones et al. 2022; Gessey-Jones et al.
2025;Maggetal.2022),orbystudyingthegravitationalwave
signatures from the mergers of Pop III star remnants (Dayal
et al. 2019; Tang et al. 2020; Tanikawa et al. 2022). Until now,
we have only general observational clues about the history of
Pop III star formation from deep Hubble Space Telescope
(HST) observations up toz∼8(McLure et al. 2013; Finkel-
stein 2016; Bouwens et al. 2021; Mason et al. 2015; McLeod
et al. 2015), which are now being complemented byJWST
observations (Donnan et al. 2023; Davis et al. 2024; Adams
et al. 2024; Harikane et al. 2023; Maiolino et al. 2026) push-
ing out toz∼15. Therefore, increasing the probability of
observing Pop III stars requires better modelling and under-
standing of how galaxies hosting them are distributed, both
spatially and as a function of redshift.
Modelling the formation and evolution of Pop III stars
presents considerable challenges, as these stars originate in
minihaloes with masses≲10 7 M⊙, at redshifts ranging from
10≤z≤30(Klessen & Glover 2023). Consequently, simulat-
ing their formation necessitates a combination of high spatial
resolution and extensive volume coverage to accurately cap-
ture the earliest structures formed in the universe. Most ex-
isting simulation efforts have focused on the high-resolution
‘zoom-in’ technique to capture the collapse of primordial gas
clouds into Pop III stars. These simulations include a variety
of physical processes like magnetic fields which are theorized
to reduce gas fragmentation (Peters et al. 2014; Sharda et al.
2021), a detailed thermochemical network that includesH−,
HD (Glover 2005; Nishijima et al. 2024), streaming veloc-
ity between the dark and baryonic matter (Tseliakhovich &
Hirata 2010; Schauer et al. 2021; Lake et al. 2024) and ra-
diation transport of LW photons (Haiman et al. 1997; Greif
et al. 2011; Schauer et al. 2021; Jaura et al. 2022; Sugimura
et al. 2023). Most of these simulations predict that the lack
of efficient cooling gives rise to a top-heavy IMF (Hosokawa
et al. 2011; Chon et al. 2024; Sugimura et al. 2020). The ex-
act metallicity (Zcrit) at which the IMF switches from being
top-heavy to a regular one is still unclear. If gas cooling is
dominates thenZ crit ∼10 −3 −10 −4 Z⊙ (Bromm et al. 2001;
Maio et al. 2010), or if the dust cooling is dominant, then
Zcrit ∼10 −6 −10 −4 Z⊙ is needed (Bromm & Loeb 2003;
Schneider et al. 2003, 2006).
Large-scale cosmological simulations build upon the in-
sights gained from small-scale studies of the Pop III ini-
tial mass function (IMF) to model the complete Epoch
of Reionization (EoR) of the intergalactic medium (IGM).
These models are typically executed through either semi-
analytical frameworks or full hydrodynamical simulations
(Somerville et al. 2012; Mason et al. 2015; Becker et al. 2015;
Bosman et al. 2022; O’Shea et al. 2015). The vast volumes
of these simulations facilitate robust statistical analyses and
investigations into the redshift dependence of the Pop III star
formation rate (SFR). However, these large cosmic volumes
inherently restrict mass resolution, preventing the explicit
resolution of the minihaloes where the first stars form. Con-
sequently, this introduces significant uncertainty regarding
the Pop III SFR and the overall contribution of these stars
to cosmic reionization. Furthermore, the treatment of stel-
lar feedback is often inconsistent; while some models imple-
ment explicit sub-grid prescriptions for Pop III stars based
on the metallicity of newly formed star particles, others
simply apply standard Population II feedback parameters
to Pop III populations. Moreover, the omission of Lyman-
Werner (LW) radiative transfer or detailed molecular hydro-
gen chemistry in many studies limits the physical fidelity of
their results. Even when simulations feature exceptionally
high resolutions, detailed sub-grid modelling, and compre-
hensive thermo-chemical networks, computational expense
typically dictates that they only run down toz >10. This
early termination restricts their predictive power and pre-
cludes a holistic understanding of early galaxy formation and
the transition from the Pop III→PopII/I regimes. Examples
of studies that analyse the formation and evolution of low-
metallicity stars in these contexts includeTHESAN-HR(Borrow
et al. 2023), simulations with dustyGadget (Venditti et al.
2023; Di Cesare et al. 2023),FLARES(Lovell et al. 2021; Vi-
jayan et al. 2021), the Renaissance Simulations (O’Shea et al.

In this paper we aim to develop a comprehensive frame-
work for modelling Pop III stars in a cosmological context.
We implement three key elements relevant to Pop III star
formation and feedback:
(i) an enhanced thermochemistry network based on
Gnedin & Kravtsov (2011) that enhances molecular hydro-
gen chemistry by including the equilibrium abundances ofH+
2
andH − species, which act as catalysts for the formation of
molecular hydrogen in primordial environments.
(ii) detailed spectra for Pop III stars, computed using the
stellar evolution codeMESA(Modules for Experiments in Stel-
lar Astrophysics; Mirouh et al. 2023) coupled with stellar
atmospheric modelling usingTLUSTY(Hubeny 1988), to ac-
curately model the radiative feedback from the first stars.
(iii) comprehensive supernova feedback from Pop III stars,
including both Core-Collapse and Pair-Instability Super-
novae based on (Heger & Woosley 2002, 2010) with detailed
elemental yields.
We implement these improvements in theAREPO-RT(Kan-
nan et al. 2019) radiation-hydrodynamics code and test the
model using cosmological zoom-in simulations within the
Thesan-Zoomframework (introduced in Kannan et al. 2025).
The paper is structured as follows: In Section 2, we de-
scribe our methods, including the new thermochemistry net-
work (§2.1), modelling of Pop III stellar spectra (§2.2), and
feedback and yields from Pop III stars (§2.3). We test the new
Pop III model using cosmological simulations in §3, and anal-
yse the gas and stellar mass evolution and the Pop III star
formationhistory.Wealsodescribehowournewphysicsmod-

2 METHODS
We use theThesan-Zoomframework (Kannan et al. 2025)
to model the impact of Pop III stars. Briefly, the simula-
tions are performed usingAREPO-RT(Kannan et al. 2019; Zier
et al. 2024), a radiation-hydrodynamic (RHD) extension to
themovingmeshcodeAREPO(Springel2010).AREPOsolvesthe
equations of (radiation-) hydrodynamics on an unstructured
meshthatadaptstogasflows.Thismeshisconstructedbydi-
viding space into Voronoi cells, which are constantly adjusted
as the gas moves. Solutions to the hydrodynamic equations
are obtained in a quasi-Lagrangian manner, by solving them
at interfaces between moving mesh cells in the rest frame
of the interface. The use of second-order Runge-Kutta time
integration in conjunction with a least square fit (LSF) gra-
dient estimate, which performs well even on highly distorted
meshes (Pakmor et al. 2015), ensures higher-order accuracy.
Additionally,themeshisregularlyregularisedusingamethod
described in Vogelsberger et al. (2012).
The gravitational forces are calculated using the Hybrid
Tree-PM approach. This method estimates short-range forces
through a hierarchical oct-tree algorithm (Barnes & Hut
1986), while long-range forces are computed using the parti-
cle mesh (PM) method. The PM method bins particles onto a
grid, and the gravitational potential is derived by solving the
Poisson equation using the Fourier method. Furthermore, if
the count of active particles falls below a specific threshold,
the gravitational force is computed through direct summa-
tion. This approach is particularly beneficial in large-scale
simulations, where the computational cost of direct summa-
tion is less than the tree algorithm for the lowest time-bins,
typically populated by only a few active particles. A hier-
archical time integration approach is employed to construct
the tree only for the currently active particle set, accelerating
gravity calculations. This is especially useful as the time-bin
hierarchy can become very deep in our simulations (Springel
et al. 2021a).
Star formation is treated stochastically, with self-
gravitating, Jeans-unstable gas cells with densities above
10cm −3 considered eligible to form stars. Each star parti-
cle (non-Pop III stars) represents a stellar population, with
an assumed Chabrier initial mass function (Chabrier 2003)
with a minimum and maximum stellar mass of0.1 M⊙ and
100 M ⊙ respectively. Supernovae (SN) feedback and stellar
winds are implemented using the Stars and MUltiphase Gas
in GaLaxiEs (SMUGGLE) model (Marinacci et al. 2019),
with further improvements discussed in Kannan et al. (2025).
Stars with massM ∗ >8M ⊙ explode as SN type II, and en-
ergy, momentum, and mass are injected into the closest gas
cells, along with metals and dust (Vogelsberger et al. 2013;
McKinnon et al. 2016). The simulations also include feedback
from SN type Ia and stellar winds from O-B and asymptotic
giant branch stars. Moreover, early stellar feedback, which
disrupts molecular clouds, is added to regulate feedback be-
fore the first SN explosions, injecting momentum for the first
5Myr after star particles are formed.
Radiationfieldsareevolved(Kannanetal.2019)bysolving
a set of coupled hyperbolic conservation equations for photon
number density and photon flux, closed using the M1 scheme
(Levermore 1984; Dubroca & Feugeas 1999). Radiation fields
are coupled to the gas through a six-species non-equilibrium
thermochemical network that models the ionization state and
cooling/heating from molecular and atomic hydrogen and he-
lium. This framework allows us to self-consistently follow the
photoionization, photoheating, and radiation pressure from
local sources on the surrounding medium. Further improve-
ments to this framework for modelling the formation and evo-
lution of Pop III stars are detailed below.

band, most photons are absorbed byHeI, and in the EUV3
band, photons are absorbed byHeII.
2.2 Radiation output from Pop III stars
A substantial portion of the energy output from massive stars
is emitted as radiation during their long-lived main-sequence
(MS) phase (see, e.g., Heger & Woosley 2010; Agertz et al.
2013). In general, the spectra of Pop III stars are thought to
differ significantly from those of present-day metal-enriched
stars, as we expect them to be more massive on average.
In this work, we combine stellar evolutionary tracks from
MESA(Paxton et al. 2011, 2013, 2015, 2019; Jermyn et al.
2023) withTLUSTY(Hubeny 1988) stellar atmosphere models
to accurately model Pop III spectra. A brief outline of this
process is given below:
(i) Model the evolution of metal-free stars at120different
masses covering[0.1,1000] M ⊙, initialized at BBN propor-
tions, usingMESA.
(ii) Using the evolutionary tracks fromMESA, and calculate
the spectra of the stars at each point during the MS evolution
usingTLUSTY.
(iii) Combine the evolutionary track history to compute
stellar spectra for each star over a given time period,
Lν(M, t).
(iv) Integrate overL ν(M, t), weighted by the initial mass
function (IMF), to compute the Pop III spectrum for a single
stellar population.

state of a Pop III star depends on the mass of the He core
at the end of the star’s life. It can die as a white dwarf, neu-
tron star, or black hole after exploding as a supernova, or
it can directly collapse into a black hole without undergoing
an explosion. The mass ranges for different end states remain
uncertain. For non-rotating Pop III stars with negligible mass
loss, the helium core mass (MHe) scales linearly with the ini-
tial mass asMHe = (13/24)×(M ⋆ −20)(Heger & Woosley
2002). Thus, it is common to discuss end states in terms of
the star’s initial mass.
For stars with masses10−25 M ⊙, the core collapse of the
central degenerate core triggers a type II supernova explosion
(hereafter referred to as a core-collapse supernova or CCSN)
that leaves behind a neutron star remnant. For stars with
25−40 M ⊙, the core collapse is unable to completely unbind
the stellar envelope, and fallback of the material is expected
to collapse it into a black hole (Klessen & Glover 2023; Heger
& Woosley 2010). Between40−70 M⊙, the stars also directly
collapse into a black hole, but in the process they eject a
significant amount of energy and metals into the surround-
ing medium (Heger & Woosley 2010). For stars with masses
of about70−100 M ⊙ to260 M ⊙, pair instabilities occur,
and they die as supernovae that may or may not leave be-
hind a compact remnant (Heger & Woosley 2002; Chen et al.
2014; Woosley 2017). These pair instabilities occur during
the post-carbon-burning stage. The core of the star reaches
a high-temperature, low-density regime that allows the pro-
duction of electron-positron pairs. Pair production reduces
pressure support and effectively lowers the adiabatic index
(γ)in the radiation-dominated plasma. This results in rapid
contraction of the core, with free-fall acceleration in the inner
part of the C/O core. The increase in temperature following
the collapse triggers rapid thermonuclear burning that can
fuse heavier elements, and the energy released in this process
is comparable to or higher than the binding energy of the
star. The contraction to higher temperature occurs with lit-
tle resistance compared with what the star would otherwise
encounter, making the star unstable. This instability results
in an implosion of the core, which is reversed by nuclear burn-
ing. If enough burning occurs, the star may be completely dis-
rupted in a single pulse – this only happens for initial mass
>140 M ⊙ and is called a pair-instability supernova (PISN),
which is one of the most energetic events in the universe.
Otherwise, the core expands for a while, kicks off the outer
layers, including any residual hydrogen layers, and then col-
lapses until it encounters the instability again – this is called
a pulsational pair-instability supernova (PPISN). For stars
more massive than260 M⊙, the star directly collapses into a
black hole without any supernova explosion.

Modelling the impact of injected SN energy is non-trivial,
particularly in cosmological simulations with limited resolu-
tion (for e.g. see discussions in Scannapieco et al. 2012). The
primary challenge is to properly capture theSedov-Taylor
phaseof the SN remnant – an early expansion phase that
conserves energy. During this phase, a highly pressurized cen-
tral gas bubble expands outward, engulfing and accelerating
surrounding ISM gas. This expansion significantly amplifies
the radial momentum of the SN shell, potentially reaching 10
times the initial value (Martizzi et al. 2015). This substan-
tial momentum boost plays a crucial role in regulating star
formation (Ostriker & Shetty 2011) and propelling galactic
winds (Kim & Ostriker 2018). Hence, an effective SN imple-
mentationthataccuratelycapturesboththetotalmomentum
and energy injection is essential for realistic galaxy formation
simulations, particularly when the Sedov-Taylor phase is not
resolved. For the Pop III stars, we follow the same prescrip-
tion as Marinacci et al. (2019). The only caveat is that for
Pop III stars, we assume no mass loss during the MS evolu-
tion (as Pop III stars do not have metal-line driven winds),
and hence chemical enrichment occurs only after a star dies.

distinct thermodynamic evolution seen in the Pop3 variations

the next generation of stars, which will be explored in future
works. For now, we refer the reader to Zier et al. (2025) for
additional discussion of the origin and evolution of metal-free
gas inThesan-Zoomgalaxies.

4 DISCUSSION
4.1 F eedback from Pop III stars
Differences across model variations, such as gas and stellar
mass, metal enrichment patterns, and the evolution of phase-
space properties, can generally be attributed to variations
in SN feedback. Fig. 15 plots the energy injected per SN
(both SNII and SNIa) as a function of redshift for different
model variations. We note that direct one-to-one comparisons
between runs are unfair due to the stochastic nature of the
subgrid model, so we focus on qualitative differences. The
simulations predict that forz >7, thePop3 M250run, on
average, has the highest energy injection per SN, followed by
thePop3 (fiducial)andPop3 Salpeterruns. A top-heavy
IMF allows more massive stars to form, which die quickly
and inject more energy (see §2.3). Forz <7, the fraction
of Pop III stars drops significantly, metal-rich stars begin to
form, and differences between stars diminish. Finally, we note
that the energy injection rate between theThesan-Zoomand
TZ + Th. Chemruns is very similar.
The impact of varying SN feedback is also evident in the
metal enrichment patterns. This is shown clearly in Fig. 16,
which plots the evolution of specific elemental yields (defined
as the ratio of metals in the gas phase to total stellar mass)
in the central galaxy (i.e. all the bound gas of the subhalo)
as a function of redshift. Significant metal build-up occurs
only forz≲11, even though the first stars form aroundz∼
14−16, because SN feedback from the first stars efficiently
expels gas and metals, delaying the enrichment of the central
galaxy. The simulations also predict that metal build-up is
slower in thePop3*runs, as stronger feedback in these runs
expels gas much farther, and it takes longer for the enriched
gas to accrete back into the galaxy (see also the associated
discussion for Fig. D1). Between10< z <6, the simulations
predict that thePop3*runs produce moreC, O, Ne, Mgand
Si. The simulations also predict that, forz >8, the central
galaxy lacksN, Fein thePop3runs, as these are produced
by AGB stars and SNIa, which occur only for Population II/I
populations in our model.
ThePop3 (fiducial)andPop3 M250runs show higher en-
richment of all metals except Iron compared with other runs.
Notably, thePop3 (fiducial)run achieves the highest levels
of Carbon and Nitrogen enrichment, while thePop3 M250run
shows the highest abundances of Oxygen, Neon, Magnesium,
Silicon, and Iron. This distinction arises because the IMF
inPop3 M250extends to250 M ⊙, enabling the formation of

4.2 Comparison with existing works
Theoretical work on Pop III star formation broadly falls into
two categories. One focuses on the detailed microphysics of
collapse within minihaloes during the era when Pop III star
formation dominates the cosmic star formation rate den-
sity(typicallyz≳15), examining internal halo conditions
and, in some cases, zoom-ins that attempt to resolve an emer-
gent IMF (Abel et al. 2002; Yoshida et al. 2003; Smith et al.
2015, 2024; Lenoble et al. 2024; Stacy et al. 2016). The second
emphasizes statistical properties derived from cosmological-
volume simulations. Our study belongs to the latter but uses
zoom-ins to achieve higher resolution, which, however, pre-
cludes a direct measurement of the cosmic-averaged star for-
mation rate density (SFRD).
Wise et al. (2012) studied the transition from Pop III (with
a top-heavy IMF, like ours) to Pop II star formation using
high resolution RHD simulations. They found that a single
PISN from Pop III stars can enrich the entire minihalo to a
value of about10 −3Z⊙, and transition it to Pop II regimes.
TheAEOSproject (Brauer et al. 2025b,a) aims to model the
early chemical enrichment and galaxy formation, which in-
cludes star-by-star treatment and detailed prescription for
Pop III stars (formation and SN feedback) in a cosmological
setting (untilz= 14.5). They found that the choice of IMF
for Pop III stars has a significant impact on the chemical
enrichment and ionization history of the ISM, but bulk prop-
erties like total star formation history remains robust. Addi-
tionally they found that compared to using subgrid models

servations in Section 3.1 and Appendix D, where early, in-
tense LW radiation from the first stars severely suppresses
cooling in the surrounding IGM and neighbouring substruc-

from UV-bright central galaxies, with only a fraction forming
within the virial radius; this spatial preference strongly cor-
roborates our findings that early Pop III formation is heavily
offset from the central galaxy.

fraction of dense primordial gas drops sharply during reion-

Several observational Pop III galaxy candidates have re-
cently been detected usingJWST(Maiolino et al. 2026).
They have low stellar masses and are spatially offset from
massive hosts. For example, Vanzella et al. (2023) detected a
potential source atz= 6.639withM ⋆ <10 4 M⊙. A couple of
galaxies atz= 10.6withM ⋆ ≃2–2.5×105 M⊙ about∼2kpc
from GN-z11 have recently been spectroscopically confirmed

2023; Maiolino et al. 2026). Similarly, a cluster atz≈8.2
withM ⋆ = (7.8±1.4)×10 5 M⊙ at a distance of∼1kpc from
its host (Wang et al. 2024) and a candidate Pop III galaxy at
z≈6.5withM ⋆ ∼10 5 M⊙ (Fujimoto et al. 2025) have also
been proposed as potential Pop III candidates. These systems
likely host mixtures of Pop III and enriched stars and align
with the expectation that late-time Pop III formation occurs
in low-mass haloes or satellites offset from central galaxies.
A better comparison of stellar masses and demographics will
require an explicit Pop III stellar evolution model simulated
over large cosmological volumes to chart the Pop III→PopII
transition and the epochs over which Pop III can dominate
within individual haloes.
4.3 Caveats
Oursimulationsincludemolecularhydrogenchemistry,radia-
tive transfer (including LW), and sufficient resolution to re-
solve the smallest star-forming haloes (∼106 M⊙). Modelling
the escape of LW photons self-consistently removes the need
for an imposed, spatially uniform LW background. Nonethe-
less, several relevant processes are absent or uncertain:
•Baryon–DM streaming velocities: We do not include the
coherent relative streaming that decays as(1 +z)and pref-
erentially suppresses collapse in low-mass haloes, shifting the
Pop III threshold to higher masses (Schauer et al. 2021). This
effect is most relevant forMhalo ≲10 7 M⊙, which contribute
substantially to late-time Pop III formation.
•Deuterium/HD cooling: Our network does not model the
formation and impact of HD. HD can form efficiently be-
low∼200K and cool the gas to the CMB floor (Nagakura &
Omukai 2005). This can be achieved via external LW fields
(Nishijima et al. 2024), higher-mass haloes, or slower collapse
(Greif et al. 2011). Cooler gas would reduce Jeans scales and
could trigger earlier star formation.
•LW background: We model local LW sources via radia-
tive transfer, and most Pop III formation occurs inMhalo >
106 M⊙ haloes that are less sensitive to a uniform LW field.
However, we do not impose an extragalactic LW background

•Metal mixing: Within resolution elements, we assume

quantitative forecasts.

framework. It has been shown that magnetic fields slow
down gravitational collapse, which can delay the onset of
Pop III star formation (Saad et al. 2022; Sharda et al. 2025).
Including magnetic fields in our model with non-ideal MHD
effects would be necessary to fully capture their impact on
Pop III formation and evolution.
•Stellar Rotation: Our MESA tracks and assumed su-
pernova yields are based on non-rotating stellar models.
Pop III stars are theorized to be fast rotators, which induces
internal mixing that can significantly extend main-sequence
lifetimes and alter the mass of the final helium core. Fur-
thermore, rotational flattening alters the surface temperature
(Teff) and gravity (logg), which would modify the emergent
spectra,andcouldalsopotentiallydrivemetalfreewinds(e.g.
Liu et al. 2021).
•X-ray Binaries: We currently treat all Pop III stars as
single stars. However, massive stars overwhelmingly form in
binary or multiple systems. Binary interactions dictate mass
transfer and the eventual formation of High-Mass X-ray Bi-
naries (HMXBs). As emphasized by recent studies (e.g., Sar-
torio et al. 2023), X-rays from these binaries provide a hard
radiation field that can deeply penetrate and ionize the dif-
fuse IGM, profoundly altering the global 21-cm signal. Incor-
porating a mass-dependent binary fraction to capture both
the modified UV/LW spectra and this hard X-ray component
remains a critical avenue for future work.
•Impact of resolution: It is unclear whether a subgrid
model that assumes each Pop III star particle represents a
single stellar population with a specified IMF remains valid
at resolutions lower than that used in this work. For instance,
if a giant molecular cloud (GMC) collapses to produce ap-
proximately10 5 to10 6 M⊙ of stars, it is ambiguous whether
all the stars will be metal-poor or whether the initial stars
formed from the GMC will promptly enrich the subsequent
stellar populations generated during gravitational collapse.
The critical GMC mass scale above which the entire cloud is
unlikely to form solely pristine Population III stars is not well
established. This issue has been partially addressed in this
work by adopting a subgrid SSP model coupled with high
resolution (∼1000 M ⊙). However, for lower-resolution simu-
lations, we plan to address this issue either by assuming that
the star particles are a mixture of Pop III and Pop II stars,
or by forming lower-mass star particles for Pop III stars. We
plan to investigate this in future work.

5.1.2 Detailed Pop III Stellar Spectra
We usedMESAto model the stellar evolutionary tracks of
120 metal-free stars in the range(0.1−1000 M ⊙)(Fig. 1)
andTLUSTYto compute detailed spectra using 1D, NLTE ra-

IMF-averaged spectra for three Initial Mass Functions: a log-
flat IMF (α=−1) withM min = 2 M ⊙ andM max = 150 M ⊙
(fiducial model), a log-flat IMF withM min = 2 M ⊙ and
Mmax = 250 M ⊙ (Fig.5), and a Salpeter IMF (α=−2.35)
withM min = 2 M⊙ andM max = 150 M⊙ were calculated and
integrated into the simulation framework.
5.1.3 Supernova Feedback Implementation
Elemental yields from type II, Pop III supernovae (§2.3)
were incorporated for the mass ranges10−100 M ⊙ and
140−260 M ⊙, modelling both Core-Collapse Supernovae
(CCSN) and Pair-Instability Supernovae (PISN). PISN in-
ject significantly more energy (up to∼1052erg) than typical
CCSN, and we track the production and evolution of 9 chem-
ical species: H, He, C, N, O, Ne, Mg, Si, and Fe.
We then use theAREPO-RTradiation-hydrodynamics code
to simulate a1.95×10 9 M⊙ halo atz= 3. Using the
zoom-in technique, we achieve a baryonic mass resolution
of1.14×10 3 M⊙. We conduct five simulation variants:
Thesan-Zoom(baseline),TZ + Th. Chem(enhanced thermo-
chemistry only),Pop3(full Pop III physics with a log-flat
IMF),Pop3 M250(full Pop III physics with a log-flat IMF but
with higherM max), andPop3_Salpeter(full Pop III physics
with a Salpeter IMF) (see Tab. 3 for the different choices of
IMF).
5.2 Major Findings
5.2.1 Pop III Star Formation and Evolution
The first stars appear atz∼16−14across different mod-
els, with the Pop III fraction dropping significantly after
z∼10as metals from SN explosions enrich the gas in galax-
ies (Fig. 10). While the formation of Pop III stars becomes
subdominant byz∼8, pockets of pristine gas allow them to
form down toz∼6(Fig. 11). The choice of IMF critically
affects the star formation history; top-heavy IMFs produce
stronger feedback that suppresses star formation more effec-
tively, leading to lower overall stellar masses than in models
with Salpeter-like IMFs or without Pop III feedback (Fig. 9).
5.2.2 Gas Phase Evolution and Thermochemistry
The updated thermochemistry network enables more effi-
cient cooling, yielding a higher fraction of dense gas (nH >
103cm−3) and lower overall temperatures (Fig. 12) than
the baseline model. The stronger Pop III radiative feedback
also introduces a distinctive photo-heated diffuse gas phase
(T∼10 4K,n H <10 −4 cm−3).
5.2.3 Pop III Feedback and Chemical Enrichment
MetalenrichmentfromPopIIIstarsproceedsdifferentlythan
from later generations of stars. ThePop3*runs predict the
formation of Carbon-Enhanced Metal-Poor (CEMP) stars,
which are absent in the non-Pop III runs (Fig. 13). Higher
energy injection from Pop III supernovae expels gas and met-
als further from the galaxy, leading to a slower build-up of
metals in the ISM (Fig. 16) at high redshifts(z >8). The spe-
cific elemental abundance patterns also differ, with top-heavy
IMFs producing higher abundances of Oxygen, Neon, Mag-
nesium, Silicon, and Iron due to contributions from PISN.
5.2.4 Predictions for JWST
Our model predicts strong, bursty Heii1640Å equivalent
widths (EW>20Å) for models with top-heavy IMFs (Pop3
fiducialandPop3 M250) down toz∼8, consistent with re-
cent earlyJWSTdetections of potential metal-free galaxies.
In contrast, models without Pop III stars or with a Salpeter
IMF produce negligible or weak Heiiemission. Although
the specific simulated galaxy is currently too faint for direct
JWSTdetection, our results support the use of Heiiemission
as a critical diagnostic of high-mass Pop III stellar popula-
tions in more massive systems.
5.3 Implications
We present a comprehensive framework for modelling the for-
mation and impact of Pop III stars in cosmological simula-
tions. This will enable more accurate predictions forJames
Webb Space Telescopeobservations of high-redshift galax-
ies. Our technical innovations include a self-consistent treat-
ment of Pop III radiation transport, including Lyman-Werner
photons; proper accounting for momentum injection during
the Sedov-Taylor phase of supernova feedback; and detailed
tracking of ionization states and molecular chemistry in pri-
mordial gas. This work advances the modelling of the tran-
sition from the first stars to subsequent generations, provid-
ing a more complete picture of how Pop III stars influenced
early galaxy formation and left imprints on the intergalactic
medium.

To validate the enhanced thermochemical network,
Fig. E1 compares the distribution ofH 2-rich gas

baselineThesan-ZoomandTZ + Th. Chem models. At
early epochs(z≈14), prior to significant metal and dust
enrichment, the baseline model forms negligible amounts
of molecular hydrogen. In contrast, the enhanced network
successfully catalysesH2 formation via the gas-phaseH+
2 ,H −
channels, yielding a significantlyH2-rich gas. At later epochs
(z≈7), after supernova feedback has enriched the ISM,
dust-drivenH 2 formation becomes dominant, and the two
models predictably converge. While thermodynamic states
at later times are subject to the stochastic divergence of
sub-grid feedback, thez≈14epoch provides verification of
the primordial chemistry network producing higherH2

tation of the explicitH+
2 ,H − primordial network i.e. theTZ +
Th. Chemcorrectly restrictsH 2 formation to the denser, col-
lapsingregionsofthehalo,asthegas-phasecatalyticchannels
are highly inefficient at IGM densities. Furthermore, the full
Pop3*runs vividly demonstrate the impact of Lyman-Werner
feedback; the intense LW radiation from the first stars (as
shown in Fig. D2) actively photodissociates molecular hydro-
gen in the halo outskirts atz >8, creating distinctH2-poor
voids that regulates star formation in neighbouring substruc-
tures.

ing observed molecular fractions requires an explicit sub-grid
clumping model to account for small-scale density enhance-
ments. Given our mass resolution of≈1.14×10 3 M⊙, our
models intrinsically miss these critical substructures. Imple-

APPENDIX F: CALCULA TION OF HEii1640 Å
EMISSION AND EQUIV ALENT WIDTHS
To model the Heii1640 Å emission from simulated galaxies,
we construct composite synthetic spectra for each subhalo by
combining the contributions of its constituent star particles.
We select all star particles within a 30 physical kpc radius of
the subhalo centre. The stellar populations are strictly parti-
tioned into Metal-Free (Population III) and Metal-Enriched
(Population II) stars, each evaluated with distinct baseline
Spectral Energy Distribution (SED) models. For Population
II stars, we use the BPASS v2.2.1 binary stellar population
models Eldridge et al. (2017), assuming a Chabrier initial
mass function (IMF) Chabrier (2003) with lower and upper
mass limits of0.1and100 M ⊙. For Population III stars, we
employ three custom metal-free spectra, namely a log-flat
IMF with a mass range of2−150 M⊙, a log-flat IMF with a
mass range of2−250 M ⊙, and a Salpeter IMF with a mass
range of2−150 M ⊙ (see Tab. 3 and Tab. 4).
From these theoretical SEDs, we extract grids of specific
continuum luminosities and ionizing photon rates as a func-
tion of stellar age and metallicity. For a given simulation
snapshot, we calculate the age (based on the particle for-
mation time) and metallicity of each star particle. We then
determine the specific particle properties by performing 2D
bilinear interpolation (overlog(Age)andlog(Z)) across the
BPASS grids for Pop II stars, and 1D interpolation (over
log(Age)) across the Pop III grids. The Heii1640 Å line
emission is driven by the flux of photons capable of remov-
ing the last electron from Heii(E >54.42eV). For each
star particle, we integrate the assigned SED to compute
