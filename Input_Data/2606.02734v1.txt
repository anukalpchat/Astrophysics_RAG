ABSTRACT
Context.Dust structures in protoplanetary discs have been widely observed and their creation remains an active field of research.
Several possible origins have already been explored, including magneto-hydrodynamics, shadows and planets–disc interactions.
Aims.The goal of this paper is to investigate whether a single migrating planet in a low-viscosity disc, including radiative processes,
is capable of generating observable dust structures. We aim to examine both the lifetime of such structures and potential asymmetries
within them.
Methods.We perform a set of high-resolution, two-dimensional hydrodynamic simulations of migrating planets using three different
equations of state: isothermal, constantβ-cooling and an adaptiveβmodel. Dust is included in all simulations and the resulting dust
density profiles are then post-processed to create radiative transfer images.
Results.For all equations of state considered, the planet undergoes one or several migration jumps, each producing dust rings and
gaps. The lifetime of these structures depends on the phase of slow migration preceding and occurring between jumps, but in all cases
they remain visible for at least 400 kyr. We find that cooling has a deciding effect on the migration behaviour and the number of jumps,
but no measurable influence on the lifetime of the dust structures. The structures exhibit relatively few asymmetries, and large-scale
vortices persist for an average of only 90 kyr.
Conclusions.Our models highlight the capacity of planets to open multiple gaps while migrating, and stress the importance of a
realistic cooling model. Care should be taken when interpreting and comparing such models directly to observations.
Key words.accretion, accretion discs – hydrodynamics – methods:numerical – planet-disc interactions – radiation-dynamics
1. Introduction
The DSHARP survey (Andrews et al. 2018) observed theλ=
1.25 mm continuum and 12CO J=2−1 line emission of 20
nearby protoplanetary discs. They confirmed without a doubt,
that the brightest and largest protoplanetary discs can contain
visible dust rings and gaps as well as asymmetries in their dust
profile. Atλ≈1.25 mm, the emission is dominated by thermal
radiation from millimeter-sized dust grains in the disc mid-plane,
which allows the identification of disc substructures. Similar ob-
servations have also been made by ODISEA (Orcajo et al. 2025),
exoALMA (Teague et al. 2025), MAPS (Öberg et al. 2021) and
the ALMA survey of the Lupus (Ansdell et al. 2018) and Taurus
(Long et al. 2018) star forming regions. A better understanding
of the formation of these dust structures would provide deeper
insight into the dynamics of the disc.
In this active field of research, several different theoretical for-
mation mechanisms for the development of substructure have
been identified. Such mechanisms range from the formation of
pressure bumps at the inner edge of the dead zone (Armitage
et al. 2001; Varnière & Tagger 2006; Flock et al. 2015; Flock
et al. 2017; Roberts et al. 2025), to spontaneous dusty rings due
to the streaming instability (Youdin & Goodman 2005; Ostertag
& Flock 2025), differential radial drift (Jiang & Ormel 2023),
magnetic effects (Béthune et al. 2016; Riols et al. 2020), or ther-
mal instabilities (Ueda et al. 2021; Sudarshan et al. 2026; Owen
2020), to warps (Smallwood et al. 2024a,b) and shadow-driven
structures (Su & Bai 2024; Qian & Wu 2024; Ziampras et al.
2025a; Zhang et al. 2025).
Another commonly proposed explanation is the presence of em-
bedded planets. They launch spiral waves, due to a resonance
between the planet’s gravitational potential and the epicyclic mo-
tion of the disc’s material (Goldreich & Tremaine 1979; Ogilvie
& Lubow 2002). For sufficiently high planetary masses or low
disc viscosities, the excited spiral waves steepen into shocks,
clearing material from the planet’s vicinity, thereby opening a
gap (Goodman & Rafikov 2001; Rafikov 2002). Zhang et al.
(2018); Miranda & Rafikov (2020a); Zhang & Zhu (2020) and
Ziampras et al. (2020) showed that a single non-migrating planet
has the ability to open multiple gaps and studied the effects of ra-
diative processes on gap formation.
McNally et al. (2019) (hereafter Mc19) and later Wafflard-
Fernandez & Baruteau (2020) (hereafter WB20), who consid-
ered higher viscosities, further investigated the migration of a
single planet in an isothermal disc and discovered migration
jumps, referred to as "intermittent migration". Those jumps were
able to create dust ring and gap structures which remained vis-
ible when plotting the continuum emission at (sub)millimetre

wavelengths. Meiners et al. (in prep.) (hereafter paper I) contin-

viscosities and disabling the gas self-gravity for all simulations.
They also observed intermittent migration jumps, further con-

In general, planetary migration is highly dependent on the level
of turbulence within the disc, typically modelled as an effec-
tive "viscosity" with anαparameter (Shakura & Sunyaev 1973).
Forα≳10 −3, planets migrate in the classical type-I and type-II
regimes (Paardekooper et al. 2011; Kley & Nelson 2012), based
on whether they can open a gap or not. Type-I migration is driven
by an interplay of Lindblad and corotation torques, resulting in
a rapid migration. For an Earth-sized planet this corresponds to
a migration timescale ofτ mig ∼10 5 yr (Kley & Nelson 2012).
If the planet opens a sufficiently deep gap, clearing its coro-
tation region, then only very few Lindblad modes are acting,
which produce a negligible net torque. Kley & Nelson (2012)
suggest, that the planet is now coupled to the viscous evolution
of the disc, since the deep gap prevents any material from enter-
ing the inner disc. On the other hand, Dürmann & Kley (2015)
found that the planet only prevents gas from crossing the gap
in its immediate vicinity, challenging the idea that the planet is
coupled to the viscous evolution. This results in migration rates
which differ significantly from the viscous timescale. Further-
more, Scardoni et al. (2022) showed that a planet in the type-II
regime might switch from inwards to outwards migration, now
decoupled from the viscous evolution but migrating with a sim-
ilar rate. Additionally, a planet might undergo phases of very
rapid migration, which are often labelled as type-III migration
(Masset & Papaloizou 2003; Rometsch et al. 2020).
In recent years, a number of studies have suggested that the
disc’s viscosity takes lower values, with a median aroundα∼
10−4 (Villenave et al. 2022; Dullemond et al. 2018; Rosotti 2023)
, but its value remains a topic of discussion. We further have to
acknowledge, that inferring values for the viscosity from obser-
vations comes with limitations. Ziampras et al. (2025b) (here-
after Z25) introduced new migration regimes in these nearly in-
viscid discs, which emerge for planets in a certain mass range.
Hence, migration is more diverse than thought of before.
As a continuation of Mc19 and paper I work, our goal of this pa-
per is to investigate a single planet’s ability to open multiple gaps
while including radiative processes in the disc. We tailor our se-
tups towards modelling the outer disc, ensuring a comparability
to the ALMA observations. We further analyse the lifetime and
appearance of the created rings and gaps and make a statement
about the structures observability.
In Sect. 2 we introduce our physical model and numerical frame-
work. We present our results in Sect. 3 and discuss them in
Sect. 4. We conclude everything in Sect. 5.

3. RESULTS
3.1. Migration regimes
One aspect of the simulation analysis is the identification of the
planet’s migration regimes and their link to the feedback and
thermal massesM f,M th (see Eqs. (12) & (13)). Mc19 and Z25
demonstrated, that in nearly inviscid discs, planets with masses
Mf ≲M p ≲M th migrate in the turbulent, vortex-assisted regime.
The planet opens a partial, asymmetric gap that is deeper behind
the planet than ahead of it, resulting in a pronounced surface den-
sity gradient at the outer gap edge (see also Fig. 5 in Z25). This
configuration is eventually unstable to the Rossby wave instabil-
ity (RWI, Lovelace et al. 1999), and is further subject to baro-
clinic forcing via radiative processes that can significantly mod-
ify the vortensity around the planet’s orbit (Ziampras et al. 2024,
2025b). The change in vortensity is directly linked to a change in
the surface density profile, which produces more RWI vortices.
These RWI vortices (driven by baroclinic forcing or not) at the
outer gap edge refill the gap and hence sustain the outer Lindblad
torques (Armitage & Kley 2019), driving the migration inwards.
With a width of∼6–23% of the half-width of the horseshoe re-
gion, these small vortices are often only visible when zooming
closely into the inverse vortensity (IV) profile. They can also be
detected by plotting the relative deviation from the azimuthally
averaged inverse vortensity: IV/IV−1. We will not distinguish
between two vortex-assisted sub-regimes, one without cooling
and one with cooling, but stress that if cooling is active, the baro-
clinic forcing significantly alters the regime. AsM th decreases
while the planet migrates inwards, the planet mass eventually
becomes super-thermal and transitions into the type-II regime,
ultimately stalling its migration.
We additionally observed the intermittent migration first intro-
duced by Mc19 (see also WB20; Z25; paper I). Intermittent mi-
gration can be described by brief episodes of type-III migration
(Masset & Papaloizou 2003), where the dynamical corotation on
the planet (Paardekooper 2014)

c) In this work, the planet’s migration is accelerated by the

generated, which in turn triggers a short episode of runaway
migration. All the material of the IV maximum ahead of the
planet is now able to enter the horseshoe region, performing
U-turns and accelerating the migration (see Eq. 15), breaking
into small vortices during this process.
d) This runaway phase ends, if all the material once in the IV
maximum ahead of the planet has entered the horseshoe re-
gion. After the jump, the planet’s semi-major axis fluctuates
for a short duration, caused by the small vortices created dur-
ing the jump.
The migration jumps observed in our models differ slightly from
the ones described in WB20, in particular as we find vortex for-
mation is triggered after a jump, more akin to the description of
paper I.

4. DISCUSSION
In this section we evaluate our results and their implications. We
further discuss their limitations of our method as well as possible
ways to improve.
4.1. Planet migration
We observe, that regardless of the chosen EOS, our planet mi-
grates in the vortex-assisted regime and may undergo one or
several migration jumps. At its initial radial location, the planet
has a mass ofM p ∼2M th in modelsiso,betaandadapt
andM p ∼1.3M th in modeladapt-low-mass. Since model
adaptdid not experience a migration jump, but modelsbeta
andadapt-low-massdid, we can infer that the transition be-
tween the intermittent and type-II migration regimes is depen-
dent on both planet mass and the local cooling timescale. This
implies that modelling observed substructures in the context of
migrating planets should be done carefully, including a prescrip-
tion for cooling.
We further carried out models with withβvalues of 0.01,3 and
7.5, the migration tracks for which are shown in Appendix A.
The simulation withβ=0.01 and 3 also undergo intermittent
migration when reaching the same radial location asisoand
beta. This is most likely related to these models having com-
parable gap structures, due to the similar angular momentum
flux profiles forβ=1 and 3 (Ziampras et al. 2026). The sim-
ulation withβ=7.5 does not experience any jumps and has a
significantly different gap structure. This suggests that planets
that open similar gap structures, regardless of cooling timescale,
should perform a jump at similar radii.
Nevertheless, we have to keep in mind thatβ fld only cools lo-
cally. The full inclusion of FLD might be relevant, for example
becauseβ fld overestimates the vortensity growth rate (Ziampras
et al. 2024). This could be studied further in future work.
4.2. Lifetime of radial structures
We have seen, that most of the created dust structures remain
visible in the intensity image att=500 kyr. Our isothermal sim-
ulation was able to confirm the results from WB20, Mc19 and
paper I. As paper I pointed out with their analysis for different
levels of viscosity, long-lasting structures were only present for
α≤10 −4.
WB20 proposed, that the lifetime of those structures depends on
the time spent in slow migration before or between the jumps. To
validate this statement in radiative discs, we additionally evalu-
ate the simulations mentioned in Appendix A. Comparing the
pressure maxima and minima and surface density profiles, we
can always find a clear correlation between the lifetimes of the
structures and the respective duration of slow migration (vortex-
assisted migration in this paper), in line with WB20. We find no
direct correlation between the cooling timescale and the dissipa-
tion of pressure maxima (although an indirect connection can be
identified, since the cooling timescale influences the planets’ mi-
gration and number of jumps). This is expected, as the lifetime of
these structures is tied to the diffusion (i.e., viscous) timescale,
which does not depend on the cooling timescale.
4.3. Vortex lifetimes
We observe large-scale vortices (∼3–5 au in radial width)
in modelsisoandbeta, but not in modeladaptor
adapt-low-mass. They are decoupled from the planet, not in-
fluencing its migration, and persist for an average of 120 kyr in
modeisoand 55 kyr in modelbeta. This is short compared
to the disc’s lifetime and simulation runtime (consistent with
Rometsch et al. 2021; Rafikov & Cimerman 2023). Thus, one
would expect observations of dust asymmetries caused by vor-
tices to be less abundantly observed than ring-like structures.
Additionally they are only created in the simulation with less
realistic heating and cooling implementations.
An additional comparison of the vortex lifetime between the dif-
ferent simulation (including Appendix A) reveals, that vortices
only survive marginally longer forβ=0.01 than forβ=1,
and no vortices are created ifβ >1. This partially agrees with
Rometsch et al. (2021), which stated the shortest vortex life-
times forβ=1 and longer lifetimes forβ <1 than forβ >1.
The planet mass used in both paper differs significantly, since
Rometsch et al. (2021) used a Jupiter mass planet. This may ex-
plain parts of the discrepancies.
We further observe, that the locations of the large-scale vortices
coincide with the created pressure maxima in modelisoand
beta. In modeladapt-low-mass, the pressure maxima still ap-
pear, but are considerably weaker without any pre-existing large-
scale vortices. Furthermore, the lifetime of the vortices does not
determine the visibility and dissipation of the dust structures.
Therefore contrary to the results of paper I, for discs with more
realistic cooling timescales, pressure maxima are nor strictly tied
to large-scale vortices.

5. CONCLUSION
This paper demonstrates, that one single migrating planet could
produce several visible dust rings and gaps for different EOS.
These structures remain visible for at least 300–400 kyr after
their creation and probably even several hundreds of kyr more, a
sizeable fraction of the median disc lifetime (Armitage 2020).
Therefore we could potentially explain the dynamical history
of observed discs with similar structures. We could additionally
confirm, that the lifetimes of the produced dust structures depend
on the time spent in slow migration before or between migration
jumps (as in WB20), even in radiative discs.
We also saw, that cooling substantially affects the migration be-
haviour of a planet. The now asymmetric baroclinic forcing is
an additional source of vortensity in the horseshoe region, which
may accelerate migration, if the planet has not yet opened a deep
gap, as seen in modelsbetaandadapt. How long a planet takes
to clear its gap at its final location strongly depends on whether it
has migrated far enough into the optically thick part of the disc,
where cooling can assist with the gap opening process (see Z25).
Some simulations showed large-scale vortices, always tied to IV
maxima. With an average vortex lifetime of only∼90 kyr in our
models, we should not observe protoplanetary discs with asym-
metries caused by them quite as often as without, if they’re re-
lated to gap-opening planets. They do also not appear in the sim-
ulations with the most realistic cooling prescription, likely due
to their faster dissipation due to cooling (e.g., Fung & Ono 2021;
Rometsch et al. 2021). This of course does not include asymme-
tries created by other mechanisms, such as material left at the
Lagrange point, observed in all simulations.
Our results indicate that migrating planets could be the origin of
some of the substructure observed in millimetre emission with
ALMA, and highlight the importance of including a realistic
cooling prescription when modelling this behaviour. As such,
care should be taken when both modelling and interpreting such
structures. Differentiating between observable signatures of mi-
grating or non-migrating planets, or even of other origin, is be-
yond the scope of this paper, but could be the focus of further
work.

Appendix A: Migration tracks of differentβ-cooling
simulations
Figure A.1 displays the semi-major axis evolution of simulations
with different constantβ-cooling timescales, compared to model
beta. We ran these additional models to further analyse the ra-
dius, where the planet undergoes its first migration jump as well
as the vortex and dust structure lifetimes as a function of the
cooling timescale for our analysis in Sects. 4.1 and 4.3. We ob-
serve that the planet in the simulations withβ=0.01 and 3 un-
dergoes one migration jump at the same radius as modelbeta.
Unsurprisingly, the simulation withβ=0.01 is very similar to
our isothermal model. On the other hand, the planet in the simu-
lation withβ=7.5 does not undergo a migration jump, since the
vortices did not accelerate the planet’s migration sufficiently for
it to catch up with its own gap.
Appendix B: Baroclinic forcing and low-vortensity
ribbons
In Sect. 3.2.2 we discussed the mechanism behind the formation
of a high-IV (i.e., low-vortensity) ribbon exterior to the planet’s
orbit, notably outside of the planet’s horseshoe region. As men-
tioned in the main text, this ribbon is the product of significant
baroclinic forcing as gas interacts with the shock front atϕ≈ϕ p
before shearing past the planet. This suggests that there should
be a clear dependence of this forcing term on the temperature
gradient across the shock∂T/∂ϕ, and, therefore, on the cooling
timescale.
To investigate this, we ran a set of five models using the
PLUTOcode (Mignone et al. 2007, 2012), mirroring the setup in
Sect. 3.2.2 but using a lower resolution ofN R ×N ϕ =256×768
and holding the planet fixed at 50 au for 10 orbits. Each of
these five models uses a different cooling prescription: locally
isothermal (β=0), cooling withβ∈[0.1,1,10], and adiabatic
(β→ ∞). We then compute the individual components of the
baroclinic forcing term att=10 planetary orbits as in Eq. (9):
