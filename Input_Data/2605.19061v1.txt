ABSTRACT
Emission from the pure rotational transitions of H2 traces warm molecular gas, providing insight into
its temperature distribution and local heating conditions. We have extended previous power-law H 2
temperature models to account for differential extinction by dust as well as non-equilibrium ortho-to-
para-H2 ratios (OPR). The turbulent environment of the M82 starburst offers a unique opportunity to
study H2 out of equilibrium conditions, using∼15 pc spatially resolved measurements from MIRI/MRS
on JWST. With extensive detections of H 2 S(1)–S(7), we use our model to assess spatial variations
in local heating conditions of molecular gas across a∼500 pc region of the M82 central starburst.
The average slope of the recovered H 2 power law temperature distribution is consistent with prior
studies, and the slope strongly anti-correlates with relative [FeII]/H 2 S(1)–S(2) strength, pointing to
the importance of shock-heating. Our models indicate that the OPR is, on average, about half of its
equilibrium value. This suppression is attributed to cloud mixing timescales which are short compared

1.INTRODUCTION
By mass and number, molecular hydrogen (H 2) dom-
inates the molecular composition of the universe. De-
spite lacking a dipole moment, its quadrupole transitions
can be observed in the near-infrared (NIR) and mid-
infrared (MIR) portions of the electromagnetic spec-
trum. The pure rotational transitions of H 2 detectable
with MIRI/MRS,J=3–1 up toJ=10–8 (S(1)–S(8)),
probe gas temperatures of∼100–1000 K and emit in the
MIR between∼5–30µm (D. Rigopoulou et al. 2002; H.
Roussel et al. 2007).
The James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) offers
unprecedented sensitivity for studying H 2 with the
Mid-infrared Instrument Medium Resolution Spectrom-
eter (MIRI/MRS). With spectral resolution R∼1300–
3700 (M. Wells et al. 2015), JWST/MIRI MRS pro-
vides higher resolution measurements than the Infrared
Spectrograph on boardSpitzer(R≲600; J. R. Houck
et al. 2004). Obtaining spatially resolved spectroscopy,
MIRI/MRS measures pure rotational transitions S(1)–
S(7) with a level of detail that previously was not possi-
ble. Measuring the H 2 emission spectrum is important
because of the information that H 2 excitation provides
about local heating conditions of the gas and the ways
that these conditions correlate with physical events such
as winds and outflows from star formation and super-
novae (T. M. Heckman et al. 1990).
Previous work on H 2 excitation in galaxies revealed
information about the temperature distribution of the
molecular gas based on the observed column densities
as measured bySpitzerand the Infrared Space Obser-
vatory. Typically this has been done by modeling the
temperature distribution with either 1- or 2-component
temperature fits (A. Fuente et al. 1999; D. A. Neufeld
et al. 2006; H. Roussel et al. 2007; M. W. Pound &
M. G. Wolfire 2023), or a power-law distribution (A.
Togi & J. D. T. Smith 2016; N. L. Zakamska 2010).
Most models assume an equilibrium ortho-to-para H 2
ratio. Beyond the temperature distribution of the gas,
the abundance ratio of ortho-to-para-H 2 encodes infor-
mation about its heating history. In this work, we ex-
pand power-law models to capture information on the
equilibrium conditions of the gas, which is of particular
relevance for gas which has been cooled or heated rapidly
on timescales shorter than the inter-species conversion
timescale.
A particularly interesting laboratory for studying H 2
excitation is the nearby edge-on starburst galaxy, M82
(d= 3.6 Mpc; W. L. Freedman et al. 1994). The cen-
tral∼1 kpc of M82 has undergone two bursts of star
formation occurring 8–15 Myr ago in the central 500 pc
and 4–6 Myr ago in a circumnuclear ring and the stel-
lar bar (N. M. F¨ orster Schreiber et al. 2003), which has
decreased in the last 5 Myr due to negative feedback pro-
cesses (P. Beir˜ ao et al. 2008). The successive bursts of
star formation produce FUV radiation and shock waves
which provide both thermal and non-thermal excitation
mechanisms for H 2 (T. M. Heckman et al. 1990; S. D.
Lord et al. 1996). A. D. Bolatto et al. (2024) and D. B.
Fisher et al. (2025) present imaging of the M82 nuclear
starburst and the base of its prominent galactic wind
with the JWST near-Infrared camera instrument, re-
vealing filaments and plumes of 3.3µm polycyclic aro-
matic hydrocarbon (PAH) emission outflowing from the
base of the galactic wind. In this paper, we leverage
resolved H2 spectroscopy across the central∼500 pc of
M82 to assess the conditions and thermal history of its
warm molecular gas content.
This work is organized as follows: In Section 2, we
discuss background information relevant to the explo-
ration of H 2 excitation; in Section 3 we detail the steps
taken to reduce the MIRI/MRS data for M82 and to
perform our spectral extractions; in Section 4 we intro-
duce our model of H2 excitation with subsequent results
and discussion in Section 5 and Section 6, respectively;
Section 7 addresses caveats of the model and Section 8
presents our conclusions.
2.H 2 EXCITATION AND ORTHO-TO-PARA
RATIO
Emission from the pure rotational transitions of H 2
probes the molecular gas reservoirs in galaxies. Under-
standing the mechanisms that excite the H 2 molecule
and the reactions that convert its nuclear spin between
its two states, ortho- and para-H 2, reveal information
about local heating conditions of the gas.
2.1.Excitation Mechanisms
H2 may be excited by both thermal and non-thermal
mechanisms. Thermally, dynamic activity such as

3
shocks, molecular outflows, and supernova remnants
can promote collisions between hydrogen molecules and
atoms, other molecules, and fast electrons (J. H. Black
& A. Dalgarno 1976; J. M. Shull & S. Beckwith 1982;
D. Hollenbach & C. F. McKee 1989; D. Rosenthal et al.
2000). Non-thermal excitation processes include the ab-
sorption of UV-photons by H 2 where fluorescence leads
to the population of excited rovibrational levels of the
ground electronic state (J. H. Black & A. Dalgarno 1976;
J. H. Black & E. F. van Dishoeck 1987). Stars in photo-
dissociation regions (PDRs) provide far-UV (FUV) pho-
tons to radiatively excite H 2 (R. I. Davies et al. 2003).

2.2.1.Thermal Disequilibrium of the OPR
The observed OPR provides a window into the recent
history of the molecular gas (D. A. Neufeld et al. 2006),
including H 2 formation on grain surfaces (with OPR
often assumed to be 3:1), H 2 photodissociation (with
preferential photodissociation of para-H2 when OPR>1)
and slow ortho-to-para conversion in the gas (and on
grain surfaces).
At high temperatures (≳300 K), spin conversion re-
actions proceed∼3 times faster for para-to-ortho-H 2
transitions relative to ortho-to-para-H 2 transitions (J.
Le Bourlot et al. 1999; T. Gonz´ alez-Lezana et al. 2021).
At lower temperatures this remains the case, with pro-
ton exchange reactions dominating species conversion
(T. Gonz´ alez-Lezana et al. 2021; F. Lique et al. 2014).
Below∼75 K the endothermic para-ortho conversion
reaction (e.g.J= 0→1) slows significantly (F. Lique
et al. 2014).
The cosmic ray ionization rate (CRIR) impacts the
timescale for spin conversion via collisions with protons
since a higher CRIR increases the number of free protons
in the gas enhancing conversion (D. R. Flower & G. D.
Watt 1984). For a CRIRζ=10 −17 s−1, D. R. Flower &
G. D. Watt (1984) find that the timescale for the OPR
and the kinetic and rotational temperatures of the gas to
come into thermal equilibrium is∼10 6 years for densi-
ties n(H 2)∼10 2–103 cm−3 and∼10 7 years for densities
n(H2)∼10 4–105 cm−3 where the free proton density is
lower. F. F. S. van der Tak et al. (2016) findsζ=(6-
80)×10−17 s−1 for M82 and thus the timescale for ortho-
H2 to para-H 2 conversion through collisions with pro-
tons is expected to be shorter. For example, L. Pagani
et al. (2013) finds that the timescale of ortho-to-para-
H2 conversion via collisions with protons to be of order
0.1 Myr forζ=10 −16 s−1 and gas density of 2×10 4 cm−3.
Inter-species conversion by collisions with H or H 2
has a substantial activation energy leading to a strong
temperature dependence of the reaction rate coefficient
(F. Lique et al. 2012; W. R. Schulz & D. J. Le Roy
1965). As a result, para-H 2 to ortho-H 2 reactions with
H atoms will only raise the observed OPR for the
fraction of the gas with kinetic energies exceeding the
activation barrier ofE/k∼3900 K. A significant in-
crease in the observed OPR therefore requires gas kinetic
temperatures of order 1000 K of higher (J. Le Bourlot
et al. 1999) on timescales exceeding the temperature-
dependent timescale for OPR thermalization. Above
300 K, where inter-species conversion reactions by col-
lisions with H become significant, the gas must be warm
for∼0.1 Myr for thermalization of the OPR, as deter-
mined from the reaction rate coefficients of F. Lique

4
et al. (2012), assuming gas densityn=10 4 cm−3. This
timescale decreases as the gas temperature increases.
In summary, at the typical temperature of warm H 2
gas, the processes which can bring ortho- and para-
H2 into equilibrium are relatively slow, ranging in
timescales from 0.1–10 Myr, depending on temperature,
CRIR, and other physical conditions. Non-equilibrium
OPRs thus provide information about the thermal his-
tory of the gas in situations where heating or cooling
events occur more rapidly than the inter-species con-
version timescale. In particular, during rapid heating
the OPR can remain substantially frozen, retaining in-
formation about the thermal history of the once cooler
gas.
3.MIRI/MRS DATA REDUCTION
JWST MIRI/MRS observations of the M82 nuclear
starburst were taken as part of the JWST Cycle 1 GO
Program 1701 (PI: A. Bolatto) between December 31
2023 and January 1 2024 (UT). The data were obtained
from the Mikulski Archive for Space Telescopes at the
DOI: 10.17909/ta3c-0426. The observations used a
4-point dither pattern with 30 groups per integration
and the FULL sub-array with the FASTR1 readout pat-
tern. Each exposure had a duration of 333.004s. The
data consist of seven pointings, with observations cover-
ing 4.9–27.9µm from all four integral-field units (chan-
nels 1-4). Observations were reduced using all three
stages of the JWST Calibration Pipeline with version
1.19.1 (H. Bushouse et al. 2025) and the reference file
jwst 1413.pmap. Changes that were made from the de-
fault pipeline to reduce the impact of artifacts are out-
lined below.
Detector and pipeline reduction artifacts were iden-
tified in the extracted spectra. On either side of the
[ArII] 6.99µm emission line profile pronounced red-
shifted and/or blueshifted absorption “valleys” were
present. This absorption is an artifact from the
straylightstep in the calwebb spec2 pipeline, which
aims to correct for contamination of extracted spectra by
the MIRI cross artifact feature in Channel 1 (A. G´ asp´ ar
et al. 2021; I. Argyriou et al. 2023). This correction
oversubtracted the continuum around the bright 6.99µm
[ArII] line, causing the false absorption features. To fix
this, the default reference file for thestraylightstep
was replaced withmrsxartcor hack.fits(D. Law, priv
comm.), which performs a milder continuum subtrac-
tion.
To reduce the impacts of fringing on extracted spec-
tra, theresidual fringingstep of the calwebb spec2
pipeline was enabled.

3.1.Spectral Extraction
The field of view of the mosaic of the MIRI/MRS data
is shown in red in Figure 1, overlaid on a 3.3µm poly-
cyclic aromatic hydrocarbon map from A. D. Bolatto
et al. (2024). The MIRI/MRS data trace the center of
the nuclear starburst in M82.
Spectral extractions are performed across the total
mosaicked area to assess spatial variations in heating
conditions. To reduce the impacts of varying spatial
resolution, the data are convolved to the resolution of
the S(1) transition at 17.04µm by approximating the
point-spread functions (PSF) as Gaussian profiles with
radii growing according to the FWHM of the MIRI MRS
PSF (Equation 1 of D. R. Law et al. 2023). Aper-
tures for spectral extractions are squares with 1 arcsec-
ond side lengths, corresponding to a projected distance
of∼17.5×17.5 pc 2, and are half-overlapping with one
another. Square-shaped apertures allow for the con-
struction of maps of spatially varying parameters (see

5
§5.4). These extractions produced 493 spectra. Spectra
are stitched together assuming multiplicative offsets be-
tween subbands, holding the flux in Channel 3 long as
truth. Offsets were generally<10%, but∼11% of spec-
tra required offsets exceeding 30% between channels.
The average spectrum across the full mosaicked area
of the M82 nuclear starburst is presented in Figure 2.
The spectrum shows prominent emission lines and dust
features, such as emission from polycyclic aromatic hy-
drocarbons and absorption from silicates.
Gaussian profiles are fit to the H2 S(1)–S(7) pure rota-
tional transitions along with a line to model the under-

for subsequent analysis. S(8) is not detected. Figure 3
shows H 2 S(1)–S(7) line fits for one of our extracted

3.2.Modeling Velocity Dispersion
A defining characteristic of M82 is its prominent galac-
tic wind (F. Walter et al. (2002); S. Veilleux et al. (2009);
A. D. Bolatto et al. (2024), S. Lopez et al. 2025, sub-
mitted), which is powered by the numerous massive star
clusters in the central burst (N. McCrady & J. R. Gra-
ham 2007; Y. D. Mayya et al. 2008; R. C. Levy et al.
2024). Such outflows and winds can cause the molecu-
lar gas to move at a range of speeds due to turbulence
which manifests as broadening in the emission line pro-
files. Additionally, M82 is an edge-on galaxy, so the
telescope beam may cover several components of gas
that could be moving at different radial velocities (N.
Krieger et al. 2021). Accounting for line broadening is
thus important for accurate measurement of emission
line fluxes.
To account for broadening in the line profiles, we al-
low the fitted width of the Gaussian profile to vary by
up to twice the width of an unresolved spectral line (i.e.,
the instrumental width, see A. Labiano et al. 2021),
amounting to∼70–100 km s −1. By modeling the in-
strumental profile of the pure rotational transitions of
H2 with a Gaussian profile and allowing its width to
increase to simulate velocity broadening, we determine
that velocity dispersions can be reliably measured down
to∼50 km s −1 for transitions S(3)–S(6). For transitions
S(1), S(2), and S(7), the threshold for reliable measure-
ment is between 70-100 km s−1. These values are deter-
mined such that the fitted, broadened velocity disper-
sion is within 5% of the input broadening.
Fewer than 9% of sight-lines demonstrate detectable
velocity broadening in at least one pure rotational H 2
transition and thus 91% of sight-lines have linewidths
consistent with the instrumental linewidth. We only de-
tect reliable velocity broadening in transitions S(3)–S(6)
whereσ v >50 km s −1.∼1% of sight-lines contain H 2
emission lines with two velocity components, with peaks
separated by>90 km s −1. To model both components,
we use two Gaussian profiles. A sample two-velocity
component line fit is shown in Figure 4, where the ve-
locity components are separated by 90.6 km s −1. Both
components have widths consistent with the instrumen-
tal width. Regions with increased velocity dispersions
in the S(3) transition are marked by the red and salmon
contours in the top panel of Figure 11, which correlate
spatially with the velocity dispersions measured in tran-
sitions S(4)–S(6).

4.2.1.Temperature-dependentξ
As discussed in§2.2.1, initially non-equilibrium OPRs
can equilibrate rapidly for gas temperatures of order
1000 K or higher (J. Le Bourlot et al. 1999; F. Lique

in addition to collisional excitation may provide extra
heating to the gas and higher gas temperatures gener-
ally have higher spin conversion reaction rates. This im-
plies that warmer molecular gas along the line of sight,
which dominates the higherJtransitions of H 2, may
equilibrate more rapidly than the cooler gas emitting
the bulk of the lowerJline luminosity. This tempera-
ture dependence of the equilibration timescale can ex-

SLEDs, as discussed in D. A. Neufeld et al. (2006).
Previous work by D. A. Neufeld et al. (2009) models
the OPR as a function of both temperature and time in
proto-stellar outflows. They found the OPR of warmer
gas to be closer to its equilibrium value compared to
cooler gas and quantified the amount of time that the
gas has been warm. Given the complexity of heating
environment and history in galactic regions spanning
≳10 pc, we take a different approach, introducing a tem-
perature dependence of the disequilibrium parameterξ.

5.3.Temperature Distribution
The power-law index of the temperature distribution
provides information about the fractional amount of
warm gas present which can help quantify the impact
of heating mechanisms such as shocks or UV pumping.
Lower power-law indices imply greater contributions of
warmer gas, which are expected in shock-heated regions.
D. A. Neufeld & Y. Yuan (2008) find that for supernova
remnant IC 443, modeling H 2 column densities with
a continuous temperature distribution produces power-
law indices ranging from 3–6, and specifically predict
n∼3.8 for C-type bow shocks.
TS2016 determines the power-law index of the tem-
perature distribution of molecular gas for a sample of
galaxies with varying environments (dwarf galaxies, ra-
dio galaxies, ULIRGS, etc.), which ranges from 3.79 to
6.39. E. A. C. Mills et al. (2017) finds power-law indices,
n= 3.22, 2.83 for two regions within the Galactic center
circumnuclear disk, indicating large fractions of hot gas.
We find that the temperature distribution of molecu-
lar gas as traced by pure rotational H 2 emission in the
nuclear starburst of M82 falls within the range of the
TS2016 sample. Figure 10 compares the values of the
power-law index of the temperature distribution in this
work to those from TS2016 with the average values pre-
sented by the vertical dashed lines. Our work presents
a narrower distribution which is expected since we are
looking within a∼500 pc-sized region of one galaxy, as
opposed to the broader TS2016 sample. Our average

5.4.2.Disequilibrium Heating
Spatial variations are also found across the M82 nu-
clear starburst for the temperature-dependent disequi-
librium parameter,ξ(Equation 7), to quantify the de-
gree to which the OPR differs from OPReq. The bottom
panel of Figure 11 presents a map ofξfor the 363 spec-
tra where errors on the fitted value ofξare less than
40%, with contours showing surface brightness of the
shock tracer, [FeII], at 5.34µm. The surface brightness
of [FeII] was determined following the fitting procedure
for H2 pure rotational transitions (see§3.1), requiring a
S/N>3 for a line to be detected. In general,ξdoes
not correlate with [FeII] emission.ξvaries from∼0.33–
0.65 with a median value of 0.49 and a standard devi-
ation equal to∼0.06. Values ofξ <1 are indicative of
disequilibrium heating (§4.2) and because we only find
ξ <1, the observed OPR isnotin equilibrium with the
molecular gas temperature in the M82 nuclear starburst.
The relatively low scatter in the fitted value ofξimplies
that on average, the observed OPR is roughly half of
its equilibrium value at a given temperature. Possible
explanations for the widespread suppression of the OPR
are explored in§6.1.
6.DISCUSSION
Armed with the disequilibrium model, we estimate
the temperature distribution of warm molecular gas as
traced by the pure rotational transitions of H 2, the im-
pact of extinction, and degree of disequilibrium heat-
ing in the M82 nuclear starburst. The disequilibrium
parameterξ(§4.2, Eq. 8) quantifies the differences be-
tween the OPR and its equilibrium value given the rota-
tional temperature of the gas, which may be out of equi-
librium with each other in situations where molecular
gas that has cooled is suddenly heated over timescales
shorter than the (temperature-dependent) inter-species
conversion timescale (ξ <1).

in the M82 nuclear starburst with the temperature-
dependentξmodel, Figure 12 presents the relationship

A weak anti-correlation between the excitation of the
gas as traced by the power-law index of the tempera-
ture distribution,n, and the disequilibrium parameter
ξemerges with a Spearman rank correlation coefficient
of−0.26 andp-value of 4.1×10 −6. Because the inter-
species conversion timescale due to reactive collisions

with atomic H rapidly decreases with increasing temper-
ature, it is reasonable to expect that regions with more
warm gas (lowern) may be equilibrating more quickly
than regions with a larger fraction of cooler gas (higher
n). However, the weakness of the anti-correlation hints
at the fact that both the current temperature of the gas
and its thermal history together drive the OPR equili-
bration. A weak positive correlation is observed between
nandτ Si with a Spearman rank correlation coefficient
of 0.39 and ap-value of 2.7×10 −6, possibly indicating
that more dust-embedded clouds have a larger fraction
of cooler H 2 gas, but the complex line of sight geometry
complicates this interpretation.
Within the central kpc of M82, there have been two
bursts of star formation roughly 8–15 Myr and 4–6 Myr
ago with peak star formation rates of 160 M ⊙ yr−1 and
40 M ⊙ yr−1 (N. M. F¨ orster Schreiber et al. 2003). The
lowermeasured OPR relative to the equilibrium value
for the estimated rotational temperature of the gas in
M82 implies that the ortho-to-para abundance ratio is
reflecting a cooler prior gas temperature. As the most
massive stars that formed end their lives, thermal and
kinetic energy is injected into the gas. Thus, if molecu-
lar gas cooled after the starburst to an OPR<3, reheat-
ing from ongoing feedback processes may raise the rota-
tional temperature of the gas on timescales faster than
the time required to raise the OPR. Variations in the de-
gree of disequilibrium heating experienced by different
parcels of gas may depend on their history of density and
temperature. The analysis of disequilibrium heating in
the M82 center as measured byξprovides a time-average
tracer of the thermal history of the varying heating rates,
withξpotentially suppressed after the sudden onset of
star formation or other energetic processes.
6.2.Turbulent Mixing
Another physical effect with the potential to globally
suppress the OPR in a starburst core is turbulence. If
the timescale for turbulent mixing becomes shorter than
the inter-species conversion timescale at relevant densi-
ties and temperatures, gas dredged up from cooler re-
gions within clouds could retain lower OPR values, “re-
membering” its cooler past environment.
The spin conversion timescale is a decreasing func-
tion of the temperature of the gas and concentration
of H atoms and protons (see§2.2.1). For gas kinetic
temperatures≳300 K, the rate coefficients of F. Lique
et al. (2012) indicate that spin conversion reactions
through collisions with H atoms proceed over timescales
≲0.1 Myr, decreasing to the order of hundreds of years
for gas as hot as∼500 K. Below 300 K where the bulk of
the warm H 2 mass is found, spin conversion timescales

should be on the order of a few Myr (P. Hennebelle &
E. Falgarone 2012). In contrast, in molecular gas dom-

find that the timescale for H 2 mixing is≲0.3 Myr.
In the shocked, turbulent environment of the M82 nu-
clear starburst, it is therefore plausible that the spin
conversion timescale in warm gas may exceed the turbu-
lent mixing timescale. Within a molecular cloud near a
source of UV radiation, turbulent mixing may bring cool
H2 with a low OPR (<3) towards the warmer surface of
the cloud, where it heats up rotationally on timescales
≲100 yr, based on transition probabilities from J. H.
Black & A. Dalgarno (1976). Because the timescale for
heating an H 2 molecule is shorter than the spin con-
version reaction timescale in this scenario, the molecule
will heat up rotationally before the OPR can thermal-
ize with the warmer state of the gas. In less turbulent
environments, longer turbulent mixing timescales would
imply that cool H 2 is brought to the warm, irradiated
surface more slowly, giving more time for spin conver-

15
sion reactions to drive the OPR closer to equilibrium in
the warmer cloud envelopes.
In summary, the OPR suppression in M82 nuclear
starburst may result from the fact that the spin con-
version timescale exceeds both the timescale for heating
H2 rotationally and the fast turbulent mixing timescales,
for gas with kinetic temperature≲300 K. Thus, in the
highly turbulent environment of the M82 nuclear star-
burst, the damping zig-zag shape observed in the SLEDs
(§5.1) indicate that only the warmest gas remains warm
long enough to thermalize.
Theoretical work exploring the turbulent mixing and
OPR thermalization timescales within realistic clouds
would be very valuable for validating this scenario. Ob-
servationally, the connection between increased turbu-
lence and OPR suppression could be further supported
through measurement of increased CO linewidths and
therefore lower values ofα CO in regions with strong dis-
equilibrium signatures.
6.3.Correlations with Ionized and Neutral Gas
With access to ionic emission lines within the
MIRI/MRS coverage, comparisons between the ionized
and molecular phases of the gas can provide a more
complete picture of the heating conditions within the
region. The following emission lines are modeled with
Gaussian profiles to measure their surface brightness:
[ArII], [SIV], [NeII], [NeIII], and [FeII]. The surface
brightnesses are compared with cool and warm molec-
ular gas as traced by the summations of H 2 S(1)–S(2)
and S(4)–S(7), respectively.
Relative to the cooler, lowerJrotational transitions
of H 2 (S(1) and S(2)), several tracers of ionized gas
([ArII], [SIV], [NeII], [NeIII]) show mild to mod-
erate anti-correlations with the power-law index of the
temperature distribution,n. The [FeII] emission line at
5.34µm anti-correlates strongly withn. The left panel
of Figure 13 presents the relationship between [FeII]/H2
S(1)–S(2) andn, which has a Spearman Rank coefficient
equal to−0.72 with ap-value of 7.2×10 −47. Iron has a
high condensation temperature above 1000 K and thus
is typically found in the solid phase (K. R. Sembach &
B. D. Savage 1996). The strong anti-correlation ofn
with [FeII] strength relative to lower-JH 2 emission is
potentially stronger than that of other bright ionized
lines due to enhancement of gas-phase iron abundance
via shock heating in neutral gas, which can liberate it
from its solid form (B. O’Halloran et al. 2008). Spa-
tially, the [FeII] emission is correlated with but some-
what more extended than [NeII]+[NeIII], as seen in the
contours of Figure 11.
The excitation of warmer molecular gas as traced by
H2 S(4)–S(7) may have contributions from UV pump-
ing (J. H. Black & A. Dalgarno 1976), increasing the
strength of emission at low power-law index,n, com-
pared to the collisionally excited lowerJtransitions
(S(1)–S(2)). Since the emission of both [FeII] and
H2 S(4)–S(7) can be enhanced at low power-law in-
dexn, these two effects approximately cancel out, caus-
ing [FeII]/H 2 S(4)–S(7) to be more weakly correlated
withn. This relationship is shown in the right panel of
Figure 13, with a Spearman Rank coefficient of−0.32
and ap-value of 4.9×10 −8. Because the collisionally-
dominated cooler phases of molecular gas are not en-
hanced at lown, [FeII] emission is stronger relative to
H2 S(1)–S(2) than H2 S(4)–S(7), providing an indication
of recent shock heating.
Recent shock heating may also play a role in the sup-
pression of the observed OPR at lowJ. The OPR ther-
malization timescale by H 2 –H collisions proceeds at a
rate proportional to the atomic H abundance. Shocks
that partially dissociate H2 will therefore boost the ther-
malization rate. If the shocks are fast enough, high
temperatures may suffice to raise the OPR to∼3 via
collisions with H atoms and protons. Slower shocks in
cooler molecular gas (S(1)–S(2)) may increase the rota-
tional temperature without dissociating H 2 contribut-
ing to the OPR falling below its equilibrium value, as
observed.
7.MODEL CAVEATS
7.1.Model Over-estimations of S(6)
The disequilibrium model (§4) often mildly overesti-
mates the column density of the S(6) transition. In
Figure 8, it can be seen that the median residuals of
model fits in the M82 center indicate the column den-
sityN obs,H2(J=6) is overestimated by∼15%. We discuss
several potential reasons for the model overestimation
at S(6) below.
7.1.1.6.02µm H 2O Water Ice Absorption

ferent widths and carriers (A. C. A. Boogert et al. 2015).

FWHM of the 6.2µm PAH feature will be narrower than
its empirical value. While we do detect the 6.2µm PAH

T. S. Y. Lai et al. (2020) investigates extinction from
silicates and water in a large sample of nearby star-
forming galaxies. They report median values for the
optical depths of silicates and the 3.05µm H 2O ice fea-

tween silicates and 6.02µm H 2O ice based on the band
strengths provided by J. Gao et al. (2013). This rela-
tion allows us to estimate the possible extinction by ice
absorption near the S(6) transition. From this relation-
ship, we find possible values ofτice range from 6.3×10 −7
to 0.19, with a median value of 0.07. Assuming screen
geometry to correct for attenuation, we obtain a range
of possible S(6) “boost factors” of 1.0–1.21, with a me-
dian value of 1.07. Since the actual strength of 6µm ice
absorption is unknown, we add an additional source of
uncertainty to the column density of S(6) appropriate for
the median boost factor, resulting in an additional 7%
uncertainty, added in quadrature toσ S(6). We then fit
the observed column densities with the correctedσ S(6)
using our temperature-dependentξmodel. We chose to
boostσ S(6) rather than correct the column density at
S(6) because the MIRI/MRS data does not have cover-
age of the 3.05µm H2O ice feature, so its strength cannot
be determined definitively. Thus, the S(6) transition is
weighted less when performing the fit to the disequilib-
rium model, indirectly reducing the median residuals at
S(1) and S(4) from<15% to<7%.
7.1.2.Self-Shielding
Steady-state photodissociation regions (PDRs) may
also experience non-equilibrium OPRs with an OPR>3,
where differences in self-shielding enhance the column
density of the more dominant species (B. T. Draine &
F. Bertoldi 1996; D. A. Neufeld et al. 1998; A. Sternberg
& D. A. Neufeld 1999; J. Le Bourlot et al. 1999; L. K.
Hunt et al. 2025). Due to its higher abundance (when
OPR>1), ortho-H 2 preferentially self-shields from pho-
todestruction in low optical depth regions. This can re-
sult in the inferred column density of ortho-H 2 appear-
ing above the para-H2 levels on a SLED (implyingξ >1)
as discussed in L. K. Hunt et al. (2025). S(6) is a para-
H2 transition, so its observed column density may be re-
duced in translucent regions with elevated FUV fluxes.
L. K. Hunt et al. (2025) finds an OPR>OPR eq in three
photodissociation fronts in the nearby, low-metallicity
dwarf galaxy, I Zw 18, and attributes it to low H2 column
densities and the preferential self-shielding of ortho-H 2.
However, we do not detect any cases of OPR>OPR eq
(ξ >1, equivalently) in the starburst region of M82, and
thus we do not find supporting evidence for preferential
self-shielding of ortho-H 2.
7.1.3.Thermalization Threshold Temperature
In our temperature dependent disequilibrium model,
we strictly enforce equilibration between the OPR and
OPReq at gas temperatures>1000 K (§4.2.1). This is an
approximation based on the fact that the rate of para-
to-ortho-H2 conversion has a strong temperature depen-

17
dence which rises steeply at∼1000 K (D. A. Neufeld
et al. 2006). Because the column density at S(6) is
over-estimated, it may hint that the OPR is thermal-
izing at temperatures below 1000 K. This is supported
by the fact that the models assuming OPR = OPR eq in
Figure 8 (TS2016 and TS2016+extinction) are the only
models where the inner three quintiles of SLED fits ac-
curately reproduce the observed column density at S(6).
To test its impact, we lower the equilibration thresh-
old temperature toT eq = 500 K in the temperature-
dependentξmodel (§4.2.1, Equation 8). Median resid-
uals at S(6) decrease from∼28% withT eq = 1000 K
to∼19%. However, with four degrees of freedom, the
overall fits are worse, with average reduced chi-square
value increasing by∼13% forT eq = 500 K relative to
Teq = 1000 K. This may indicate that OPR thermal-
ization is proceeding over a range of gas temperatures
500 K< T eq <1000 K.
WithT eq set to 500 K, the median values ofnand
τSi increase slightly by∼1.5% and 5.4% to 4.66±0.25
and 1.36±0.35, respectively. The median value ofξ
decreases by∼48% to 0.33±0.10. This reduction inξ
withT eq = 500 K compared toT eq = 1000 K (median
ξ= 0.49±0.06) reinforces the measured suppression in
the OPR in the M82 nuclear starburst, but highlights
the sensitivity ofξto the choice ofT eq.
Compared to the pure power-law plus extinction and
single-ξmodels (for models withT eq = 100 K andT eq =
2000 K, respectively,§5.1), the average chi-square value
increases by a factor of four and two, relative toT eq =
1000 K. Thus,T eq = 1000 K provides the best overall
fit for OPR thermalization in the M82 nuclear starburst
for the four values tested forT eq. Future models may
seek to pin down more precisely the gas temperature
dependence of OPR thermalization.
8.CONCLUSIONS
In this work, we have built upon existing frameworks
for modeling H 2 excitation in galaxies using power-law
temperature distributions (e.g., TS2016) to incorporate
dust extinction and the effects of non-uniform disequilib-
rium heating on the observed ortho-to-para ratios. De-
viations from the equilibrium-value OPR reveal valuable
information about the thermal history of the molecular
gas, providing a time-average tracer of varying heating
rates by identifying instances of rapid heating within
galaxies. Our model accurately reproduces the distribu-
tion of observed column densities in the central regions
of the nearby, edge-on, starburst galaxy, M82. The ad-
ditional physical processes in our model permit its use in
a wide range of environments, including recently heated
and obscured gas, which previous models could not ac-
count for. With the rich MIR spectroscopy from JWST,
a physically-motivated model of H2 excitation allows for
a deeper understanding of the time-varying interactions
occurring in the molecular gas.
We find the following results:
•We detect high signal-to-noise emission of pure
rotational transitions of H 2 from S(1) to S(7) in
413 regions in the MIRI/MRS coverage of the
M82 nuclear starburst. The observed trends in
column density show varying power-law slopes of
their temperature distribution as well as depar-
tures from equilibrium-value OPRs indicated by a
damping zig-zag shape in excitation diagrams.
•Median residuals between our temperature-
dependent disequilibrium model and observations
are<18% for all transitions S(1)–S(5) and S(7),
reducing the median residuals relative to the pure
power-law model by a factor of∼5. Moderate re-
maining residuals at S(6) could result from com-
plications such as the unknown presence of the
6.02µm water ice absorption feature, thermaliza-
tion of the OPR at lower temperatures, and/or
optically thin shielding effects at highJ.
•Attenuation of H 2 rotational emission near 9.7µm
(S(3)) due to silicate absorption is detected in all
regions, with a median optical depthτ Si∼1.3±0.3.
This value is inferred by the attenuation-driven de-
viations seen in the S(3) transition relative to the
other detected transitions, producing attenuation
estimates that closely track values inferred from
the 5.5–14.5µm continuum with screen geometry.
•We find the mass of warm molecular gas (T >
100 K) to be (2.0±0.1)×10 7 M⊙ across the
JWST/MIRI MRS coverage of a∼500 pc region
of the M82 nuclear starburst, representing∼7.6%
of the total molecular mass in the disk, as inferred
from CO(1-0) observations.
•The median value for the power-law index of
the temperature distribution,n, is found to be
4.58±0.20, consistent with but slightly warmer
(lowernby 5%) than previous studies of galaxies
within varying galaxy environments (TS2016).
•The OPR is found to be nearly uniformly about
half of its equilibrium value across the starburst
core. This can be attributed to the turbulent en-
vironment of the M82 nuclear starburst where cool
gas is heated and mixed on timescales shorter than
the spin conversion timescale.

18
•Relative to low-JH 2 line luminosity from S(1) and
S(2), the strength of [FeII] emission is enhanced in
gas where the temperature distribution is biased
towards warmer gas (lowern). Only a weak en-
hancement is seen in ionized lines such as [NeII]
and [NeIII], which may suggest that shock heating
is both liberating [FeII] from its solid phase while
also increasing the temperature of the gas.
Future models that seek to understand the thermal
history of gas within galaxies may find OPR disequi-
librium provides a useful physical diagnostic for quan-
tifying the effects of time varying heating conditions.
Observationally, the connection between increased tur-
bulence and OPR suppression may serve as a predictor
for increased CO linewidths and lowα CO.
