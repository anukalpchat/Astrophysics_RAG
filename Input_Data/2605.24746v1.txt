ABSTRACT
We present results from very-long-baseline-interferometry (VLBI) observations of the10
nucleus in the lobe-dominated quasar 3C207. These observations were completed at 8.411
or 10.7 GHz (X-band) from 1981 to 2010, spanning 29 years. The nucleus of 3C207 is12
the strongest and most variable in the 3CR complete sample of LDQs, which is under13
study to test relativistic jet models over a wide range of jet orientation angles. Images14
have typical resolutions of∼0.5-1.0 milliarcseconds (mas) and sensitivities of∼0.1-0.215
mJy beam −1. The VLBI core region has flux density outbursts at mean intervals of16
∼7 yr; two of these are multiple outbursts from a stationary “true” core that feeds a17
“swinging component”∼0.5 mas to the east. The position angle (PA) of the swinging18
component shows a long-term increase of∼40 ◦, with a short-term reversal of∼10 ◦.19

1.INTRODUCTION31
This work is part of a larger, long-term very-long-baseline-inteferometry (VLBI) survey to test32
models of relativistic jets in active galactic nuclei (AGN) and AGN unification (Hough & Readhead33
1989). In contrast to the various highly productive surveys of bright, compact AGN (e.g., Readhead34
et al. 1978; Lister et al. 2009), we have selected a complete sample of 25 lobe-dominated quasars35
(LDQs) from the revised 3CR catalog (Laing et al. 1983). Unlike the bright, compact AGN, the36
LDQs have been selected by their extended, steep-spectrum radio emission on kpc scales, which37
minimizes orientation bias due to highly-beamed structures and permits tests of jet models over a38
wide range of orientations. To define the complete sample, Hough & Readhead (1989) considered39
all quasars in the 3CR catalog, used a strict flux density cutoff for the extended emission (10 Jy40
at 178 MHz), and examined source structure and spectra to select all the “double-lobed quasars”41
(DLQ) with two steep-spectrum lobes straddling a nucleus coincident with the optical identification.42
We also used the definition of the quantityRto be the ratio of arcsecond nucleus flux density to43
extended flux density at 5 GHz, following Orr & Browne (1982). Hough et al. (2002) then adopted44
the term “lobe-dominated quasar” and defined an LDQ to haveR <1. All 25 DLQs were found to45

We report here on a nearly three-decade X-band VLBI study of the nucleus in the LDQ 3C20757
(1Jy 0838+133), with redshiftz= 0.6805 (DESI Collaboration et al. 2026), projected linear size of58
98 kpc and J2000 coordinates of the nucleus: 08 h40m47.587s, 13◦12′23.54′′ (Hough et al. 2002). Tang59

complete sample.64
Observations were made at 10.7 GHz from 1981.76 to 1991.15, and at 8.4 GHz from 1991.16 to65
2010.81. While our previous results over a shorter time range showed that jet speeds are faster66
further from the core in 3C207 (Hough 2008), the picture over 29 years is more complex, with a67
swinging component at nearly constant radius next to the core, variable direction of component68
ejection, component acceleration and possible deceleration, and recollimation on the∼100 pc scale.69
For context, the kiloparsec-scale VLA structure of 3C207 shows a prominent one-sided jet to the70
east (Kharb et al. 2010; Fernini 2014), roughly aligned with the parsec-scale jet studied herein. The71
closest jet knot, at a distanced= 0.78 ′′ from the arcsecond nucleus, has position angle PA = 94 ◦;72

3.RESULTS120
3.1.Flux Density Variability in the Milliarcsecond Core Region121
As the best means of tracking variability, we plot the X-band VLBI flux densities of the milliarc-122
second core region as a function of time as determined by modelfitting (Figure 2). Super-resolved123
imaging and modelfitting both show that the core region consists of a close double: the “true” core C124
(the base of the jet) to the west and a “swinging” component S to the east that appears to maintain125
a nearly constant radial distance from the core while undergoing significant PA changes; these are126
discussed further in Section 3.2. The only exception is epoch 2002.03, for which a single elliptical127
Gaussian component was fitted to the core region. Five “outbursts” are evident, and while the time128
sampling is not sufficient to define them all precisely, we can characterize the outbursts in terms129
of their typical spacing, rise and decay times, and rise and decay amplitudes. We identify these130
outbursts by their highest observed flux densities at 1983.47, 1989.27, 1995.67, 2003.45, and the last131
epoch 2010.81, where the flux density was still rising. This is suggestive of quasi-periodic events132

with a mean spacing of 6.9±0.9 yr. The rise/decay times and amplitudes are simply defined by133
adjacent local minima and maxima. With only one observation prior to the 1983.47 maximum, we134
take the minimum rise time for this event to be 1.7 yr. Including the large pre-outburst gaps, the135
maximum rise times for the 1989.27 and 1995.67 events are 4.7 and 4.5 yr, respectively. The rise136
time for the 2003.45 event is 3.8 yr, and the minimum rise time for the 2010.81 event is 2.0 yr. These137
data are consistent with a typical rise time of∼3-4 yr. By similar methods, the typical decay time138
is also∼3-4 yr. The rise and decay amplitudes range over a factor of∼10, at∼100 mJy for 1983.47,139
∼400 mJy for 1989.27,∼200 mJy for 1995.67, and∼1000 mJy for 2003.45; the rise amplitude is140
at least∼300 mJy for 2010.81. This indicates that the outbursts may alternate regularly between141
weaker and stronger amplitudes.142

we first briefly discuss the jet structure from pre-VLBA imaging. We then describe in detail the jet186
features as seen in VLBA images. These maps were made in several ways: at conventional resolution,187
with “super-resolution” to probe the core region, with light tapering to enhance features further from188
the core, and with heavy tapering to detect faint, distant jet components.189

4.DISCUSSION584
4.1.Variability585
The quasi-periodic outbursts in the core region of 3C207 have a mean spacing in the co-moving586
frame of 4.1±0.5 yr, with rise and decay times of∼2 yr. Despite nearly three decades of observations,587
the coarse sampling of most of the outbursts does not allow us to make any strong claims regarding588
periodicity. We do note that periods of order ten years in flux density monitoring data, when well-589
determined, have been interpreted as evidence for binary black holes in, e.g., OJ287 (Valtonen et al.590
2006). The minima between outbursts suggest a quiescent level of∼400 mJy. If we define the591
fractional variability amplitudef v = (S max −S min)/Smin, the outbursts range fromf v ∼0.25 for592
1983.47 tof v ∼2.5 for 2003.45. Thus the outbursts range over an order of magnitude in strength.593
This range could of course be intrinsic. If it is due to redirection of an inner relativistic jet component594
in the core region, we can estimate the range of anglesθto the line of sight using the Doppler factor595
δ= [γ(1−βcosθ)] −1, whereγis the Lorentz factor andβis the speed parameter (assumed constant596
for this calculation). If we treat the core region as a single flat-spectrum component, then aδ 3 ∼10597
flux density range only requiresδto range over a factor of∼2. Forγ= 10, this is easily accomplished598
ifθvaries from, e.g., 15 ◦ to 10◦.599
The details of shorter-term variations in flux density and component diameters are of particular600
interest during the 2003.45 outburst. As we have already seen, there are actually three sub-outbursts601
of C only∼1.3 yr apart in the co-moving frame. For the first two sub-outbursts, the component602

S mirrors these with a lag of∼0.6 yr. The diameter of C is remarkably stable during the entire603
2003.45 outburst. The diameter of S begins at less than∼0.2 mas. It first has a distinctly non-zero604
diameter of 0.16 mas at 2002.2 just prior to the peak of the first S sub-outburst, rises to a maximum605
of 0.37 mas at 2005.0 just past the peak of the second S sub-outburst, and then eventually drops to606
0.13 mas.607
These behaviors for C and S support an interpretation in which three closely-spaced sub-outbursts608
in C each create a new component of small diameter (less than∼0.2 mas) that continually expands609
as it moves out. As the first component passes through the S region and blends with it, the “S610
blend” increases in diameter. As the second component later does the same, the diameter of the S611
blend reaches a larger maximum value. This could be because the first new component also remains612
at least partly blended with S, having not yet completely cleared the S region, at least as can be613
determined given the lower quality of the 2005.02 data noted previously. By 2005.22, the first new614
component is seen to be separately resolved from S, and the diameter of S decreases. We note that615
the 2002.03 epoch – at which the core region could only be modeled with a single elliptical Gaussian616
component – is halfway between the first maxima for C and S. If the first new component was about617
midway between C and S at this time, it is plausible that the resulting quasi-continuous line of flux618
required an elliptical Gaussian representation at our resolution.619
We identify the newly-resolved component at 2005.22 as J4. The emergence of the second new620
component is not observed during the 2005 observations, but for 2008 through 2010, J4 has to be621
represented as a double (sub-components J4a and J4b) or triple (third sub-component Jc). This is622
fairly convincing evidence that the triple structure of J4 has its origins in the triple sub-outbursts623
in the core C. It also leads us to the reasonable speculation that the 1989.27 outburst consisted of624
two sub-outbursts, responsible for the wide diameter of J2 at its first VLBA epoch in 1995.67 and625
its double nature (J2a and J2b) at all succeeding epochs through 2001.63. It is not clear why J2626
thereafter presented as a single component from 2001.86 through 2010.81. Component J3 has always627
appeared as a single component; this raises the possibility that the 1995.67 outburst was a single628
event producing only one component. Component J1 has only been observed as a single component629

no observations at all of any earlier events) that may have produced it, so we can say nothing about631

4.2.Component Motions633
For the swinging component S, the unshifted positions are consistent with no radial motion, but634
the proper motion using the shifted positions (0.008±0.003 mas yr −1) corresponds to a subluminal635
apparent transverse velocity (in units ofc)β app = 0.3±0.1. Thus at most, S is undergoing mildly636
relativistic outward motion. Its angular motions, however, are quite significant. The largest angular637
motion of∼5 ◦ yr−1, at the meand= 0.44 mas, yields a modestly superluminalβ app ∼1.5. This638
feature must therefore be associated with changes in direction of the mechanism that is ejecting new639
jet components, and likely represents – in the radial direction – a (nearly-)stationary shock in a640
(quasi-)continuous flow.641
The linear fits to core separationdas a function of time for the four jet components yield the642
following apparent superluminal speeds:β appJ1 = 7.7±1.8,β appJ2 = 9.9±0.5,β appJ3 = 9.7±0.3,643
andβ appJ4 = 3.0±0.7. As discussed in Section 3.4.3, J2 is better modeled with a two-component644
linear fit that yieldsβ appJ2 = 6.8±0.4 before 2004.5 andβ appJ2 = 14.2±0.9 afterwards. Likewise,645
J3 is better modeled with a two-component linear fit that yieldsβ app = 12.8±1.2 before 2004.5 and646
βapp = 9.0±0.3 afterwards. The slower speed for component J4 is consistent with our earlier 15 GHz647
results (Danielson et al. 2007; Polito & Hough 2010) and the 15 GHz results of Lister et al. (2013).648
This may be due to both blending with the swinging component S at small radial separations and a649
lower apparent transverse velocity for J4. We note that despite mistaking S for other components,650
Hough et al. (2002) did find reasonable speed estimates for J1 (β app ∼10) and J2 (β app ∼7), because651
these components had been recently created at the times of misidentification and were then essentially652
still coincident with S.653

4.3.Model for Jet Cone Opening Angle with Recollimation Zone654
The time gaps between the outbursts that spawned J2 and J3, and then between those that gave655
rise to J3 and J4, are roughly equal, and all three outbursts are quite clear in the VLBI flux density656
record (see Figure 2). However, it is possible that the gap between the outbursts that formed J1 and657
J2 is considerably longer. Our identification of an outburst peaking at 1983.47 is only tentative; in658
fact, within the uncertainties, the core region flux density could have been constant between 1981.76659
and 1984.60. Moreover, the time gap between J1 and J2 is clearly the largest one on the core distance660
plot in Figure 9.661
This large time gap is important in attempting to model the projected trajectories of the four jet662
components in terms of an orderly progression of ejection angle, as in,e.g., a precessing jet. We663
are compelled to do so by the orderly progression of position angles for the successive components664
J2, J3, and J4. The trajectories in Figure 15 could possibly be explained by precession– e.g., from665
a binary black hole or jet/disk misalignment– if the time gap between ejection of J1 and J2 is long666
enough. For example, J1 may have been ejected when the launching mechanism was directed along667
the back side of the precession cone with PA∼90 ◦. The ejection of J2 may have then have occurred668
when the launch direction was at or near its smallest (i.e., most northerly) position angle. Then J3669
and J4 may have followed, closer in time and at successively larger position angles, as the precessing670
jet axis moved back toward PA∼90 ◦ along the front side. Beyond∼2.5 mas, the trajectory of J2671
strongly suggests a recollimation zone where the opening angle of the jet precession cone is reduced672
from its value in the inner jet. Finally, we see that individual jet components expand until reaching673
the recollimation zone (Figure 17).674
We now have several pieces of evidence that allow us to develop a working model that might explain675
much of the rich VLBI phenomenology in 3C207. The model requires a quasi-regular motion (perhaps676
precession) of the swinging component S around the axis of a cone. Deprojection of the observed677
PA range for S based on the superluminal jet speeds suggests a cone with true half-angle of∼5-6 ◦678
or less. The jet components follow straight paths along this cone, so there is no evidence for helical679
motion related to magnetic fields. It is interesting that J4, with the slowest motion, is associated680

40
with the most powerful – and therefore perhaps most highy beamed – outburst in 2003. This could681
be explained by motion along the cone at a very small angle to our line-of-sight. Beyond 2-3 mas,682
there is a narrowing of the opening angle in a recollimation zone, which accounts for the redirection683
of J2 and J3, and seems to be associated with their apparent acceleration and possible deceleration,684
respectively. While bending of the jet must play some role for both components, it is difficult to685
produce an increase in J2 fromβ app ≈7 toβ app ≈14 without intrinsic acceleration.686
5.CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE WORK687
After thirty years, we have a working model for 3C207 in which jet components are ejected at688
different angles around a cone, with the flow recollimating at 2-3 mas downstream, of order∼100 pc689
deprojected distance. But the jet in 3C207 is most likely aimed close to our line-of-sight. So the690
question remains: How well does our model apply to the other LDQs, taking larger orientation angles,691
beaming, and projection effects into account? Other sources have evidence of swinging innermost692
components, but projection effects – and thus observed PA variations – are smaller. Good determi-693
nations of the amplitude of PA variations in several objects would help to constrain the intrinsic cone694
angle. Other sources show evidence of acceleration, but we still need to confirm trends observed thus695
far. It is still unclear the circumstances in which apparent jet acceleration/deceleration occurs, and696
whether it is explained best by jet bending or intrinsic acceleration. Other sources show bending or697
misaligned jet features, but it is not clear yet when this may be associated with a recollimation zone698
as it is for 3C207.699
Future VLBI monitoring of 3C207 and other LDQs can help to resolve these questions. We note700
that two additional epochs of VLBA observations of 3C207 were obtained by D.H.H. in 2014.45 and701
2018.64 for this project but were not modeled. It would be interesting to know if jet components702
J2-J4 were detected in these epochs, and if new components appeared on the expected time interval703
of∼7 yr. However it might be challenging to match components because of the 4-year gaps between704
these and prior observations.705
Other VLBI observations of 3C207 exist as well. Using 15 GHz VLBA observations between 1994706
and 2016, Lister et al. (2019) identify seven moving features in the core of 3C207, with median and707
